## The life: the last founder, and the longest arc in concrete poetry

Augusto Luis Browne de Campos was born on 14 February 1931 in Sao Paulo, Brazil. (C13023, leg 1) The month matters and is worth stating plainly, because the brief for this study said 14 December and every dated source read gives February: Wikipedia, Encyclopedia.com and Prabook all give 14 February 1931, and his official biography gives only the year. December is treated here as an error in the brief, not as a competing date. (C13024, leg 1) He is a Brazilian poet, translator, literary and music critic, and visual artist. (C13025, leg 1) No source read states his formal education -- no law degree, no university course -- so none is claimed. (C13072, leg 1)

The career runs in six phases, and the hinge is the same as the doorway: the Noigandres invention of 1952-1958, then a seventy-year widening of what a poem can be made of, long after the orthodox concrete phase ends -- and unlike Pignatari (#663) and Haroldo, Augusto is still alive to keep widening it.

(1) Beginnings, 1948-1951. He published his first poem, "Fuga", in the Sunday edition of *Jornal de Noticias* on 27 June 1948, at age 17. (C13026, leg 1) Haroldo and Augusto met Decio Pignatari in 1948, and their first books appeared between 1950 and 1951, Augusto's being *O Rei Menos o Reino* (*The King Minus the Kingdom*, 1951). (C13027, leg 1) The first book is verse, and it is the starting point against which the later break with verse is measured. (C13075, leg 2)

(2) Noigandres and the invention, 1952-1958. In 1952, with his brother Haroldo de Campos and Decio Pignatari, he launched the literary magazine *Noigandres*, the origin of the Noigandres Group, which initiated the international movement of concrete poetry in Brazil. (C13028, leg 1) The name comes from Ezra Pound's Canto XX, quoting the Occitan troubadour Arnaut Daniel, whom Pound held up as an example of poetry by the "inventors". (C13029, leg 1) His series of colour-poems *Poetamenos* (*Minuspoet*) was written in 1953: six poems, sometimes printed in six different colours, inspired by Anton Webern's *Klangfarbenmelodie* (tone-colour melody). (C13030, leg 1) *Poetamenos* appeared in *Noigandres* no. 2 (1955), and three of the poems were presented that same year in four voices with slide projections by the music ensemble Grupo Ars Nova, already under the title "Concrete Poetry". (C13031, leg 1) From 1953 to 1958 he corresponded with Ezra Pound, then confined in St Elizabeths Hospital in Washington. (C13032, leg 1) In 1956 he helped organise the First National Exhibition of Concrete Art (Painting and Poetry) at the Museum of Modern Art in Sao Paulo, where he and his associates declared the beginning of the concrete poetry movement. (C13033, leg 1) Pignatari met Eugen Gomringer in Ulm in 1955 through designer Alexandre Wollner, and in 1956 Gomringer agreed to the label "concrete poetry", a label Augusto de Campos had first proposed for the Brazilians' own production a year before. (C13034, leg 1) The "Plano-Piloto para Poesia Concreta" (Pilot Plan for Concrete Poetry), by Augusto, Haroldo and Pignatari, was published in *Noigandres* 4 in 1958. (C13035, leg 1) He co-authored *Teoria da Poesia Concreta* (*Theory of Concrete Poetry*) with Haroldo de Campos and Decio Pignatari, published in 1965. (C13036, leg 1) The Pilot Plan month is flagged: sources confirm *Noigandres* 4, 1958, but "March 1958" was not corroborated by any source read. (C13072, leg 1)

(3) The classic concrete works and the political turn, 1960s. His trilingual poem "cidade/city/cite" was written in 1963, built from Latin-origin prefixes that take the suffixes *-cidade* / *-city* / *-cite* in Portuguese, English and French, and was letterpress-printed by Ian Hamilton Finlay's Wild Hawthorn Press in Edinburgh in 1963/1964. (C13037, leg 1) *Linguaviagem*, a cube poem measuring 25 x 25.5 x 25.5 cm, was created in 1967 for the international Brighton Festival's concrete poetry exhibition, with three letters on each face of the cube (LIN / GUA / VIA / GEM) yielding *linguagem*, *viagem*, *lingua* and *via*. (C13038, leg 1) Brazil's military dictatorship, imposed in 1964, lasted 20 years and upset the group's poetics: as Augusto put it, "we could not just make structural poems any more" and "the concrete rationalism was run over as our utopias shattered on the floor". (C13039, leg 1) The *Popcretos* series (1964-1966) consists of five collage works made from magazine and newspaper clippings and packaging debris on wood boards -- "Olho por olho", "SS", "O anti-ruido" and "goldWeater" (October-November 1964) and "Psiu!" (1966) -- responding to pop culture, US imperialism and censorship after the 1964 coup. (C13040, leg 1) "Olho por olho" (*Eye for an Eye*, 1964, published 1965) uses traffic signs to denounce the military dictatorship, and "Luxo" (1965) deconstructs a luxury-apartment advertisement by turning *luxo* (luxury) into *lixo* (garbage). (C13041, leg 1) The *Luxo* idea came from an incident at the Popcretos exhibition with Waldemar Cordeiro at Galeria Atrium in December 1964, when the works were found vandalised with ballpoint-pen insults, including the word "garbage" written on one of his anti-dictatorship pieces. (C13042, leg 1) His book *Balanco da Bossa (e Outras Bossas)* (1968-1974) gathered his pioneering studies of Tropicalism and Brazilian pop music, and his critical writings of the late 1960s helped win recognition for the poetry and experimentation of MPB, especially tropicalismo. (C13043, leg 1)

(4) The poem as an object, 1968-1979. His poem-object collections with graphic artist and designer Julio Plaza were *Poemobiles* (1974) and *Caixa Preta* (*Black Box*, 1975), and *Pulsar* (1975), part of the *Stelegramas* series (1975-78), is considered his first "constellation poem". (C13044, leg 1) Most of his poems up to that point were gathered in *Viva Vaia: Poesia 1949-1979*, published in 1979. (C13045, leg 1) *Despoesia* (*Unpoetry*) is dated 1993 in the NT2 and MoMA listings but 1994 in his official biography -- see Conflicts below. (C13046, leg 1)

(5) New media, sound and the digital turn, 1980-2000s. From 1980 on he intensified his experiments with new media, presenting poems on electric billboards, videotext, neon, holograms, lasers and computer graphics, and in multimedia events involving sound and music. (C13047, leg 1) The videoclip-poem *Pulsar*, with music by Caetano Veloso, was produced in 1984 on an Intergraph high-resolution computer station. (C13048, leg 1) Four of his holographic poems, made with holographer Moyses Baumstein, were shown in the exhibitions *TRILUZ* (1986) and *IDEHOLOGIA* (1987). (C13049, leg 1) He developed a plurivocal reading of *CIDADECITYCITE* with his son, the musician Cid Campos, in 1987/1991. (C13050, leg 1) *BOMB POEM* and *SOS*, with music by Cid Campos, were animated in 1992-1993 on a Silicon Graphics computer station at the University of Sao Paulo. (C13051, leg 1) His cooperation with Cid, begun in 1987, produced *Poesia e Risco* (*Poetry is Risk*), a CD launched by PolyGram in 1995 and developed into a multimedia "verbivocovisual" poetry/music/image performance with video editing by Walter Silveira, presented in several cities in Brazil and abroad. (C13052, leg 1) An installation assembling his digital poetic animations, the *Clip-Poems*, was shown in 1997 at the Casa das Rosas exhibition *Arte Suporte Computador*, and his book *Nao* (2003) was issued with a CDR of his clip-poems. (C13053, leg 1) *Chance Words* was conceived in 2006, after he acquired his first computer, using Flash to add movement and sound to his colour-and-form research, and it was later remade by the aarea platform (reprogrammed by Adriano Ferrari) after Flash was discontinued. (C13054, leg 1) In 2004 his volume of computer-generated poetry *Nao* was named Brazil's National Library book of the year, and the institution organised an event and multimedia retrospective to honour his work. (C13055, leg 1)

(6) Late career and retrospectives, 2010s-2020s. The Sesc Pompeia retrospective "REVER_Augusto de Campos" ran in Sao Paulo through 31 July 2016. (C13056, leg 1) An exhibition dedicated to his digital art, *TransCreation*, was held from 2 August to 29 October 2021 at The NEXT Museum, Library, and Preservation Space. (C13057, leg 1) His poems have been gathered in more than 15 volumes, including *Despoesia* (1993), *Nao* (2003), *Outro* (2015) and, most recently, *Pos Poemas* (2025). (C13058, leg 1) His older brother Haroldo de Campos (1929-2003) and Decio Pignatari (1927-2012) are both dead, leaving Augusto the last surviving founder of the Noigandres group. (C13059, leg 1) His son Cid Campos is a musician, and their artistic cooperation began in 1987. (C13060, leg 1)

Honours (confirmed): Premio Jabuti in 1979 in the literary-translation category for *Verso, Reverso, Controverso*. (C13061, leg 1) Premio Ibero-Americano de Poesia Pablo Neruda in 2015, awarded by Chile's National Council of Culture and the Arts and the Pablo Neruda Foundation. (C13062, leg 1) In 2015 he also received Brazil's Order of Cultural Merit, in a homage at the Palacio do Planalto in Brasilia from then-president Dilma Rousseff. (C13063, leg 1) In 2017 he was awarded the Janus Pannonius Grand Prize for Poetry by the Hungarian PEN Club, a 50,000 euro prize whose ceremony was held on 23 September 2017 in Pecs, in the Csontvary Hall of the Janus Pannonius Museum, with a gala in Budapest the next day. (C13064, leg 1) His work is held in the collections of the Museo de Arte Latinoamericano de Buenos Aires (MALBA), the Museo Reina Sofia in Madrid, and the Ruth and Marvin Sackner Archive of Concrete and Visual Poetry at the University of Iowa Libraries. (C13065, leg 1) The honours record is the fullest of the three Noigandres founders in these studies: Pignatari (#663) had one confirmed honour, the Jabuti 1997; Augusto has four, and the Neruda and Janus Pannonius prizes are international, not Brazilian. (C13182)

Controversies: no personal scandal, legal case, or public controversy attached to Augusto de Campos himself was found in the sources searched. (C13067, leg 1) The documented disputes around him are artistic and historical rather than personal: the Rio de Janeiro poets and artists broke with the Sao Paulo Paulistas for ideological reasons soon after the 1956 exhibition and declared themselves "Neoconcretos", with Ronaldo Azeredo the exception, having already joined Noigandres. (C13068, leg 1) Concrete poetry fell out of favour with the academy in the 1980s and 1990s, and Marjorie Perloff's later writing on the Noigandres poets was framed as rehabilitating them after that eclipse. (C13069, leg 1) Unlike Gomringer (#662), whose "avenidas" poem was painted over in a public row, and unlike Pignatari (#663), whose most famous poem was read as an attack on advertising, Augusto's work provoked its argument with the dictatorship and with the academy -- institutions, not individuals -- and the argument stayed inside the work. (C13183)

## M1 -- Colour as voice

*Poetamenos* (W03) is six poems written in 1953 and first printed in *Noigandres* 2 in 1955, sometimes printed in six different colours, inspired by Anton Webern's *Klangfarbenmelodie* -- tone-colour melody, in which a melodic line is passed from instrument to instrument so that its colour changes while its notes stay the same. (C13030, leg 1) The mechanism is literal: words and phonemes are printed in up to six colours, so colour groups cue different voices and reading paths, and a single name such as "Lygia" is split across colours and languages. (C13101, leg 2) Three of the poems were presented in 1955 in four voices with slide projections by the music ensemble Grupo Ars Nova, already under the title "Concrete Poetry" -- the colour on the page was a score, and the performance read the score by splitting it into voices. (C13031, leg 1) *Poetamenos* is considered the first consistent example of concrete poetry in Brazil, with verse and conventional syntax abandoned and words rearranged in graphic patterns. (C13128, leg 3)

What matters mechanically is that colour is not decoration here and not emphasis. It is the syntax that verse used to be. In a normal poem the line break and the stanza tell you where one voice ends and another begins; in *Poetamenos* the colour does that work, and because a word can change colour mid-word, a voice can hand a syllable to another voice the way Webern hands a note to another instrument. The reader's eye has to follow the colour, not the line, and the reading order is the order the colours dictate.

**Takeaway:** when colour carries the voice, the page is a score and the reader is a performer, not a consumer. A piece that colours words without changing who speaks when the colour changes has the decoration and not the mechanism.

## M2 -- Graphic space as syntax

*Tensao* (W04) breaks words into three-character segments set inside shrinking quadrilateral squares, so reading moves in several directions at once and the centre word "tension" holds sound against silence. (C13102, leg 2) The poem opposes "with sound" and "without sound" on the diagonal, with "tension" at the centre. (C13078, leg 2) The squares shrink as they approach the centre, so the eye is pulled inward by the geometry itself, and the word at the centre is the one the whole structure is tensed around.

There is a second, stranger fact about *Tensao* that belongs in the mechanism, not in a footnote: it is a unique, unpublished manuscript for a book of poems composed between 1958 and 1963, commissioned by Gomringer for his transcontinental initiative *Konkrete Poesie -- Poesia concreta*; on Gomringer's return to Germany from Switzerland in 1967 he abandoned his press, and the manuscript was never printed. (C13144, leg 3) The poem about tension was itself held in tension -- commissioned, composed, then stranded by a press that closed. The dating conflict follows from that history: 1956 as a poem in the MDPI article, 1958-1963 as the manuscript book (production date 1963), never printed. (C13121, leg 2) Both dates are carried; the poem is dated 1956 and the manuscript 1958-1963.

**Takeaway:** space is syntax when the layout decides the reading order and the centre word is the load the layout carries. A grid that only frames text is a frame; a grid that shrinks toward a word and forces the eye to choose a direction is a sentence.

## M3 -- Fused-string unpacking

*cidade/city/cite* (W07) is a long unbroken string of city-words in Portuguese, English and French that accumulates and then resolves into the three language forms cidade / city / cite. (C13103, leg 2) The rule behind it is exact and was stated by the sources: only words sharing the same grapheme in at least three idioms are admitted, exploiting an "anti-Babelian" rarity that unifies differing idioms in one formal and contextual situation. (C13146, leg 3) The poem was written in 1963 and letterpress-printed by Ian Hamilton Finlay's Wild Hawthorn Press in Edinburgh in 1963/1964. (C13037, leg 1) Later it was given a plurivocal reading with his son Cid Campos in 1987/1991, and the recording starts as noise from which a voice emerges. (C13081, leg 2; C13050, leg 1)

The watchable behaviour is the unpacking itself: a dense string that looks like one long word, then separates, under the eye, into three cities in three languages that turn out to be the same city. The mechanism is a filter -- admit only what survives in all three languages -- and the filter is what makes the resolution feel earned rather than arranged. Babel says the languages divide us; the poem says the shared letters were there all along, and the string only had to be pulled apart to show them.

**Takeaway:** a fused string earns its resolution when the rule that built it is strict enough that the ending is discovered, not decorated. Let the accumulation run long enough that the viewer doubts it will ever separate, then let the separation be the payoff.

## M4 -- Portmanteau as object

*Linguaviagem* (W10) is an invented word fusing lingua, viagem, linguagem and via -- tongue, voyage, language, way -- and because it is a standing cube poem, the word is watched by turning the object so interior and exterior faces spell different parts of it. (C13104, leg 2) The cube measures 25 x 25.5 x 25.5 cm and was created in 1967 for the international Brighton Festival's concrete poetry exhibition, with three letters on each face of the cube (LIN / GUA / VIA / GEM) yielding *linguagem*, *viagem*, *lingua* and *via*. (C13038, leg 1) It is printed text on heavy paper folded into a square -- a standing poem, not a page. (C13084, leg 2)

The mechanism is that the portmanteau is not a joke about a word but a word that needs a body. On a flat page "linguaviagem" is a clever compound; on a cube it is a word you cannot read all at once, because the faces hide each other, and the act of turning the object is the act of reading. The interior faces spell parts the exterior does not, so the poem has an inside and an outside, and the reader's hands do the work the eye used to do.

Note the title trap, carried honestly: *Linguaviagem* is two objects, a 1967 cube poem (Getty) and a 1987 book of the same title -- distinct, not one work. (Combined summary, correction 2) The mechanism in this section is the 1967 cube only.

**Takeaway:** a word becomes an object when you cannot read it without moving around it. If the piece can be read from one fixed viewpoint, it is a picture of a cube, not a cube poem.

## M5 -- Fold as reading order

*Poemobiles* (W11) is thirteen fold-out poems in unbound folios with no fixed sequence, in primary red, yellow and blue, made with Julio Plaza, including "Abre" (1969) and "Viva Vaia" (1974). (C13085, leg 2) The mechanism is sharpest in "Abre": the diamond-folded sheet opens and closes, so the words open, close, yellow, reopen, blue and red change position and sequence as the reader folds and unfolds it. (C13105, leg 2) The first poemobiles were published as *Objetos* in 1968-69, and *Poemobiles* the book followed in 1974; *Caixa Preta* (W12, 1975) continues the same procedure as a portfolio of object-poems with Plaza. (C13122, leg 2; C13086, leg 2)

The story of how the collaboration started is part of the mechanism. Plaza, who had travelled to Brazil in 1967 for the IX Bienal de Sao Paulo and met Augusto there, began experimenting with cut and folded paper in the late 1960s; Augusto was brought in to write a critical essay as the foreword to the book project, but instead chose to write concrete poetry integrated into Plaza's moving shapes, and the result, published as *Objetos*, was in fact the first poemobiles. (C13148-C13149, leg 3) The essay became a poem because the shapes would not sit still for criticism. The reader is told, in *Colidouescapo* (1971), to refold the loose pages and mix them at will -- the sequence is the reader's, not the author's. (C13097, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** a fold is a reading order when opening and closing the sheet changes which word comes next. The interaction is not "tap to reveal"; it is the fold itself, and the piece must let the viewer fold and unfold and watch the sequence change.

## M6 -- Truncated-word completion

"O anti-ruido" from *Popcretos* (W08) sets fragments in two concentric circles on a deep red ground, with complete English monosyllables at the centre and broken Portuguese words such as "compr-" and "terr-" in the halo, which the reader must complete ad libitum -- at will, in any way they like. (C13106, leg 2) The *Popcretos* are five collage works on wood boards, made from magazine and newspaper clippings and packaging debris in October-November 1964 and 1966, as sociopolitical commentary after the 1964 coup. (C13082, leg 2) The title means "the anti-noise": the poem is built out of the noise of advertising and the press, and its centre is the small, complete English words -- the language of the imported product -- while the Portuguese words around them are broken and left for the reader to finish.

The mechanism is a contract with the reader. The poem does not finish its own words; it hands the completion over, and the completion is the reading. "Compr-" can become comprar or compreensao or compromisso, and the poem does not care which -- the point is that the reader supplies the ending the dictatorship's language will not. The deep red ground is the only colour the piece needs: it is the ground the fragments sit on and the alarm the title names.

**Takeaway:** a broken word is an invitation only when the completion is genuinely open. If the piece has one right answer, it is a quiz; if it has many, it is a poem.

## M7 -- Wordless image, title as key

"Olho por olho" (*Eye for an Eye*, 1964, published 1965) from *Popcretos* (W08) has, apart from the title, no words at all: only clippings of eyes, lips, headlights and traffic signs arranged as a pyramid, so meaning is read through gaze and the "no left turn" / "turn right" signs at the summit. (C13107, leg 2) The pyramid of eyes looks back at the viewer, and the traffic signs at the top -- the instruments of the state telling bodies where they may and may not go -- are the punchline the title sets up: an eye for an eye, the law of retaliation, stated by the state in its own signage. The work uses traffic signs to denounce the military dictatorship. (C13041, leg 1)

The mechanism is the title doing all the verbal work. Without "Olho por olho" the collage is a pyramid of faces and signs; with it, the eyes become witnesses, the headlights become surveillance, and the turn signs become orders. The title is not a label on the work; it is the one word the work is allowed, and it unlocks everything else. This is the most economical mechanism in the whole study: one title, zero words in the image, and the meaning is complete.

**Takeaway:** a title can be the whole poem when the image cannot speak without it. The piece must earn the title's work -- if the image says the same thing without the title, the title is decoration; if the image is mute without it, the title is the mechanism.

## M8 -- Kinetic clip-poem

*O Pulsar* (W15) is the print constellation poem from *Stelegramas* (1975-78) remade as a videoclip-poem in 1984 on an Intergraph high-resolution computer station, with music by Caetano Veloso. (C13089, leg 2) The constellation poem is animated on the computer station with the music, so words pulse, move and change form in time with sound, a procedure continued in the clip-poems made directly on the computer and issued on the *Nao* CDR (2003). (C13108, leg 2) *Pulsar* is described as the artist's first "poema constelacao" and one of his best examples of poetic construction. (C13087, leg 2) From 1980 on he presented poems on electric billboards, videotext, neon, holograms, lasers and computer graphics, and in multimedia events involving sound and music. (C13047, leg 1) *BOMB POEM* and *SOS*, with music by Cid Campos, were animated in 1992-1993 on a Silicon Graphics computer station at the University of Sao Paulo. (C13051, leg 1) *Chance Words* was conceived in 2006, after he acquired his first computer, using Flash to add movement and sound to his colour-and-form research. (C13054, leg 1)

The mechanism is the pulse itself: the word does not move for decoration, it moves because the sound moves it, and the viewer watches a constellation breathe in time with the music. This is the endpoint of the whole arc -- the word that began as coloured type in 1953 is now a moving shape on a screen, still a word, still carrying its sound, but no longer on a page at all. The honest limit is stated in the Depth line and repeated here: the *Clip-Poems* CDR and the *Poesia e Risco* CD audio were not accessible this run, so the sound and motion claims for W18-W19 rest on secondary description, not on hearing or playing the works. (C13119, leg 2) What was read end to end is the record of the procedure, not the procedure itself.

**Takeaway:** a word pulses when the sound drives it, not when an animation loop decorates it. Tie every movement to an audible beat, and let the word change form only when the sound changes first.


## The aesthetic logic: the word that keeps changing its body

Augusto de Campos's aesthetic logic is a single sentence that holds across seventy years: the word is a material thing, and its material keeps changing. In 1953 the material is colour. In 1956 it is space. In 1963 it is three languages at once. In 1967 it is a cube. In 1974 it is a fold. In 1984 it is a pulse on a screen. At no point does he abandon the word for the image, and at no point does he keep the word in the same body for long. That is the difference between him and the orthodox concrete poets who stayed with the page: Gomringer (#662) remained more faithful to the initial orthodoxy, while the Brazilians continued to be more experimental and "baroquizing". (C13143, leg 3) Augusto is the most baroque of the three founders in exactly this sense -- not in ornament, but in the willingness to let the poem become an object, a hologram, a videoclip, and still call it a poem.

The second half of the logic is the reader's share. In every mechanism the reader does real work: following colour instead of lines (M1), choosing a reading direction (M2), pulling a string apart (M3), turning a cube (M4), folding a sheet (M5), completing a broken word (M6), unlocking an image with a title (M7), watching a pulse and hearing it at the same time (M8). The poem is never finished before the reader arrives. That is why the political turn of the 1960s is not a break in the work but a continuation of it: when the dictatorship broke the language, the poems broke the words further and handed the pieces to the reader, because a reader who completes a word is a reader who has not been silenced.

The third half -- and there is a third half, because the logic has three terms -- is translation. Augusto spent his life translating Pound, Mallarme and Joyce into Portuguese, and translating Joyce's *Finnegans Wake* with Haroldo (*Panorama do Finnegans Wake*, 1962). (C13133, leg 3) Translation is the same operation as the concrete poem: take a word out of one body and put it into another, and see what survives. *cidade/city/cite* is a translation that never leaves the original behind; *Linguaviagem* is a translation of four words into one; the cube is a translation of a page into an object. The word survives each translation by changing, and that is the whole poetics.


## The network: the triangle, and the two adjacents who changed the medium

The core network is the Noigandres triangle, and it is the same triangle as #663, seen now from its third corner. Haroldo de Campos (1929-2003), the older brother by about a year and a half, met Pignatari with Augusto in 1948; the three were already thinking of poetry together by 1949, and in 1952 they paid for the publishing of the magazine-book *Noigandres*. (C13125-C13126, leg 3) The trio co-authored the *Plano-Piloto* (1958) and *Teoria da Poesia Concreta* (1965), took "verbivocovisual" from Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*, and co-translated Pound and Mallarme into Portuguese. (C13130-C13134, leg 3) Decio Pignatari (1927-2012) embarked for Europe in 1954, met Gomringer in Ulm in 1955, and wrote the 1970s poem-manifesto "Interessere", whose line "In Concretism it is of interest what is not Concretism" could stand as Augusto's motto as well as his own. (C13135-C13137, leg 3)

Eugen Gomringer is the transatlantic link, and it is mediated, not direct: no interaction between Augusto de Campos and Gomringer in person was evidenced in the sources read; the link runs through Pignatari, the *Tensao* commission, and correspondence. (Leg 3, Conflicts) Gomringer was the first European poet in whom the Brazilians located the affinities they were searching for, and to their surprise he had started, like them, from Mallarme's *Un Coup de Des*. (C13139, C13141, leg 3) After the meeting the two sides converged toward a simplification of language, and for the orthodox phase from 1956 until the end of the decade they approached each other closely, arriving at a kind of least common multiple of poetical language, even at the one-word poem -- then diverged. (C13142, leg 3) Augusto's *Tensao* manuscript was commissioned by Gomringer for his transcontinental initiative and never printed when Gomringer abandoned his press in 1967. (C13144, leg 3) The Sackner collection holds the "gracious correspondence between the brothers and Gomringer". (C13145, leg 3)

Two adjacent artists changed Augusto's medium, and both are the #665 candidates. Julio Plaza, born in Madrid, travelled to Brazil in 1967 for the IX Bienal de Sao Paulo, where he met Augusto; their cut-and-fold experiments produced *Objetos*, *Poemobiles* (1974), *Caixa Preta* (1975) and *Reduchamp* (1976), and they worked together through the 1970s and 1980s. (C13148-C13151, leg 3) Waldemar Cordeiro and the Grupo Ruptura gave the poets their exhibition: Augusto's group met Cordeiro's geometric artists in 1952, Cordeiro organised the 1st Exposicao Nacional de Arte Concreta at MAM/SP in December 1956, and in 1964 Cordeiro created the "popcrete" with Augusto, works aimed at introducing a semantic approach into concrete art. (C13152-C13155, leg 3) The Rio poets -- Ronaldo Azeredo, who joined Noigandres rather than breaking to Neoconcrete, and Ferreira Gullar, whose relationship with the group was "brief albeit polemic" -- complete the adjacent network. (C13156-C13158, leg 3) Further out: Pound, corresponded with 1953-1958 while Pound was confined in St Elizabeths Hospital, and Ian Hamilton Finlay (#661), who wanted to know Mallarme's *Un Coup de Des* and received a copy Augusto had made himself, by hand, from the NRF edition. (C13147, leg 3) Max Bense used Augusto's 1963 photograph of Ouro Preto in his book *Brasilianische Intelligenz* (1965). (C13159, leg 3) And Cid Campos, his son, collaborated from 1987 on the plurivocal reading of *CIDADECITYCITE* and on *Poesia e Risco* (1995). (C13160, leg 3)
