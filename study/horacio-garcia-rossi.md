# Horacio Garcia Rossi: Study Notes

**Site:** Horacio Garcia Rossi, born 24 July 1929 in Buenos Aires, died 5 September 2012 in Paris, aged 83 -- Argentine kinetic artist, GRAV co-founder, the man who put real movement and light inside a box: ninety acrylic rods on the face of a wooden box, two lights inside, a motor-turned disk carrying metal squares on wires, so the front flickers and never repeats; hand-cranked boxes and rotating cylinders the spectator turns by hand; his own name lit in block letters; and, late in life, flat paintings in which colour alone does the work the motors used to do, a band of light painted into the canvas. He came to Paris in 1959 under the influence of Victor Vasarely's Yellow Manifesto, having sold his library to buy the ticket, and co-founded the group that took Vasarely's idea that the sole artist was outdated and turned it into labyrinths, street actions and games for the spectator (C6201, C6202, C6209, C6251, C6252, C6301).

## The life: from tracing-paper films to the box that never repeats

Horacio Garcia Rossi was born in Buenos Aires in 1929 (C6201, C6202). The day is corrected in this study: the brief said 28 January 1929, but that date appears in only one source, Pontiart, a recent site whose page reads like generated copy, used snippet-only (C6218). Against it, the French, German and Spanish Wikipedias, Wikidata and the Novalis gallery bio all give 24 July 1929 (C6201, C6208). This study uses 24 July 1929 and treats 28 January as an error to avoid (C6353).

His first interest was cinema: from 1935, still a child, he made his own films with tracing paper (C6209). He studied at the advertising school of Buenos Aires in 1948-1949, where he first tried oil painting (C6209). His fine-art training is reported two ways, and the study reports both rather than picking one silently: the standard gallery version (Sicardi, French Wikipedia, Lelia Mordoch, LIFA) says he studied at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires from 1950 to 1957, where he met Hugo Demarco, Julio Le Parc and Francisco Sobrino, and then taught painting and drawing there until 1958 (C6202, C6201, C6203); a more detailed French valuation-site biography splits the same years into the Manuel Belgrano preparatory school (1950-1952) and the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredon (1953-1959), naming his teachers as Hector Cartier (colour theory) and Lorenzo Gigli (drawing) (C6209). During his studies he shared a studio with Demarco and Sobrino, and together they moved toward constructivism (C6209). From 1945 to 1958 he took part in numerous collective exhibitions in Argentina and Latin America (C6212). The Duke University Press chapter on GRAV adds that Garcia Rossi, Le Parc and Sobrino took classes with Lucio Fontana in Buenos Aires -- that detail comes from a search-result snippet and was not verified on the opened page (C6213).

In 1959 he moved to Paris, where he lived and worked for the rest of his life (C6202, C6201). What drew him was Victor Vasarely: the detailed French biography says he was "very inspired by the Manifeste jaune (Yellow Manifesto) of Victor Vasarely, sold his library to buy a transatlantic ticket, and left to settle in France" (C6209). That same year he took part in the first Paris Biennial and in the MOTUS exhibition in Milan (C6202, C6203). A direct, dated, one-to-one meeting with Vasarely was NOT found in any source read. What the sources support is contact in general terms: Le Parc encountered Vasarely's work in a 1958 solo exhibition at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires, which had a "very strong visual impact" on him; within months of arriving in France, Le Parc was joined by Garcia Rossi, Sobrino and Demarco, and "both together and separately the men made visits to Vasarely's studio" (C6216). Morellet's own account, quoted in #620, says the three Argentines "first visited Vasarely, who pointed them to Francois and Vera Molnar, then to me and to Vasarely's son Yvaral" (C6301). So the study says Garcia Rossi came to Paris under Vasarely's influence, joined a circle that visited Vasarely's studio, and worked from Vasarely's ideas on visual perception -- not that a specific first meeting took place on a known date, because no source gives one (C6351).

In 1960 Garcia Rossi co-founded the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel (CRAV) (C6202, C6203). Sicardi gives the full eleven-name CRAV roster: Francisco Garcia Miranda, Julio Le Parc, Francisco Sobrino, Francois Molnar, Sergio Moyano Servanes, Yvaral, Joel Stein, Hugo Demarco, Francois Morellet, and Garcia Rossi himself (C6202). Le Parc's own GRAV page dates the founding decision precisely: "In July 1960, several painters... decided to create the Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel" (C6205). CRAV dissolved shortly after forming and was restructured as the Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel (GRAV) (C6202); the six-name core roster is confirmed by several independent sources: Garcia Rossi, Le Parc, Sobrino, Morellet, Stein, Yvaral (C6213, C6214, C6215). GRAV was active in Paris from 1960 to 1968 and agreed to dissolve in November 1968, "based on their recognition that it was impossible to maintain the rigor of a joint program" (C6206). Two roster wrinkles, not smoothed over: the January 1961 GRAV text "Propositions on Movement", reproduced on Le Parc's site, is signed by only five names -- Morellet, Le Parc, Sobrino, Yvaral, Stein -- without Garcia Rossi (C6205); other sources give different manifesto signatory lists, seven names incl. Demarco in one, Vasarely added in ArtPulse, seven names sent in April 1961 in a Moderna Museet document (C6219, C6214, C6217). The six-name core is stable; the manifesto signature lists vary by source. GRAV's programme, as quoted by Sicardi from art historian Jacqueline Barnitz: the artists believed art should be based on "scientific investigation of the physical properties of vision and statistics and probabilities, as the means through which they could best achieve their political and philosophical goals" (C6202). They built interactive, labyrinthine structures viewers could walk through, using industrial materials such as aluminium, Plexiglas, mirrors and lights (C6202). Its first Labyrinth was held at the 1963 Paris Biennale, presenting three years of work in optical and kinetic devices (C6206); on 19 April 1966 GRAV staged Une Journee dans la rue (A Day in the Street), a day-long itinerary through Paris inviting passers-by to manipulate kinetic objects (C6206, C6213).

Evolution: his earliest works were "clearly dominated by the use of black and white on a two-dimensional plane" (C6204) -- the multiplication of a form, the perception of movement in flat works, using only black, white and gray, anonymity and programming of forms, virtual movement (C6202, C6212). From 1962 he introduced real movement and light (C6203, C6212, C6201): first geometric-form experiments on screens, then works the spectator could manipulate -- rotating cylinders -- and research on the instability of light and movement, creating unstable light boxes whose colours and patterns could be manipulated, and structures with changing-colour lights (C6201, C6203). In 1964 he made his first Plexiglas sculptures, sometimes combined with the light boxes for layered light effects (C6209). From 1966 he experimented with the visual identification of writing, leading to a "moving alphabet" and, in 1969, to an ambiguous alphabet system, "trying to give each letter the movement that its form and its meaning engender" (C6203, C6201). After GRAV dissolved he returned to two-dimensional problems and simple structures; between 1972 and 1973 he returned to two-dimensional exploration, and from 1974 through 1978 he was mainly interested in semiotics as an artistic issue (C6202, C6204, C6201). From 1978 he worked on the problem of couleur-lumiere (colour-light). His own definition, quoted in French Wikipedia: "In these researches, colour manifests itself neither as a decorative element in itself, nor as a variety of mixed colours, but as a grouping intended to create a new structure of visualisation: colour-light" (C6201). In the late 1970s he focused on the interaction of light and colour, and in recent years developed a series titled Chaos, "dominated by the harmony of 'color-light'" (C6204); in the 2000s colour-light moved off the canvas into space, sculpture and objects, and he stayed active until his death in 2012 (C6209).

Exhibition spine: first Paris Biennial 1959; first GRAV show, Galerie Denise Rene, Paris, 1961; Labyrinth, third Paris Biennale, 1963; Documenta 3, Kassel, 1964; The Responsive Eye, MoMA, New York, 1965; Lumiere et Mouvement, Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, 1967; Plus by Minus, Albright-Knox, Buffalo, 1968; Kinetics, Hayward Gallery, London, 1970; survey, Centro Cultural de Arte Contemporaneo, Brescia, 1989; Garcia Rossi and le GRAV, Hermitage Museum, St. Petersburg, 2008; posthumous: Una vision otra: G.R.A.V. 1960/1968, Tamayo Museum, Mexico City, 2013 (C6202, C6211, C6209, C6206). His work is held by the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires; Musee National d'Art Moderne, Centre Pompidou, Paris; Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris; Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden, Washington DC; Museum of Fine Arts, Houston; Albright-Knox Art Gallery, Buffalo; the State Hermitage Museum, St. Petersburg; and others (C6203, C6211, C6202). The Pontiart page also names MoMA, New York, but that claim was only seen in a search snippet and is not confirmed (C6218). He died in Paris on 5 September 2012, in the 15th arrondissement, aged 83, and is buried in Montparnasse Cemetery, division 3 (C6201, C6207).

## M1 -- The unstable light box: a motor, a disk, and ninety rods

Structure a lumiere instable no. 29 (W10, 1966, Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, object 2004.1618) is the best-documented mechanism in the whole catalogue. "There are ninety acrylic rods of different thickness and length on the exterior. Two lights on the inside of the front panel project light onto the back of the box, on which a wooden disk with a forest of shiny metal squares is mounted. The disk is turned by a motor, causing the metal squares to shift position on their wires, casting light randomly through the acrylic rods" (C6251). The back label specifies the ideal ambience as "penombre" (half-light) and 110V current (C6252). The dimensions are 59.9 x 59.9 x 48.9 cm by the museum measurement; the artist's own back label gives 60 x 60 x 35 cm -- the two disagree on depth, both recorded, not reconciled (C6252). The viewed exterior image (1200x675) shows a circular cluster of amber points on black, lit in darkness; the interior conservation record (1068x1068) shows the two projector fixtures, the circular disk with metal squares, wiring, and the label "2004.1618 / BEFORE TREATMENT" (C6251, C6252). Conservation history is part of the behaviour: the lights had stopped working after acquisition; the original light source is believed to have been four bare 40W incandescent bulbs, later replaced; with too-even LED light "all the acrylic rods in front [were] solidly illuminated, with almost none of the flickering necessary for the required 'unstable' effect", and treatment restored the flicker (C6251). The viewer therefore sees a dark box face spangled with amber points that brighten, dim and shift at random, never repeating the same pattern. The unevenness of bare bulbs is part of the mechanism -- even light kills it.

**Takeaway:** put the light inside a box, put a motor between the light and the face, and let the face be ninety rods of different thickness. The flicker is the work, and it never repeats.

## M2 -- The light box as a diffusing screen: blurred blobs, not points

Boite a lumiere instable (W07, 1965, painted wood and metal, motor, light bulbs, Plexiglas, 61 x 61 x 33 cm; sold at Sotheby's, authenticity confirmed by Barbara Garcia-Rossi) is the large square light box, roughly twice the face size of W05-W06 but shallower in proportion (C6260). The viewed image (1000x1209, assigned by elimination from the Invaluable artist page, flagged) shows the box standing alone in a dark studio: a black frame with a frosted Plexiglas window full of soft, out-of-focus warm blobs in a grid -- the internal lights seen through a diffusing screen, so the viewer sees glowing patches rather than sharp points (C6260). The blur is the mechanism made visible: screens, filters and lenses "consistently distort the source of the illumination, so that it would undergo constant subtle shifts in its color, shape, and edge" (C6257).

**Takeaway:** frost the window and the bulbs become weather. The light is never seen directly -- only its blurred, shifting ghost on the screen.

## M3 -- The relief as a light field

Relief a Lumiere Instable (W05, 1965, acrylic on wood, Plexiglas, motor, electric light bulbs, aluminum) is a deep relief box, deeper than it is wide in the Sicardi measurement (35.6 x 35.6 x 41.9 cm; a second auction record for the same title and year gives 37 x 37 x 33.5 cm -- the two dimension sets differ, so either two examples exist or one record measures differently; both recorded, not resolved) (C6253, C6260). The motor and bulbs sit behind a Plexiglas face; the front is a field of rod-ends that glow. The viewed image (837x670) shows a black square frame with a recessed black field carrying a circular grid of warm amber rod-ends, brighter toward the centre (C6253). The viewer stands in front of a dark box and watches the points of light shift as the motor runs; the relief is the light field, not carved form.

**Takeaway:** the relief is not carved -- it is lit. A deep box whose front is a field of glowing rod-ends, brighter at the centre, shifting as the motor runs.

## M4 -- The viewer as motor: crank it, turn it

The first machine-based box (W03, 1963) is the bridge between the flat vibration paintings and the motorised light boxes: "by 1963 he built his first machine-based box, although it required the viewer to turn its handle" (C6257, prose only). The viewer is the motor: turning the handle drives the internal movement, so the work is incomplete and still until a spectator cranks it. Cylindres en rotation (W13, from 1963) is the same idea as a type, not a single dated example: interactive objects, "manually moveable" (C6256), the viewer turning and rotating the cylinders by hand, changing the visible pattern (C6254, C6255, prose only, series description). This is Garcia Rossi's clearest statement of the GRAV participation idea in a single object: the work is only complete when handled. Before migrating to Paris he was already producing "kinetic-inspired gouaches at the end of the 1950s" (C6257).

**Takeaway:** the work is still until a spectator cranks it. The hand is the motor -- no handle turned, no movement, no picture.

## M5 -- Passive vibration: the buzz without a motor

Vibration No. 2 (W02, 1962, Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden, Washington DC; shown in "Le Onde: Waves of Italian Influence, 1914-1971" at the Hirshhorn, through 3 January 2016) is a checkered pattern painting whose "buzzing lines" produce vibration "without a motor" (C6262, prose only). This is the passive-kinetic mechanism: the pattern appears to move only while the viewer moves or scans it; the work itself is static. It is the clearest early example in this catalogue of movement produced by the viewer's eye rather than by a machine, and the direct ancestor of everything that follows -- the same buzz, first made by a pattern, then by a motor, then by light.

**Takeaway:** the eye is the motor. A checkered field that buzzes as you scan it -- movement with no machine in the room at all.

## M6 -- The name as a luminous motif: the alphabet that moves

One of "his earliest light boxes, including one from 1965-66 framing his name in luminous block letters" (W09, 1965-66, prose only) is the direct precedent for the alphabet works: the artist's own name becomes the luminous motif, block letters lit from inside the box, so the signature is the image and the light is the medium. It "demonstrates a degree of conceptual self-consciousness" (C6257). Instable Light Box: Ambiguous Portrait of the GRAV Members (W11, 1966, prose only, illustrated as Figure 1.22 in Lily Woodruff, "Disordering the Establishment", Duke University Press, 2020) is the same device as a group portrait: the six GRAV members portrayed ambiguously -- legible and illegible at once -- through the unstable light (C6266). From 1966 he studied writing, developed moving primers, then studied each letter of the alphabet to produce a moving translation of its meaning (C6207); his first moving alphabet dates to 1965 and his three-dimensional alphabet to 1969 -- portraits made from the letters of the model's first name, exploring the link between speech, form and meaning (C6209). Autoritratto del mio nome I (W15, 1974, print, 50 x 70 cm, ed. 81/100, listing only) carries the device into the semiotic period: a flat, two-dimensional work, no motor and no light source in the object itself, the name as image and the alphabet as a system to be read in motion (C6260, C6254, C6255).

**Takeaway:** light your own name and let the letters move. The alphabet is a system to be read in motion -- each letter given the movement its form and its meaning engender.

## M7 -- Colour-light in flat painting: the light band painted in

Couleur lumiere (W17, 1986, acrylic on canvas, 50 x 50 cm, listing only) opens the late mechanism: a small square acrylic from the colour-light period, the flat-painting version of the light-box effect -- overlapping translucent colour planes that make a band of light seem to sit in front of or behind the surface (C6260, C6254). Couleur lumiere No. 25 (W18, 1992, acrylic on canvas, 149.9 x 149.9 cm, painted in Paris, exhibited at the Hermitage 2008 and used on the cover of the Hermitage catalogue) is the same mechanism at monumental scale; no image was obtained, the Christie's lot page fetch hung (C6263). Couleur lumiere (Khaos Programme) (W20, 2007, acrylic on canvas, 80 x 80 cm, offered by Artegrafika via Artsy, US$8,000) is the late statement: the instability once produced by motors and bulbs is now produced by colour alone (C6264, C6256; no image, the Artsy fetch errored). The viewed also-noted works show the mechanism directly: Couleur lumiere transparence (May 2007, acrylic on canvas, 100 x 100 cm, viewed 1000x1500, mapping by page order) -- blue translucent overlapping squares with a vertical rainbow light beam; Couleur lumiere decalee (November 2008, oil on canvas, 50 x 50 cm, viewed 1000x1002) -- black ground, grey translucent planes, and a diagonal band striped red-orange-yellow-blue with a thin white centre line, like a beam of light crossing the canvas (C6260, C6265). A 1991 Couleur lumiere uses "concentric arcs to build a sense of depth, with each band subtly shifting in value" (C6267), and a 1992 one is built around "the central diamond... bisected by the pure white line" (C6268).

**Takeaway:** when the motors stop, paint the light instead. One bright band -- a white line, a rainbow beam -- and the flat canvas glows from inside.

## M8 -- Rotation painted: the disc frozen mid-turn

Couleur lumiere - rotation (W19, May 2006, acrylic on canvas, 79.5 x 79.5 cm) is a square acrylic whose subject is rotation itself, painted (C6260). The viewed image (1000x1500, mapping by page order, flagged) shows a large dark disc on a grey ground, crossed by concentric red arcs that step outward in value, cut by a thin bright white ring -- the whole composition reads as a rotating disc frozen mid-turn, with the white ring as the "light" that the colour circles around (C6260). The viewer reads motion from the stepped arcs and the off-centre split in the disc, even though nothing moves. It is the whole career compressed into one flat square: the rotating cylinders of 1963, the motor disk of 1966, the colour-light of the 2000s, all held still at once.

**Takeaway:** paint the turn, not the turning. Concentric arcs stepping outward in value around a dark disc, cut by one white ring -- rotation held still, and the eye supplies the spin.

## The aesthetic logic: the box opens, the light gets out, the paint learns the trick

Garcia Rossi's logic runs in one direction for fifty years: from the flat black-and-white plane toward real light and real movement, and then -- when the motors and bulbs are gone -- back to the flat plane, carrying the light with him as paint. It starts with virtual movement, a form multiplied on a two-dimensional plane in black, white and gray (M5, W01-W02), moves to the box: real movement and light from 1962, the unstable light box in which a motor-turned disk throws light randomly through ninety rods (M1), the diffusing screen that blurs the bulbs into weather (M2), the relief that is a light field rather than carved form (M3). Then the hand takes over from the motor: the cranked box and the rotating cylinders, works that are still until a spectator turns them (M4) -- the GRAV programme in a single object, the spectator put to work. Then the name and the alphabet: his own name lit in block letters, the six GRAV members portrayed ambiguously through unstable light, letters given the movement their form and meaning engender (M6). Then the return to two dimensions, and the long colour-light research from 1978: the light band painted into the canvas, the instability once produced by motors now produced by colour alone (M7), the rotating disc frozen mid-turn (M8). At every step something is taken away -- the motor, the bulb, the box, the hand -- and the light is kept: first inside the box, then on the screen, then in the name, then in the paint. "Colour-light" is the definition he leaves: colour "manifests itself neither as a decorative element in itself, nor as a variety of mixed colours, but as a grouping intended to create a new structure of visualisation" (C6201).

Against Morellet the contrast is exact and is the doorway of this study: Morellet's motor is a rule fixed in advance with chance admitted inside it -- a phone book read aloud, pi deciding the angles -- and the picture is made by a system the artist refuses to own. Garcia Rossi's motor is a literal motor in a literal box, turning a disk of metal squares so the light never repeats; his chance is mechanical, not numerical, and it lives in the flicker of bare bulbs, not in the digits of pi. Morellet dematerialised the artist into a rule you can read in the title; Garcia Rossi dematerialised the picture into light you can only see while the motor runs -- and when the LED relighting made the light too even, the flicker died and the work stopped being itself until it was restored (C6251). Against Vasarely the contrast is one of generation inside the same circle: Vasarely's alphabet of coloured units permuted by assistants into endless serial pictures, the pattern moving although nothing moves; Garcia Rossi's box in which something really moves, the light really changing, the pattern never repeating. Vasarely pointed the three Argentines to Morellet and to his son Yvaral, and GRAV picked up Vasarely's concept that the sole artist was outdated (C6301, C6318); Garcia Rossi is the hinge of that pointing -- the man Vasarely sent to Morellet, who then built the box Morellet never built. Against Le Parc and Soto the contrast is one of scale inside the same group: Le Parc's light rooms and Soto's wires make spaces you walk through, while Garcia Rossi's boxes make rooms you look into -- a dark box face spangled with amber points, watched in half-light, the penombre the label itself prescribes (C6252). The group portrait is the proof of the circle: six men, one unstable light box, each of them legible and illegible at once (C6266).
