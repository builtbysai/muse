## The second channel

The through-line across all three eras of Grossman's work is the second channel: a signal beside the signal, already present and almost never used. In the PhD years it is the hand's motion along a ray, used to disambiguate depth. In the Microsoft internships it is the pen's hover above the surface, used as a command layer. In the Autodesk years it is the texture under a fingertip, the face of the finger that touches the phone, the gaze that falls on a menu item, the history a workflow leaves behind. In the Toronto faculty years it is the words a teacher already says to a class, read as camera directions, and the images a user keeps or discards, read as a prompt. The instrument changes every few years; the move never does: find the signal that is already there, and build the interface on it instead of adding a button, a mode, or a menu. [C35466]

**Takeaway:** Every Grossman system is a bet that the user is already saying more than the interface is listening to -- and the piece is the act of listening to the extra channel.

## Kinetic textures: the picture that breathes (Draco, CHI 2014)

The most generative-ready mechanism he ever published. Draco (Rubaiat Habib Kazi, Fanny Chevalier, Grossman, Shengdong Zhao, George Fitzmaurice, CHI 2014, pp. 351-360; Honorable Mention, Best Talk, Best Video) lets an artist add animation to a still drawing by sketching a kinetic texture: a small patch of representative objects -- a few fish, a few leaves, a few bubbles -- plus motion properties, from which the system generates a whole collection. Motion is defined at two scales: global motion, the trajectory of the collection as a whole (the school's path), and granular motion, each object's individual wiggle around that path. Two types: emitting textures, where objects are continuously generated at a source and travel along a flow (bubbles rising, steam), and oscillating textures, where a fixed set of objects sways in place (leaves, water ripples). The user sketches the patch and a path; the system fills in the collection. The paper's stated design goal is animation that "provide continuous animation effects while preserving the unique timeless nature of still illustrations" -- the picture stays a picture, but it breathes. It transferred: Draco shipped as Autodesk SketchBook Motion, Apple's 2016 iPad App of the Year. [C35467]

**Takeaway:** One drawn thing plus a path becomes a living field, at two time-scales at once -- slow drift of the group, fast jitter of each member -- with visible birth and death in the emitting case and visible phase in the oscillating case.

## Motion amplifiers: seven ways to fall (Skuid, CHI 2016)

Skuid (Kazi, Grossman, Nobuyuki Umetani, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2016, pp. 4599-4609) takes the seven classical Disney principles of 2D animation -- squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, follow-through and overlapping action, slow in and out, arcs, exaggeration -- and implements each as an independent, composable "motion amplifier". Each amplifier deforms an underlying grid, which in turn deforms the drawn strokes. The user sketches a character, records a base motion, then applies amplifiers at chosen strengths and mixes them arbitrarily: add squash-and-stretch to a bouncing ball, add anticipation (a backward crouch) before a jump, add follow-through so a tail keeps moving after the body stops. The amplifiers break a complex effect into "independent, understandable chunks", and the mix is the art. A drawn figure that moves with cartoon exaggeration is the watchable result: it squashes flat on landing and stretches long in the air, winds up before it leaps, its tail lags behind and overshoots, then settles with a damped wobble, motion following arcs, timing easing in and out instead of moving at constant speed. [C35468]

**Takeaway:** Seven named, separable deformation rules, each with an instantly readable visual signature, and a built-in composition rule -- mix at will -- so a piece can let one amplifier dominate and then rotate through the others.

## Energy brushes: wind you can paint (UIST 2016)

Energy-Brushes (Xing, Kazi, Grossman, Wei, Stam, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2016, pp. 755-766) model elemental dynamics -- fire, water, smoke, explosions -- without physical simulation. The user paints with a coarse "energy brush", a broad gestural stroke that lays down a stable, repetitive velocity field, carried by flow particles: each particle creates a local velocity field that influences and deforms neighbouring drawn shapes. The brush stroke is the control gesture; the particles are the engine; the drawn strokes (waves, fire, smoke, splashes) are what gets deformed. The artist's gesture IS the force field. The paper is explicit that prior tools produced "kinetic" motion where velocity is static over time; Energy-Brushes adds time-varying dynamics. What the viewer sees: a painted gesture becomes a wind -- smoke curls along the brush's path, flame tongues flicker and lean in the field's direction, a splash throws droplets along the stroke, and where two brush fields cross, the flow bends and eddies. The field itself is invisible; only its effect on the drawn matter is seen, like wind made visible by leaves. [C35469]

**Takeaway:** An invisible field, made visible only by what it deforms -- the brush stroke is the weather, and the drawn matter is the leaves that prove it.

## Motion sculptures: the trail becomes the thing (ChronoFab, CHI 2016)

ChronoFab (Kazi, Grossman, Mogk, Schmidt, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2016, pp. 908-918) takes a 3D animated object and materialises its motion as geometry, using four static motion-depiction techniques borrowed from 2D comic art and motion-sculpture history: motion lines (speed lines, geometry sculpted along a trajectory), multiple stroboscopic stamps (several frozen instances of the object at successive times, overlaid), sweeps (shapes sampled from many instances of a 2D curve attached to the object's surface over a time range), and particle systems (trailing elements -- smoke, fluid -- left behind). The result is a static 3D-printed object that SHOWS motion that happened; in user sessions it cut authoring time 79 percent versus traditional 3D modelling. For a generative piece the sculpture is the end state and the watchable behaviour is the accumulation: the moving object leaves its own trail behind as persistent geometry, frame by frame, until the trail IS the picture -- a natural loop structure of move, leave trace, fade, reset. [C35470]

**Takeaway:** Motion does not have to be shown by moving; it can be shown by what the moving thing leaves behind, until the leftover is the whole image.

## The Bubble Cursor: a radius that breathes (CHI 2005)

The Bubble Cursor (Grossman and Balakrishnan, CHI 2005, pp. 281-290; Best Paper, top 1 percent) is Grossman's most-cited paper and the purest example of his modelling instinct. A normal point cursor has a single-pixel hotspot; an area cursor has a larger hotspot, which makes small targets easier to hit -- but when the area covers more than one target, it is ambiguous which one is selected. The bubble cursor fixes this by continuously resizing its circular activation area based on the proximity of surrounding targets, so that exactly one target is inside the hotspot at any time. The radius is set to the minimum of the containment distance of the closest target and the intersection distance of the second-closest target, with a small gap left in practice and a morphing second bubble that envelopes a target the main circle only intersects, as a visual cue that the target is captured. Formally the technique partitions the screen into Voronoi regions -- each target's activation boundary is its Voronoi cell, so every target's effective width in motor space is enlarged to the maximum possible extent. Two controlled experiments showed it significantly outperforming the point cursor; in Experiment 1 the mean movement times were 1.250 seconds (point) versus 0.879 seconds (bubble), and performance was modelled by Fitts' law with r^2 = 0.9858 using the effective width -- as fast as if the target had really been that wide. The companion journal version is Grossman and Balakrishnan, ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction 12(3), September 2005, pp. 435-459. [C35471]

**Takeaway:** A cursor whose size is a function of the layout around it -- swelling in open space, shrinking to a pinprick as it threads between dense targets -- so empty space itself acquires a radius that pulses with the scene.

## Hover Widgets: the layer above the ink (CHI 2006)

Hover Widgets (Grossman, Hinckley, Baudisch, Agrawala, Balakrishnan, CHI 2006, pp. 861-870; patented by Microsoft; work done during the 2005 Microsoft Research internship) exploit the pen's tracking state -- the zone above the display where the pen is sensed but not touching. Pen input has three states in Buxton's model: out of range, hovering/tracking, touching. A Hover Widget is invisible during normal pen use; when the user moves the pen along a short path while hovering and then presses down, the widget appears and activates at that spot -- a marking menu, a scroll ring -- summoned by a gesture made in the air above the surface. Because the gesture happens above the surface, it creates a command layer clearly distinct from the ink layer on the surface, with no mode button and no confusion between a stroke meant as ink and a stroke meant as a command. In a formal experiment Hover Widgets were faster than a traditional command-activation technique and reduced errors caused by divided attention, because the hand never leaves the work area. [C35472]

**Takeaway:** Two layers, one above the other -- the ink below, and a ghost command layer that exists only while the pen is airborne -- so the interface has a whole second floor that appears on demand and vanishes when not needed.

## Tape drawing and ShapeTape: the ribbon remembers (CHI 2002; CHI 2003)

The earliest signature idea, and the one that chains this study to #815. Car designers draw a car's "principal curves" -- the few essential curves that define its styling -- at full scale on a wall, traditionally by laying black photographic tape. "Creating Principal 3D Curves with Digital Tape Drawing" (Grossman, Balakrishnan, Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Khan, Buxton, CHI 2002, pp. 121-128) lets a designer create genuinely non-planar 3D principal curves by drawing a series of 2D tape curves, with the trick of drawing the depth curve FIRST: the designer draws (or selects) a curve that defines the shape in the third dimension, the system shows the 3D surface that curve sweeps, and the designer draws the final curve on an orthographic plane and it is projected onto that depth surface, producing a true 3D curve. A second mechanism, OrthoTumble, smoothly interpolates the viewing matrix from orthographic to perspective over 50 frames and then lets the user tumble the camera directly, so the switch between drawing view and inspection view is gradual rather than a jarring cut. The hardware was an 8 by 6 foot back-projected screen with extended-range magnetic trackers held in each hand, and marking menus for fast command access. The follow-up, "An Interface for Creating and Manipulating Curves Using a High Degree-of-Freedom Curve Input Device" (Grossman, Balakrishnan, Karan Singh, CHI 2003, pp. 185-192), replaces the wall with ShapeTape itself: a flexible strip that senses bend and twist along its length, so the user bends the physical strip and the digital curve bends with it, and twisting the strip twists the curve. The input device IS the curve -- instead of indirect manipulation (click a control point, drag it, watch the curve follow), the hand holds a physical object whose shape is the curve's shape. Per the 2003 University of Toronto Magazine profile, Grossman wrote 9,100 lines of code for the Shape Tape software in one summer. [C35473]

**Takeaway:** A ribbon that bends in the hand and leaves a glowing 3D curve hanging in space behind it, the curve always trailing the hand by a fixed length and smoothing jitter into a clean line -- the hand draws, the tape remembers, the curve follows at a lag.

## Reaching into the volume (UIST 2004; UIST 2006; PhD 2008)

The PhD core. A volumetric display renders real 3D images inside a transparent sphere -- Grossman demonstrated it with a sucrose molecule, a human skeleton, a helicopter flying over a wire-frame mountain range -- viewable from 360 degrees with true depth. "Multi-Finger Gestural Interaction with 3D Volumetric Displays" (Grossman, Wigdor, Balakrishnan, UIST 2004, pp. 61-70; Best Paper, top 1 percent) gives the first direct-manipulation gestural vocabulary for such displays: the user's fingers are motion-tracked, so they reach in and around the enclosure and grab, move, and manipulate the virtual objects with multi-finger gestures. The companion selection paper, "The Design and Evaluation of Selection Techniques for 3D Volumetric Displays" (Grossman and Balakrishnan, UIST 2006, pp. 3-12), names and measures a problem that only exists in true volumetric displays: a ray cast from the hand through a true 3D volume intersects MULTIPLE targets at different depths, so the intended target is ambiguous -- and treats the hand's motion along the ray itself as the disambiguation control, moving forwards and backwards to cycle through the objects the ray intersects, keeping selection in the same gesture as pointing rather than bolting on a button or a mode. Both papers feed the PhD thesis, "Interaction with Volumetric Displays" (2008). [C35474]

**Takeaway:** Selection as a grasp in depth -- a hand reaching into a glass sphere, fingers closing around a molecule that hangs in real space, with the ambiguity of "which layer did you mean?" resolved by moving along the ray itself.

## Magic Finger: the surface decides (UIST 2012)

Magic Finger (Xing-Dong Yang, Grossman, Wigdor, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2012, pp. 147-156) inverts the usual touch setup: instead of instrumenting the surface, instrument the FINGER. A thimble-like device combines a low-resolution, high-speed optical-flow sensor (tracks 2D movement, so any surface becomes a touch screen) with a higher-resolution micro RGB camera that reads the texture of whatever is being touched. A technical evaluation showed 22 everyday textures recognised (32 textures overall, 98.9 percent accuracy). Texture becomes an INPUT channel -- the paper's own claim is that while texture had been explored as an output channel for touch input, no prior work used texture as an input channel. The same swipe does different things on wood, cloth, skin, or paper: swiping your shirt answers the phone, touching your face starts voice recording. The finger carries its own sensing everywhere, so input is available on any surface, with no cameras to occlude and no environment to instrument. [C35475]

**Takeaway:** The world as a set of labelled touch-zones -- the same motion means a different thing on each material, so context is carried by the surface itself, not by a menu.

## ToolClips and the learnability programme (CHI 2009-2011)

The Autodesk decade's central question was how people learn complex software, and Grossman attacked it as a deliberate sequence. First the foundations: "A Survey of Software Learnability" (Grossman, Fitzmaurice, Attar, CHI 2009, pp. 649-658; Honorable Mention), which found the field in open disagreement about how learnability is defined, measured, and evaluated, and derived a new evaluation method, the question-suggestion protocol -- users are prompted to ask questions and make suggestions as they go, which exposed significantly more learnability issues than think-aloud. Then the systems. ToolClips (Grossman and Fitzmaurice, CHI 2010, pp. 1515-1524; Honorable Mention) augments the humble tooltip with short video: hover over a tool and, instead of a line of text, you get a brief clip showing the tool in use, in context, inside the application. In a second study, users with ToolClips completed 7 times as many unfamiliar tasks as users with a commercial, professionally developed online help system, and retained the information, performing tasks significantly faster a week later. And it is Grossman's cleanest technology-transfer story: ToolClips became a multi-product Autodesk component and a registered trademark, shipping in AutoCAD, Revit, 3ds Max, Inventor, and Maya, reaching millions of users. The programme continues through "Strategies for Accelerating On-line Learning of Hotkeys" (Grossman, Dragicevic, Balakrishnan, CHI 2007, pp. 1591-1600), CommunityCommands (a command recommender, UIST 2009, deployed as an Autodesk App Store product, IAAI 2014 Innovative Application Award), Chronicle (workflow histories, UIST 2010), and Sketch-Sketch Revolution (a tutorial system generated from a real expert's recorded workflow, UIST 2011, pp. 373-382), which coaches the hand stroke by stroke, not just the menu choices. [C35476]

**Takeaway:** Help where the confusion is, at the moment of need, demonstrating rather than describing -- a small label that unfolds into a tiny moving picture, in place, looping, silent.

## Skillometers: the gap, made visible (UIST 2013)

Skillometers (Malacria, Scarr, Cockburn, Gutwin, Grossman, UIST 2013, pp. 321-330) attack the "satisficing" problem: users stick with novice procedures even when they know faster ones exist. A Skillometer is a lightweight display that visualises the benefits of a better technique: it shows the user's past performance, the time they WOULD have saved had they used the faster method (hotkeys instead of mouse menu selections, say), and the specific binding to learn. The HotkeySkillometer prototype demonstrably accelerated hotkey learning in a controlled experiment. It is a feedback loop aimed at the gap between actual and possible behaviour -- and the watchable image is a small meter that fills as you repeat a slow action, showing a ghost of the faster path alongside it, a "you could have saved X seconds" counter that grows. [C35477]

**Takeaway:** The distance between what you did and what you could have done, kept as a visible, accumulating quantity -- the interface as a mirror that shows the faster self beside the actual one.

## YouMove: the ghost lets go (UIST 2013)

YouMove (Anderson, Grossman, Matejka, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2013, pp. 311-320) is a Kinect-based movement trainer built around a 3.2 by 1.8 metre half-silvered mirror, which shows the learner their own reflection with the recorded expert movement overlaid as a ghost. Training proceeds in stages that gradually reduce guidance and feedback -- full ghost, fading ghost, no ghost -- scaffolding withdrawn on a schedule. In a user study it improved learning and short-term retention 2 times versus a traditional video demonstration. The visible drama is convergence: two bodies becoming one, and then the guide letting go -- your reflection shadowed by a translucent second body doing the movement correctly, the ghost solid at first, then thinning and fading stage by stage until you are moving alone, and the moment your movement matches, the two bodies coincide. [C35478]

**Takeaway:** The strongest narrative arc in his work: the guide is solid, then thins, then lets go -- and the proof of learning is the moment two figures become one.

## Duet: one interaction, two bodies (CHI 2014)

Duet (Chen, Grossman, Wigdor, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2014, pp. 159-168; Best Paper, top 1 percent; People's Choice Best Talk) treats a smart phone and a smart watch as one instrument, coordinated by their spatial configuration: the watch becomes a tool palette while annotating text on the phone; a simultaneous pinch-to-close swipe on both devices mutes their notifications; and the watch's orientation senses WHICH PART of the finger touches the phone -- pad of the finger writes, side of the finger scrolls, knuckle selects -- so mode switches happen by rotating the hand, not by tapping a menu. Input and output, visual and tactile, are extended from each device onto the other. It is the same move as Magic Finger one level up: a signal that is already there, unnoticed, carrying meaning -- here the finger itself is a multi-mode input device, and the watch is the sensor that reads which face of it landed. [C35479]

**Takeaway:** One interaction split across two bodies -- a palette blooms on the wrist as the phone is touched, a gesture on both at once silences both, the same fingertip does three jobs depending on which face of it lands.

## Object-Oriented Drawing: the property is the object (CHI 2016)

Object-Oriented Drawing (Xia, De Araujo, Grossman, Wigdor, CHI 2016, pp. 4610-4621; Best Paper, top 1 percent) attacks the WIMP interface at its root -- not by adding gestures to menus, but by deleting the menus and turning the ATTRIBUTES themselves into manipulable objects. A colour, a stroke width, a layer is not a value in a panel but a thing on the canvas that can be moved, cloned, linked, and freely associated with drawing objects; attribute-level blending and undo are demonstrated the same way. The application supports simultaneous touch and pen input. An expert assessment found that direct physical manipulation of Attribute Objects lets users quickly perform interactions that were previously tedious or even impossible, with a coherent and consistent interaction experience throughout. The "object" in object-oriented drawing is not the drawing; it is the property. [C35480]

**Takeaway:** Properties as things -- a colour you can pick up, a width you can stretch, a layer you can hand to another shape -- so the interface is made of the same stuff as the picture.

## DreamSketch: the sketch parents a family (UIST 2017)

DreamSketch (Kazi, Grossman, Cheong, Hashemi, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2017, pp. 401-414) is the fabrication-era counterpart to Draco: where Draco adds continuous ambient motion to a still drawing, DreamSketch adds generative multiplicity to a rough sketch. The user coarsely sketches the design context -- a chair's surroundings, a bracket's load points, a manikin for scale -- then a generative-design algorithm (topology optimisation) produces multiple 3D solutions augmented into the sketched scene. The sketch defines the problem loosely and ambiguously; the algorithm fills the space of solutions; the designer navigates between them. What the viewer sees: a loose pencil sketch of a room, and inside it a dozen ghostly chair-variants blooming, morphing and swapping as the solver runs -- the same sketch parenting a family of forms, each one a different answer to the same loose question. [C35481]

**Takeaway:** Ambiguity in, multiplicity out -- one loose sketch, a family of forms, and the design is the act of choosing between siblings.

## Geppetto: edit the feeling, not the parameters (CHI 2019)

Geppetto (Desai, Anderson, Matejka, Coros, McCann, Fitzmaurice, Grossman, CHI 2019, Paper No. 369, pp. 1-14; Best Paper, top 1 percent) is an interactive, data-driven system for designing expressive robot behaviours by editing in a SEMANTIC space instead of a parameter space. Designing a robot's motion normally means editing a large number of unintuitive control parameters (gait pattern, gait time, speed, joint trajectories, friction constraints). Geppetto combines a physics-based simulation that captures the robot's motion capabilities with a crowd-powered framework that estimates the emotional perception of motions, and regression analysis that maps motion parameters to perceived emotion. The designer then edits "happy", "tired", "confident" -- and the system finds the parameters -- and can explore possible motions mixed-initiatively. Demonstrated on a quadruped walking robot (periodic coordinated limb movements, trajectory optimisation under kinematic, collision, and friction constraints). A user study found the system useful for developing desirable behaviours more quickly than manual parameter editing. It is the same move as the Bubble Cursor's Voronoi mapping -- a clean mathematical bridge between what the user means and what the machine does -- applied to a robot's body instead of a cursor. [C35482]

**Takeaway:** Speak in the language of the effect and let the system speak in the language of the cause, with the crowd as the translator -- a robot that walks "tired" because you asked for tired.

## Stargazer: the camera reads the lesson (CHI 2023)

Stargazer (Li, Sousa, Mahadevan, Wang, Aoyagui, Yu, Yang, Balakrishnan, Tang, Grossman, CHI 2023, Article 800, pp. 1-16) is a camera robot -- a single camera on a 7-degree-of-freedom robot arm -- that films how-to videos for tabletop-scale physical tasks without a cameraperson. The camera autonomously tracks regions of interest based on the instructor's actions (it follows the hands; in instructor shots it follows the head), and the instructor adjusts its high-level behaviour with SUBTLE CUES that are already part of teaching: pointing at a tool to make the camera pan to it, or saying to the audience "now if you look at how I put A into B from the top" -- speech transcribed and labelled for camera intent by GPT-3 (standard versus high angle, normal versus tighter framing) -- and the camera reframes accordingly. The cues are chosen to balance subtlety against false activation. A user study with six instructors, each teaching a distinct skill, showed participants creating dynamic tutorial videos with a diverse range of subjects, framings, and angles. It closes a loop that started with the learnability programme: the same how-to videos that ToolClips and Sketch-Sketch Revolution helped people LEARN FROM are now being FILMED by a system that understands the instructor. [C35483]

**Takeaway:** The camera direction is read off the teaching, not commanded -- the signals the instructor already sends to the audience double as signals to the robot.

## Promptify and the AI turn (UIST 2023)

Promptify (Brade, Wang, Sousa, Oore, Grossman, UIST 2023, Article 96, pp. 1-14; arXiv:2304.09337, April 2023) applies the learnability programme's oldest insight -- the system should teach while you work -- to generative AI. Crafting a prompt that captures the user's creative intent normally means laborious trial and error; Promptify adds an LLM-powered suggestion engine that helps users quickly explore and craft diverse prompts, lets users organise generated images flexibly, and, based on their preferences, suggests potential changes to the original prompt -- a feedback loop that iteratively refines prompts, enhancing desired features while avoiding unwanted ones. A user study showed Promptify lets users create visually appealing images on their first attempt with significantly less cognitive load than a widely used baseline tool. It is Grossman's first major generative-AI system and the hinge into his current era: CodeAid (a classroom-deployed LLM programming assistant, CHI 2024), ABScribe (human-AI co-writing, CHI 2024), IdeaSynth (CHI 2025), StoryEnsemble (UIST 2025), Script2Screen (IUI 2026), and the LearnAid AI tutoring platform deployed in University of Toronto CS courses. The prompt is not a string you write and hope, but a space you explore with a guide that learns what you like from what you keep. [C35484]

**Takeaway:** The second channel, in its latest form, is the user's own taste -- read off what they keep and discard, and fed back as the next suggestion.

## What not to build from

Two corrections, stated plainly so no later study repeats them. First, "Kinematic Templates" is NOT Grossman's: it is Fung, Lank, Terry and Latulipe (UIST 2008, "Kinematic Templates: End-User Tools for Content-Relative Cursor Manipulations"), with no Grossman authorship -- his work is only cited inside it. His related, genuine work is Digital Tape Drawing (CHI 2002). Do not build a "Kinematic Templates" piece and attribute it to him. Second, Grossman has no visual style and no images: his demo videos are plain academic figures -- black strokes on white, flat UI screenshots. Do not present any prompt's imagery as "Grossman's artwork" or "in the style of Grossman". The prompts in this study are of two kinds, and the study keeps them apart: prompts built on Draco, Skuid, ChronoFab and Energy-Brushes translate his tools' real demo motion, which is observable; prompts built on cursors, widgets and proximity translate an interaction RULE into invented imagery, and the imagery is the coordinator's, the rule his. ToolClips and Skillometers should not be built as standalone pieces -- they are widgets about learning, not systems with watchable behaviour; at most they supply a sub-rule (a looping demo at the point of need; a visible gap between actual and possible). Do not invent a "Grossman palette" or visual language. [C35485]

**Takeaway:** The rule is his; the picture is ours. Say which is which in every prompt, and never dress a rule up as his art.

## The aesthetic logic

Grossman's aesthetic, insofar as a researcher has one, is the aesthetic of the instrument that disappears into the task. Nothing in his work is decorated; everything is a measurement or a demonstration. The visual language of his papers is the language of the lab: a cursor, a bubble, a ribbon, a mirror, a table, a sphere with a molecule inside it. What makes it generative material is not how it looks but how it BEHAVES: every system has a rule you can state in one sentence, a state you can watch change, and a failure mode that is interesting (the bubble that would swallow two targets, the ray that hits three depths at once, the ghost that must let go). The pieces this study feeds should therefore be built as instruments, not pictures: a field with a rule, a cursor with a radius, a ribbon with a lag, a texture with two time-scales. The palette is the coordinator's choice and should be justified on its own terms -- the study's prompts use ink-on-paper black and white with one warm accent, because that is what his demo figures actually look like, and because a single accent makes the moving element legible against the still one. The motion should be ambient and endless where the mechanism is ambient (Draco, Energy-Brushes), and staged and scheduled where the mechanism is staged (YouMove's fading ghost, ChronoFab's accumulation). Interaction belongs only where the system earns it: the Bubble Cursor piece earns a pointer, the tape piece earns a drag, the Draco piece earns nothing but watching -- and click-to-reload is never the interaction, because none of his systems reload. [C35486]

## Evolution: three eras

Era 1, the PhD years, 2001-2008 (DGP, University of Toronto; MSc 2004, PhD 2008, advisor Balakrishnan): the question is how hands meet 3D space -- large-display 3D modelling, tape drawing, bendable-curve input, volumetric displays, and the pointing models that underpin them. The work is empirical and modelling-heavy -- Fitts' law, controlled experiments, formal models of pointing -- and the collaborators are the DGP core: Balakrishnan on nearly everything, Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Buxton, Wigdor. The artefacts are lab prototypes: a wall screen, a volumetric display globe, a bendable strip. Era 2, Autodesk Research, 2008-2018 (Distinguished Research Scientist, UI Research Group, Toronto): the question becomes how people learn complex software and make things with it. The learnability programme runs in a deliberate sequence -- survey the field and fix the method (2009), contextual video help (2010), community command recommendation (2009, 2014), workflow histories (2010), guided sketching tutorials (2011), gamified tutorials (2012, 2014) -- in parallel with new input hardware and surfaces (pen, finger instrumentation, wearables and watches, the body itself), sketching and animation (Draco, Kitty, Motion Amplifiers, DreamSketch), and fabrication (Series of Tubes, RetroFab, ChronoFab, Crowdsourced Fabrication). The collaborator centre of gravity shifts to Fitzmaurice and to a large cohort of interns and students who become the next generation (Chen, Kazi, Xia, Gannon, Lafreniere, Matejka). And this is the era of transfer: ToolClips in every major Autodesk application, Draco as SketchBook Motion, CommunityCommands, Chronicle, AppMap -- reaching millions of users. Era 3, University of Toronto faculty, 2018-present (Assistant Professor 2018, Associate Professor 2021, Professor July 2025; NSERC/Autodesk Industrial Research Chair 2019): the question becomes how people direct intelligent systems. The physical-learning thread continues (Stargazer, Loki, ASTEROIDS, immersivePOV), robots become a subject in their own right (Geppetto, "Grip-that-there", Mimic, ImageInThat), XR selection and error mediation is revisited with modern hardware (Kinematic Template Matching, PinchLens, GazeRayCursor, Adaptique), and from 2023 the generative-AI turn dominates: prompt exploration, LLM programming assistants and education, AI co-writing and co-design. The collaborator centre shifts again to his own students (Li, Wang, Kazemitabaar, Sousa, Sidenmark) and to Tanya Jonker's group on gaze and error detection. The through-line across all three eras is the second channel: hover, texture, finger-part, gaze, subtle speech and gesture, and now the LLM reading the user's intent from what they keep and discard. The instrument changes; the move -- find the signal that is already there and build the interface on it -- does not. [C35487]
