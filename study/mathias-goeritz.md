# Mathias Goeritz: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-04. ig-artist-study #321.

**Site:** Werner Mathias Goeritz Brunner (Danzig, 4 April 1915 - Mexico City, 4 August 1990), German-born Mexican sculptor, painter, architect, poet and art historian; proclaimed "arquitecto emocional" by Luis Barragan. Builder of the Museo Experimental El Eco (1952-53), a manifesto in walkable form; co-author of the Torres de Satelite (1957-58); hammerer of the perforated gold Mensajes (1957-68); coordinator of the Ruta de la Amistad (1968). Identity anchors: the building as a penetrable sequence; the monochrome wall as an instrument; the prism read at speed; the perforated gold skin; the monument to nothing.

**Doorway:** Hop from the Wolfgang Burger study: from Mexico seen through a caricaturist's egg to Mexico built as a feeling. Burger never reached Mexico; Horna did, and her camera found Goeritz's Automex factory before it found her surrealist circle. Goeritz arrived in 1949 and within four years pronounced a manifesto, raised a museum with no function, and declared that emotion was the only design criterion that mattered. The chain so far has been painters and a satirist working in frames. Goeritz works in the frame itself: the corridor, the wall, the courtyard, the highway median. This study is about what happens when the picture stops being a picture and becomes a route the body walks.

No mechanism invented; every engine below is footed to a viewed frame or an end-to-end read.

## M1. The building as a penetrable sequence

The Museo Experimental El Eco (1952-53) is the whole thesis in one building. A 530 m2 plot in Colonia San Rafael, handed over by patron Daniel Mont with the instruction "do whatever you want," became a structure with no functional program: corridors, blind walls, ceilings, rooms and openings sequenced so the visitor walks a prescribed route and the architecture produces a prescribed emotional arc, compression then release then surprise. The control parameters are contrast pairs, claro-oscuro, mass-void, horizontal-vertical. Blind walls are the main element: they force perspective, induce direction, and block the natural light from touching the interior, so the sequence controls the mood, not the sun. The opening night choreography (Walter Hicks dancers moving with La Serpiente, orchestrated by Luis Bunuel) treats the building as a stage the body performs in. Mont died of a heart attack in 1953 and the museum life collapsed within a year; the Tamayo back-wall mural was never painted; by 1955 the building was a restaurant, later a nightclub, a theater, a political meeting place. The failure is part of the mechanism: a manifesto building whose patron dies immediately is a manifesto nobody got to live in, which is why the ideas had to live in concrete elsewhere instead.

Against the chain: Carrington (#318) built cosmology inside frames; Horna (#319) staged domestic interiors as theaters and photographed them; Burger (#320) drew the room on a page. Goeritz builds the room and refuses the frame. Horna's M6 made the unmade bed a stage; Goeritz makes the stage and throws away the play. Nobody else in the chain treats walking as the composition.

**Takeaway:** Build the route before the object; fix the sequence, then let the materials serve it. Use contrast pairs as control parameters and keep the sun out so the route, not the weather, owns the mood. Watch the visitor: when the body slows at a wall and hurries through a corridor, the mechanism is on.

## M2. The monochrome wall as an emotional instrument

In El Eco's patio stands one freestanding yellow wall between dark and white walls, no door, no sign, no function. Goeritz's "psychology of color" treats the wall as an instrument that acts on the viewer's psyche the way a chord acts on a listener: one saturated plane, confronted in the open air, that detonates against grey concrete and brick pavers. Barragan's colors sit in the central space; the patio wall is the purest statement. It exists to be walked up to and stood before, and it does nothing else. This is M1's engine reduced to one element: sequence compressed to a single confrontation.

Against the chain: Horna (#319 M6) used one candle in a dark room to aim the eye; Burger (#320 M1) used one finger to aim the violence. Goeritz aims the whole courtyard with one wall. Carrington's palettes were cosmologies; Goeritz's palette is a single note played loud. The chain's painters colored surfaces. Goeritz colors the air between the viewer and the surface.

**Takeaway:** One saturated plane, no function, placed to be confronted: let color do the work of a whole composition. Watch the approach: when the visitor stops farther from the wall than from anything else and still feels it first, the mechanism is on.

## M3. Triangular prisms at landscape scale for a moving viewer

The Torres de Satelite (1957-58), commissioned by developer Mario Pani for the entrance to Ciudad Satelite, are five triangular-section concrete prisms, 36 to 52 meters tall, planted on a highway median with the painter Jesus Reyes Ferreira. The audience is in motion, drivers at speed, so the towers are designed to be read at a distance against the sky, a skyline before there is a city. The inspiration is the medieval towers of San Gimignano, Italy; the aesthetic is the manifesto's: visual impact through geometric volume and tonality. Board-formed concrete gives the surfaces a horizontal grain that catches light as the viewer moves. The tallest reaches 52 m. Declared Patrimonio Artistico in 2012. The naive credit ("Goeritz's towers") is wrong: it is triad work, Goeritz, Barragan and Reyes Ferreira, operating through what the Reina Sofia seminar calls "friendship, exchange and creative loans," not a conventional commission.

Against the chain: Burger (#320 M7) drew a sleeping giant for readers standing still with a newspaper; Carrington (#318) painted giants as citizens of a cosmology, viewed from a chair. Goeritz's giants are viewed at 80 kilometers an hour and they have to land their joke in one glance. The chain made images for the still eye. Goeritz made sculpture for the moving eye, which is the first mechanism in this chain designed for an audience that cannot stop.

**Takeaway:** Design the silhouette for one glance at speed; stagger heights and colors like an organ chord and let the cluster re-resolve as the viewer passes. Watch the highway: when the towers read as a chord rather than five separate objects, the mechanism is on.

## M4. The folded black ribbon, scale-invariant

La Serpiente, also titled Ataque, is a single continuous band of triangular cross-section folded into sharp alternating peaks and valleys, cast iron, matte or lacquered black. The band touches the ground at a few points and leaps between them; the silhouette reads as serpent, as attack, as war, depending on where you stand. Goeritz produced it from tabletop maquette to the 8 x 3 m patio piece that stood at El Eco from 1953 to 1964, so the mechanism is scale-invariant: the same fold, tabletop or monumental, because the engine is the fold, not the size. A second, different serpent predates it: the 1951 reinforced-concrete Animal del Pedregal, the raised-head serpent at the Jardines del Pedregal entrance, Goeritz's first public-space work, invited by Barragan. Two works share the serpent name; the fold is the 1953 one.

Against the chain: Burger (#320 M1) folded his satire into a kitchen object; Carrington (#318) folded hers into animals parked beside sitters. Goeritz folds a band of iron and the fold IS the content, no sitter, no kitchen, no joke attached. Horna's (#319 M5) montages folded images; Goeritz folds matter. The chain's folds were pictures of folds. This is the fold itself, and walking around it is the only reading.

**Takeaway:** One band, one fold repeated, black so the form reads as pure silhouette; produce it at every scale because the mechanism is the fold. Watch the walk-around: when the same fold reads as blade, then leg, then wave from three angles, the mechanism is on.

## M5. The perforated metal skin

The Mensaje series (1957-68): sheets of iron or tin laid over painted wooden supports, then hammered through with nails. The pattern of holes is the composition: rigid horizontal lines whose density varies, some holes punched clean through to tiny black voids that show the colored wood beneath (LACMA's 1967 Message: burgundy wood through bruised gold). Mensaje 32 is built as a quilt of many small perforated plates, each nailed on in a patchwork grid with visible seams, copper and olive patinas varying plate to plate, dark nail heads punctuating the field: armor, quilt, tiled roof at once. The mechanism is a controlled juxtaposition of smooth against jagged, reflective against matte. At a distance the surface reads as woven textile; up close, with raking light, every puncture is a tiny crater and the surface becomes topography. Three of these entered MoMA and Guggenheim collections and the series showed in "The Art of Assemblage" (MoMA 1961).

Against the chain: Horna (#319) perforated nothing; her surfaces were found and photographed. Burger (#320 M1) punctured an egg. Goeritz punctures metal by hand, thousands of times, and the punctures are the drawing. Varo's panels were built up in paint; Goeritz's are broken through by force. The chain's surfaces receive marks. This one receives wounds, and the wounds are the composition.

**Takeaway:** Hammer the composition into the surface; let the density of the holes draw, and let what shows through the holes (colored wood, darkness) finish the sentence. Watch the raking light: when the flat panel becomes topography, the mechanism is on.

## M6. Gold as spiritual technology

The metachromatic gold Mensajes (1958-62) and the gold-leaf reliefs apply gold leaf to geometric reliefs and panels not as decoration but as what the Henrique Faria release, quoting Daniel Garza Usabiaga, calls a technology of the absolute: "representations of nothingness and the absolute; through the luminosity of the gold leaf he sought to actualize a traditional relation between spirituality and light." The radiance makes light palpable and softens the object's physical boundary, blurring light and material. Time is a co-author: LACMA's observer records the gilding aging to a bruise-like greenish purple with spider-cracks, and the aging deepens rather than degrades. The panel seems to emit rather than receive light.

Against the chain: Carrington's golds were alchemical stage properties; Horna's (#319) gold moments were candlelight on skin; Burger (#320) had no gold, only ink and newsprint. Goeritz is the chain's first artist for whom gold is a system, not a color: a material whose job is to dissolve the boundary between object and light. The chain's painters used gold to say "precious." Goeritz uses it to say "nothing here is solid," and then the decades agree with him.

**Takeaway:** Use gold to unmake the object's edge; let the panel glow instead of reflect, and let time do the second half of the gilding. Watch the boundary: when you cannot tell where the panel ends and the light begins, the mechanism is on.

## M7. Concrete poetry as urban signage

In the 1960s Goeritz treats language as a building material. Mensaje de oro (1965): twelve gold-leaf panels built as a linguistic game on "oro," a palindrome, resolved through gestalt laws. Pocos cocodrilos locos (1967): a poem-mural in white steel letters across three walls of a Zona Rosa cafe, 43 square meters, destroyed in the 1985 earthquake. The mechanism: the poem as verbivocovisual object, met from any angle at any moment, poetry as architecture. "Oro" reads the same forward and backward, so the wall has no wrong direction; reading becomes walking. This is the manifesto's sequence-engine applied to text: the reader walks a fixed route and the letters produce the emotional arc.

Against the chain: Burger (#320 M4, M6) made the caption lie to the image and the reader grin at the headline; his texts were jokes with fuses. Goeritz's texts have no joke; they are the building. Horna (#319 M2) built photo-stories in sequence; Goeritz builds word-stories in steel. The chain used text to caption pictures. Goeritz removes the picture and the text stands up by itself, forty-three square meters of it.

**Takeaway:** Set the poem as a wall, not a page; choose words that survive being read from any direction (the palindrome has no wrong side). Watch the reader walk: when reading becomes pacing, the mechanism is on.

## M8. The anti-manifesto as group performance

On 30 November 1961, at the Galeria Antonio Souza, eleven artists including Goeritz, plus a hen named Inocencia, published the Los Hartos manifestos: "Estoy harto," "Please Stop," "El arte plegaria contra el arte mierda." Goeritz's position: fed up with imposed logic and reason, with bluff and artistic jokes; convinced that plastic beauty manifests most where the artist intervenes least. The mechanism is negation as creation: define the work by what it refuses, functionalism, folk anti-aesthetic, egocentric boredom, and stage the refusal as a group performance with a live hen in the cast. The text IS the artwork; the performance of refusal produces the emotion the manifesto demands. The 1953 manifesto was proclaimed at a museum opening with dancers; the 1961 anti-manifesto is proclaimed at a gallery with poultry. The staging instinct never leaves.

Against the chain: Burger (#320 M4) wrote captions that contradicted images; his negation was inside the frame. Goeritz's negation is the whole frame, a room full of people reading that art is shit. Horna (#319) never performed; she witnessed. Goeritz performs the refusal, and the hen is the witness, which inverts Horna's whole invisibilist method: here the camera is replaced by an audience and the artist refuses to disappear.

**Takeaway:** Write the refusal as the work; cast the performance (even a hen counts) and let the negation produce the emotion. Watch the room: when the text being read aloud is funnier and truer than any object in the room, the mechanism is on.

## M9. The constellation ground-plan

Osa Mayor (1968): seven polyhedral concrete columns, yellow and Mexican pink, 15 meters high, laid out on the ground in the pattern of the Ursa Major constellation beside Felix Candela's Palacio de los Deportes. An invited work, not a Ruta de la Amistad stop: Goeritz composed it against the Palacio's architecture, and because planes flew overhead, it could be read from the air like the constellation it maps. The mechanism: two correct viewing positions, and the second one is ten thousand meters up. From the ground it reads as a row of towers; from the sky it completes itself. The work only finishes for a viewer who is not there.

Against the chain: Carrington's (#318) cosmology was painted for the eye at the wall; Horna's (#319) frames were composed for the camera six feet away; Burger's (#320 M7) giant was drawn for the newspaper reader's lap. Goeritz is the chain's first artist to design for a viewer position no human in the scene occupies. Horna's invisibilist disappeared from the photograph. Goeritz designs for an absence that is literal: the seat is in an airplane, and the ground viewer gets the fragment on purpose.

**Takeaway:** Lay the pattern so its meaning lives in a viewpoint the audience cannot stand in; let the ground view be the fragment. Watch the sky: when the work is obviously incomplete from where you stand and obviously right from where you cannot, the mechanism is on.

## M10. The annular void

Goeritz's contribution to the Espacio Escultorico at UNAM's Ciudad Universitaria (1979): a ring of prismatic reinforced-concrete blocks forming an annulus, with a core of natural lava rock left untouched. The monument is the void at the center, the "monumento a la nada," a megalithic reproduction of pre-Hispanic cosmogony. The built ring frames the unbuilt earth; the artist's work is a frame for something he did not make. This is M2's single wall inverted: instead of one saturated plane to confront, a geometric ring around raw nature to be left alone. The through-line of forty years, hand-carved wood to monumental concrete, is the refusal to fill the center.

Against the chain: Burger (#320) filled every page to the edge with ink; Carrington (#318) filled her canvases with citizens; Horna (#319) filled her frames with staged worlds and then removed herself. Goeritz builds the ring and leaves the middle to the volcano. The chain's artists composed the center. Goeritz is the first to declare the center is not his to compose, which is the manifesto's final form: emotion as the design criterion, and the strongest emotion produced by what was deliberately not built.

**Takeaway:** Build the frame, not the middle; let the untouched core be the work and the geometry be its setting. Watch the eye: when it is drawn inward to the rough lava and then outward to the ring, and rests on neither, the mechanism is on.

## Evolution

1940s Spain and Morocco: hand-carved biomorphic wood, small scale, a pseudonymous first show ("Ma-Go," Libreria-Galeria Clan, Madrid, June 1946), the Altamira cave trip with Angel Ferrant (summer 1948) and the Escuela de Altamira (1949, 1950): intimate, handmade, abstraction with prehistoric guilt in it. 1949, Mexico via the Guadalajara teaching post: the pre-Hispanic encounter pushes him from painting to sculpture. 1951-53: the manifesto pivot, El Eco as penetrable sculpture, La Serpiente in the patio, the 7 September 1953 pronouncement (published 1954), the collapse of the museum life within a year. Mid-1950s to 1960s: the scale jump, reinforced concrete at landscape scale (Animal del Pedregal 1951, Torres 1957-58) running parallel to the intimate hammered-gold Mensajes (1957-68), the "Realizations" street works (1959), the "Do it Yourself" rearrangeable blocks (1960), the Los Hartos anti-manifestos (1961), stained glass and synagogue and cathedral commissions, concrete poetry on cafe walls. Late 1960s-80s: the coordinator years, Ruta de la Amistad (1968), Osa Mayor, the Jerusalem labyrinth (project span 1973-1980), the Espacio Escultorico void-ring (1979), screenprints and collages. The through-line: the same geometric primitives, triangle, prism, folded band, gold field, migrate across scales and media for forty years, and the constant is emotion as the design criterion and the refusal of function as the brief.

## Aesthetic logic: the emotional sequence

Every Goeritz work is a route through feeling, whether the route is a corridor or a hammered skin. The manifesto's demand is not a style, it is a procedure: fix the emotional arc first, then choose the materials that produce it, and refuse every function that does not serve it. The corridor compresses and the patio releases; the yellow wall confronts; the prism chord resolves at speed; the fold repeats until it is pure attack; the nail holes draw by density; the gold unmakes the object's edge; the palindrome reads from any side; the constellation completes ten thousand meters up; the ring frames the lava it refuses to touch. Scale is not a property of the work, it is a property of the route: the same fold at tabletop and at eight meters, the same triangle in a gold panel and at fifty-two meters of concrete. What the artist intervenes least in, the lava, the light, the sky, the reader's walk, is what carries the most beauty. That is the Los Hartos sentence, and it is also the whole career.

## Network

Evidenced, no inference. The working unit is the triad: Goeritz, Luis Barragan (co-author of the Torres, inviter to the Pedregal, the "arquitecto emocional" label, his colors in El Eco's central space), and Jesus Reyes Ferreira ("Chucho" Reyes, painter-antiquarian who painted the Torres), operating through friendship, exchange and creative loans rather than commissions. Patron Daniel Mont (the Sullivan 43 plot, dead 1953). Marianne Gast (wife from 1942, photographer, with Armando Salas Portugal an interpreter and conduit of the work). Madrid years: Angel Ferrant (Altamira trip, Escuela de Altamira), Ricardo Gullon, Ignacio Diaz Morales (the Guadalajara post). El Eco's opening: Luis Bunuel orchestrating the Walter Hicks dancers; Carlos Merida's abstract mural over the long bar; Rufino Tamayo's planned back-wall mural, never painted. The opposition, named and real: Diego Rivera (ten minutes of insults "with much grace" at the Palacio de Bellas Artes) and David Alfaro Siqueiros, who attacked emotional architecture as a threat to modern Mexican architecture. Public works: Mario Pani (the Torres commission), Pedro Ramirez Vazquez (the Ruta coordination, the Huichol hall mural with Ricardo de Robina), Herbert Bayer (his "Muro Articulado," the one Ruta work Goeritz praised), Felix Candela's Palacio de los Deportes (composed against), Federico Silva (the Espacio Escultorico proposal), John Lautner (the Marbrisa door), David Serur (the synagogue engineer), Manuel Felguerez (ally in the abstract-sculpture fight). Documented by his camera: Kati Horna photographed the Automex factory. Influenced, per the record: Pedro Friedeberg, Helen Escobedo. Scholars: Francisco Reyes Palma, Daniel Garza Usabiaga, Chus Tudelilla. Flagged inference, not used above: Wolfgang Paalen, whose DYN prehistoric program parallels Goeritz's Altamira years without a documented meeting.

## Study method note

Twenty-two downloads, fifteen closely inspected as images, seven identified by source-page labeling (frames 02, 05, 11, 13, 17, 18, 19). Twelve sources read end to end; the full manifesto text was not located, so nothing here quotes it as a complete document, only excerpts. The Jewish-vs-Protestant family-background conflict is unresolved and neither claim is made. The manifesto is pronounced 1953, published 1954; sources collapsing both to 1953 are noted. "La Serpiente" names two different works and they are kept separate. Frame 14 is an unverified gold relief, not the Muro Amarillo. Works described but not viewed as images, and sources cited but not read (Garza Usabiaga, Tudelilla, Reyes Palma, Kassner), are listed in the working data, not smuggled into the mechanisms. Load-bearing corrections to the naive reading, stated plainly: the Torres are triad work (Goeritz, Barragan, Reyes Ferreira), not his alone; El Eco's museum life lasted about a year, not decades; the gold Mensajes are 1957-68, not 1940s wood pieces; the 1951 Pedregal serpent and the 1953 folded ribbon are two different works; Osa Mayor is an invited aerial work, not a Ruta stop; the working method was friendship and creative loans, and Rivera and Siqueiros opposed it by name.
