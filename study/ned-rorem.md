## M1. Text first, song defined by the poem

What it is: Rorem's own definition, quoted by Song of America, a song is "a lyrical poem of moderate length set to music for single voice and piano", and he chose poems for dramatic connotation and for vowels that sound well on high or low notes, in the Ohio State thesis on his "Writing Songs" and American Record Guide 1960, and his main interest was the setting of poetry, not the sound of the voice as such, in Wikipedia, and Grove, via Wikipedia, says he "sets words with naturalness and clarity, without compromising the range and scope of vocal lines", and the watchable behaviour is a poem read aloud first, and the music following the poem's shape, never the other way round, and the words staying intelligible at every moment.

**Takeaway:** the poem must come first, and a song that makes the words unintelligible has broken the mechanism.

## M2. The "perfect song" lesson

What it is: his teaching in one sentence, reported by his pupil Daron Hagen from the first lesson, September 1981, in the attic studio at 1726 Locust Street, Curtis, "I can't teach you how to write a good tune, but I can show you how to make a 'perfect' song", and professional composers craft "inevitability", which listeners interpret as inspiration, and Hagen's gloss, learned from Rorem, is to set for "sense" first, not for "feel", and the listener must understand the text above all, and only then may emotion and colour be added, and the most important word in a phrase is generally the highest note, and the climax tone is chosen, then the line leads up to it and down from it, and the watchable behaviour is one word in a line rising above all the others, and the whole phrase an approach to it and a fall from it, and the sentence understood before it is felt.

**Takeaway:** the sense must come before the feeling, and a song that emotes before it is understood has put the colour before the word.

## M3. The double setting, mirror form

What it is: in Poems of Love and the Rain of 1963 he set eight poems twice, in contrasting settings, the poems returning in reverse order in the second half, with Roethke's Interlude set once at the apex, 17 songs in a pyramid, and the Wooster thesis calls it a mirror form producing a "psychological metamorphosis" in the cycle's subject, and Encyclopedia.com says "each poem of the first half receives a different setting in the second half and the poems appear in reverse order", and the Desto liner notes, in Rorem's own words, say "To realize the device I chose eight American poems", and the watchable behaviour is the same words returning later, upside down in order and opposite in feeling, and the listener hearing a text change its mind about itself.

**Takeaway:** the return must change the meaning, and a repeated text set the same way twice is a reprise, not a mirror.

## M4. The cycle as architecture

What it is: Rorem stressed a cycle's overall structure, song order, progression of keys, transitions between songs, and its theatricality, one overarching message carried by a unified emotional affect or mood, in Wikipedia citing Henry 1986, and Evidence of Things Not Seen is built in three named sections, Beginnings, Middles, Ends, moving from optimism through coming of age and war to death, including friends killed by AIDS, and four voices share the 36 songs, and two pianists alternate, Blier and Barrett never playing together, per Classics Today, and the watchable behaviour is songs as rooms in one house, not beads on a string, and the key and the mood of each song an answer to the one before it.

**Takeaway:** the cycle must be a house, and a set of songs with no key or mood answering the one before is a recital, not an architecture.

## M5. Tonal but dissonant, the French harmonic palette

What it is: basically tonal, but built from added-tone chords, chords of superimposed thirds, chords in fourths, polychords, parallel chords and extensive 7th, 9th and 11th chords, which give some songs a jazz flavour, in Encyclopedia.com, and AllMusic calls it "vertical extrapolations: through modality, polymodality, and chordal alterations, of an essentially tonal framework", and later instrumental works add tone clusters and altered chords, and the models named everywhere are Debussy, Ravel, Stravinsky, Hindemith, Poulenc and Satie, and the watchable behaviour is a plain diatonic tune sitting on a chord with one note too many, and the extra note is the colour, and the tune is the line.

**Takeaway:** the dissonance must colour a tonal line, and a chord with no tune underneath is decoration, not harmony.

## M6. The French and German dichotomy as a working rule

What it is: he opened his 1994 memoir Knowing When to Stop with the claim "I very early understood that the universe is divided between two aesthetics: French and German. Everything is either French or German", in Gramophone and Kennicott, and he called himself French, "an ideal, almost a religion", and Virgil Thomson called him "an American Poulenc", and Grove, for "restraint, wit, elegance and direct yet unsentimental expressivity", and he rejected Germanic system-building, no serial orthodoxy, no sonata-form obligation after 1958, no electroacoustic music, and the watchable behaviour is clarity, brevity and wit chosen over development and weight, every time, and a piece ending when it has said its thing, not when a form says it must.

**Takeaway:** the choice must be French every time, and a piece that develops past its point has gone German.

## M7. Ground bass and contrapuntal devices inside songs

What it is: the Renaissance ground bass, a slow repeated bassline in the piano's left hand under a free melody, opens "Spring" of 1947 and recurs across the songs, with ostinatos, imitation and contrary motion, in Encyclopedia.com and Song of America, and the piano is not accompaniment but a second voice that answers the singer in counterpoint or doubles the line, in Wikipedia, and the watchable behaviour is a bass line walking the same few steps again and again while the voice wanders freely above it, and the repetition is the floor the song stands on.

**Takeaway:** the bass must be the floor, and a song with no repeated ground under the wandering voice has no floor to stand on.

## M8. The piano as full complement, not accompaniment

What it is: Rorem was a pianist, and his piano parts carry motives that paint textual elements, rain, clouds, and are "wildly diverse in function, sometimes responding to the voice in counterpoint or simply doubling the vocal line", in Wikipedia, and Bret Johnson's summary of his hallmarks is "chiming piano, rushing triplets, sumptuous harmonies", and Song of America says he learned early "how to manipulate square phrases in the poetry, and how to make the text flow smoothly and naturally by means of syncopation, meter changes, misplaced accents, and other rhythmic devices", and the watchable behaviour is the piano chiming, rushing and commenting, and the singer's line and the pianist's hands two characters in the same scene, not soloist and servant.

**Takeaway:** the piano must be a character, and a piano part that only accompanies has demoted half the song.

## M9. The diary as a second career, kept in parallel

What it is: he kept a diary from youth, started at 12 in 1936, gave it up after a few months, began again in 1945, in Honest Broker and Ted Gioia, and the Paris Diary draws on journals of 1951-55, published 1966, then The New York Diary of 1967, The Final Diary of 1974, The Later Diaries 1961-1972 of 1983, The Nantucket Diary 1973-85 of 1987, Lies: A Diary 1986-99 of 2000, and sixteen books in all, five of them diary volumes, plus essay collections, Music from Inside Out 1967, Setting the Tone 1983, Settling the Score 1988, and he said "No newspaper article can be me. **Takeaway:** the diary must run beside the music, and a life with only the music recorded has lost the names the notes were written for.

## M10. Self-quotation and revision as a method

What it is: he recycled his own material openly, and Evidence of Things Not Seen of 1997 reuses two songs from 1953, "Boy with a baseball glove" and "He thinks upon his death", in Classics Today, and Miss Julie was cut from a two-hour, two-act opera of 1965 to a 90-minute one-act opera of 1978 after a bad premiere, and revised again before the 1994 revival, and The Anniversary of 1961 prints its serial tone row on the title page, a system tried once, then left behind, and the watchable behaviour is an old song reappearing inside a new cycle wearing different clothes, and a failed opera not abandoned but cut in half and tried again thirteen years later.

**Takeaway:** the old material must be reworked, not repeated, and a revision that only shortens has not rethought.

## M11. Knowing when to stop, brevity as doctrine

What it is: the title of his 1994 memoir is his working rule, and he held teaching posts for two years at a time, Buffalo 1959-60, Utah 1965-67, because "this is the kind of assignment that should not last more than two years as a teacher begins to believe what he says after that long a time and becomes sterile", in Wikipedia, and he broke the rule only at Curtis, 1980-2001, and he essentially stopped composing after 2010, "I've kind of said everything I have to say, better than anyone else", and two exceptions only, "How Like a Winter" of 2013 and Recalling Nadia of 2014, and the watchable behaviour is short posts, short pieces, short books of aphorism, and the career a series of clean endings, not a long fade.

**Takeaway:** the ending must be clean, and a career that fades has not known when to stop.

## M12. Opera by adaptation, at chamber scale

What it is: both full-length operas adapt famous plays, Strindberg's Miss Julie, Elmslie libretto, and Wilder's Our Town, McClatchy libretto, rather than inventing plots, and Our Town is a chamber opera, and the play's small-scale set and condensed narrative "was matched by Rorem's setting as a chamber opera" and "the economy of resources may well be the key to the opera's mobility and then its success", in Johnson via Wikipedia, and its final Emily monologue-aria is the song-writer's aria placed inside an opera, and is now standard soprano repertoire, and his own gloss on opera, via Holmes, is "a gift for tune and a gift for tragedy do not always join hands", and the watchable behaviour is a familiar play, shrunk to a room and a handful of singers, and one aria at the end carrying the whole evening.

**Takeaway:** the opera must be a room, and an adaptation that needs a cast of thousands has missed the economy that makes it move.

## M13. The etude by subtraction, orchestra as ten small bands

What it is: Air Music is ten movements, each restricted to a different combination of instruments, solo tuba and violin, three clarinets, three trumpets, snare drum, solo violin and pizzicato strings, solo viola with bassoon, four horns and harp, and so on, framed by two tutti movements at the start and one at the end, and the same device family runs through Book of Hours, flute and harp, 1975, Day Music and Night Music, violin and piano, 1971-72, and the "Music" series, Sky Music, 1976, harp, and colour comes from taking instruments away, not adding them, and the watchable behaviour is the full orchestra heard only at the edges, and in the middle, small unlikely groups taking turns in the light.

**Takeaway:** the colour must come from subtraction, and an orchestra that only ever plays all together has never been taken apart.

## M14. The concerto as portrait and occasion

What it is: his concertos are written for a named person and a named circumstance, and Piano Concerto No. 4 for left hand of 1991 was written for his Curtis colleague Gary Graffman, whose right hand was injured, premiered 1993, Andre Previn conducting, and the English Horn Concerto of 1991-92 was a New York Philharmonic commission for its 150th anniversary, premiered 1994, and the Mallet Concerto of 2003 was written for Evelyn Glennie on his stipulation that no unpitched instruments be used, "non-pitched percussion is superfluous, even in Beethoven... I am morally against all cymbal crashes, and feel that snares and bongos are strictly ornamental... The four elements of music are melody, harmony, counterpoint and rhythm. Rhythm is the most dispensable", in the Guardian, and the watchable behaviour is a soloist's limitation or a hall's anniversary becoming the piece's form, and the restriction announced in advance and then obeyed.

**Takeaway:** the restriction must become the form, and a concerto written for no one in particular has no portrait to paint.

## M15. The provocative utterance as criticism

What it is: his prose weapon is the aphorism, often aimed at the avant-garde he refused to join, and on why he writes music, "Because I want to hear it", and on the Beatles in 1968, their best songs "compare with those by composers from great eras of song: Monteverdi, Schumann, Poulenc", and "genius doesn't lie in not being derivative, but in making right choices instead of wrong ones", in the NYT obituary, on "Norwegian Wood", and on the Pulitzer, "the decree that bitterness is henceforth unbecoming. And if you die in shame and squalor, at least you die Official", and he attacked Boulez and Elliott Carter in print and was attacked back as a miniaturist unable to sustain longer pieces, and the watchable behaviour is a one-line verdict that cannot be answered politely, and the sentence doing the work the essay would have done.

**Takeaway:** the sentence must do the work, and an essay that needs ten pages to say what one line could say has not found its verdict.

## M16. The single-poet cycle against the anthology cycle

What it is: most of his cycles are devoted to one writer, Herrick, Darley, Yeats, Whitman in three cycles, Ashbery, Plath, Stevens, Tennyson, Koch, Moss, Roethke, Goodman, in Wikipedia, and against that stands the late anthology cycle, Evidence of Things Not Seen, 36 texts by 24 writers, deliberately heterogeneous, from William Penn and Baudelaire to Mark Doty and Paul Monette, and the single-poet cycle is a portrait, and the anthology is a self-portrait by other people's words, and the watchable behaviour is one voice held for twenty minutes, then, at the end of a life, twenty-four voices held for ninety.

**Takeaway:** the cycle must choose its portrait, and a mixed anthology early in a career is a sampler, not a self-portrait.
