Klingelhoeller was born in 1954 in Mettmann, Germany, and studied at the Staatliche Kunstakademie Duesseldorf; no source read gives the years of study, a degree, or a Meisterschueler status, and no day or month of birth appears anywhere. He is said to have been a student of Klaus Rinke in the 1970s, but that rests on a single search-summary source, the Nelson-Freeman 2008 press release, and is carried as such. His first shows come in 1978-1981 -- Wuppertal, Belgrade, the Kunsthalle Duesseldorf, a sculptors' symposium in Chicoutimi, Canada, and P.S.1 in New York -- with first solo gallery shows at Galerie Ruediger Schoettle, Munich, in 1981 and Galerie Schmela, Duesseldorf, in 1982. German Wikipedia dates his language sculptures to the late 1970s; Portikus says since the beginning of the 1980s he has pursued the relation between sculptural form and language form; the galleries date the centre-of-work claim to the mid-1980s. The two framings are kept apart here: the title-and-form concept is late-1970s, the settled programme is mid-1980s. The earliest dated works in the record are the two Mudam pieces of 1981, alphabet-like elements that can be rearranged at every presentation. [C28034]

The practice as the galleries and museums describe it in agreement is this: he uses letters, words and phrases as sculptural elements, making language itself into the basis for a three-dimensional experience, and he calls himself a linguistic flaneur, choosing words for their capacity to transfer the viewer into a different space. The words come from the press, from poems, from medical and legal texts. The title is neither an explanation of the work nor illustrated by it; titles are metaphoric-poetic formulations in tension with form, producing a deliberate openness of association. Over time he evolved from using the title as a decoding key to making it an independent composition developing a theme parallel to the sculpture. The materials run from fragile paper, cardboard and mirror glass to heavy steel and granite, with plaster, wood, bronze and, in the late star works, solid profiles of copper, brass and lead. Goldfarb's text names three characteristics that run through the whole oeuvre: horizontality -- sculptures at railing or balustrade height, "in between", a spatial condition of closure between "here" and "there"; pieces that lean against walls and become dependent on the architecture; and the linguistic relation itself. [C28035]

The twenty works inspected run 1981-2022 and trace one practice shedding its metaphor and becoming a system. Early, 1981-1987: letters as rearrangeable, quasi-legible fragments -- 25 letters from the words "Einmal im Leben" that can be recomposed at every showing (W01, 1981), 38 pieces in the form of 19 signs for "Tisch" (W02, 1981), plaster fruit making the metaphor of Frucht der Arbeit literal (W03, 1984), 28 cardboard letter-profiles leaning against a wall of which only four letters -- e, n, i, c -- can be made out, in an order bearing no relation to the word they come from (W04, 1986), the same letters piled on the floor with a circle (W05, 1987), and then outdoors at architectural scale, mirrored glass and 21 yews in a law-faculty courtyard (W06, 1987). The title explains the work, then stops explaining it. Middle, 1992-1990s: the literal turn -- the letters of the title take on volume and become the building material, cut from corrugated cardboard or wrapping paper, piled, wrapped or hung on solid constructions, legible in origin and mute in form: the deictic pair Ich bin hier, du bist hier (W07, 1992), the legal phrase Die Wohnung ist unverletzlich (W08, 1992), the apocalyptic pile Im Ruecken die Hitze einer brennenden Welt in bronze, brown paper and steel (W09, 1997), the self-reflexive Wenden sich den Bildern zu (W10, 1990s), and Implikation, where solid wood letters serve as a pedestal for a cardboard element above -- the word holds up the thing (W11, 1990s). Then, 2002-2015: the version system -- one title re-made as a cabinet whose drawer sizes follow the word lengths (W13, W14, W16), a shadow that is the projected silhouette of an older work in lacquered steel plate (W15, 2007, from a 1992 original), a chain whose coloured links are the words of the title in sequence (W17, 2015), or an echo that is the spoken title's sound wave in thin hexagonal brass bars (W18, 2015), with the rare legible exception, The City in Thomas Pynchon's Work (W12, 2002), proving the rule. Late, 2020-2022: the star-shaped floor works in copper, brass and lead, rays sized by the printed title words, the version count now explicit in the title itself -- Die Welt erzaehlt, zweifach, sternfoermig (W19, 2020) and Schneefall erzaehlt, dreifach, sternfoermig (W20, 2022). Grades: A 6, B 13, C 1 -- the six A grades are W01, W02, W04, W06, W09 and W19; the one C grade is W10, named only as one of three sculptures demonstrating the 1990s principle. Only four works have stated dimensions: W06 (150 x 400 x 20 cm), W09 (109 x 74 x 74 cm), W13 (86 x 81 x 63.5 cm) and W14 (39 x 26 x 112 cm). ## M1 -- The title that does not explain

The work carries a long, poetic, often idiomatic title -- a phrase from the press, a poem, a medical or legal text -- and the object refuses to illustrate it. The title is not an explanation and the work does not picture the title; the two develop a theme in parallel, in tension. The watchable behaviour is the viewer shuttling between reading and looking and finding no key: the phrase "Die Wiese lacht" (the meadow smiles) hangs over mirrored glass and yews, "Im Ruecken die Hitze einer brennenden Welt" (at the back the heat of a burning world) hangs over a mute pile of cardboard letters on bronze, and the meaning arrives only as an association the viewer makes, never as a label the work confirms. Seen in W06 (the title's first half refers to Ernesto Grassi on rhetoric, but the title does not describe the work), W09, W04 (the title twists the German expression "schreiendes Unrecht", blatant injustice, by changing suffixes) and throughout the oeuvre.

**Takeaway:** give the work a sentence for a title and let the object refuse it; the gap between reading and looking is the work. [C28039]

## M2 -- Letters as building material: the literal turn

In the 1990s the letters of the title stop being signs and become stuff: cut from corrugated cardboard or wrapping paper, they take on volume and are piled up, integrated, wrapped or hung on more solid constructions, "disengaged from any linguistic function". The watchable behaviour is language going mute in front of you: you can trace the letters back to a title you half-recognise, but the object is a heap, a stack, a wrapping -- the sentence has become a pile. Seen in W08 and W09 (letters piled and integrated on bronze and steel, "as pure objects, suspended in their crudest material aphonia"), W10, and W11, where the breakdown is reversed -- solid wood letters as a pedestal, a cardboard element above -- so the word literally holds up the thing.

**Takeaway:** cut the title out of cardboard and pile it up; when the letters become material, the sentence goes silent. [C28040]

## M3 -- The rearranged alphabet: re-composable letter elements

The earliest works break a word into alphabet-like pieces that can be rearranged at every presentation, so no single installation is definitive. The watchable behaviour is the work refusing to settle: the same 25 letters from "Einmal im Leben" or the same 38 pieces for "Tisch" can be laid out differently each time, and the piece is the set of possible arrangements, not one arrangement. The title already performs the mechanism -- a familiar word broken into sign-pieces that no longer spell it. Seen in W01 and W02 (both 1981, Mudam), the earliest dated works in the record, where the elements recall the shapes of characters from the alphabet and can be rearranged for each presentation.

**Takeaway:** make the alphabet a set of loose pieces; the work is every arrangement it can take, not the one on show. [C28041]

## M4 -- The lean and the rail: horizontality and wall-dependence

Sculptures sit at railing or balustrade height, "in between", in a spatial condition of closure between "here" and "there"; pieces lean against walls and become dependent on the architecture. The watchable behaviour is the object borrowing its stance from the room: cardboard profiles lean at the same slight angle as the first two that touch the wall, mirror pieces rest on the upper part of the groups, and the whole thing would fall or lose its meaning if you moved it away from the wall. Seen in W04 (28 cardboard elements leaning at a slight angle against the wall, two touching it, the rest leaning on one another at the same angle), in the Goldfarb characterisation of the practice, and in W06, where the mirrored railing is aligned with the redesigned courtyard's axes.

**Takeaway:** lean the work on the wall at rail height; let the room hold it up, and the room becomes part of it. [C28042]

## M5 -- The cabinet version: word length becomes drawer

From 2005, the Schrankversionen (cabinet versions) make the determination of the formal shape by the chosen language form directly visible: the measurements and arrangements of the individual drawers refer to the length of the individual words that give the work its title. The watchable behaviour is a piece of furniture that has swallowed a sentence: a wall-hung cabinet in plaster or aluminium with half-opened drawers, each drawer a word, the language disappeared and replaced by a drawer, the relationship between word and visualisation "exactly, and yet completely abstract". Seen in W13 (Das Meer bei Ebbe getraeumt, Schrankversion, 2006, plaster and steel, 86 x 81 x 63.5 cm), W14 (Waldrand gespiegelt, Schrankversion, 2006, 39 x 26 x 112 cm, long and low because the title runs horizontally), W16 (Ueber schmelzendem Schnee, Schrankversion, shown 2010), and the cabinet titles named in the 2008 text, Haeuser zwischen Haeusern zwischen vergessenen Haeusern and Raeume hinter Raeumen hinter erzaehlten Raeumen.

**Takeaway:** turn each word into a drawer; the sentence becomes furniture you can half-open. [C28043]

## M6 -- The shadow version: an older work projected flat

A Schattenversion takes an earlier sculpture and renders its projected shadow physical: three superposed lacquered steel plates on the floor, the title changed only by adding "Schattenversion". The watchable behaviour is a work remembering its own earlier self as a flat silhouette: the shadow, potentially already part of an object but needing to be unveiled, becomes the object, and a title about distance -- "Hier" als ein Abstand, "here" as a distance -- becomes a distance made flat. The arithmetic escalates in the titles themselves: shadow versions triple over platforms quadruple (2021). Seen in W15 ("Hier" als ein Abstand, Schattenversion, 2007, from the 1992 original), the Schneefall erzaehlt shadow versions of Sleep Soundly and All Metaphors will Come True (2022), and the floating Schwebenden, new constellations of previous shadow versions with silhouettes changed in size, multiplied and deformed into three dimensions.

**Takeaway:** cast the shadow of an old work in steel plate; the silhouette is the sequel. [C28044]

## M7 -- The chain: a sentence as coloured links

The words comprising a title are materially transferred into the sequence of a chain and represented by the different colours of the links. The watchable behaviour is a sentence you can follow link by link, hanging, draping or lying in the room: the linear flow of letters made into a literal chain, each word a colour, the whole thing countable and sequential. Seen in W17 (Wo jeder Gegenstand seine eigenen Worte so traegt wie jede Blume ihren eigenen Duft (Kette), 2015, chain links in different colours), the same chain idea in the 2016 Jeffrey Stark show title Resembling something not occurred (Kette), and In the Dreamer's Dream the Dreamed Wake Up (chain) in the 2022 Schneefall show, where the chain and the star touch on the theme of evening and dream.

**Takeaway:** colour each word a link and hang the sentence; the chain is the sentence, counted. [C28045]

## M8 -- The echo: a spoken question as a sound wave

The form does not derive from the space of the letters and words but from the two-dimensional drawing -- rendered as sculpture and exhibited vertically -- of the sound wave produced by reading the title. The watchable behaviour is a question you can see: thin hexagonal brass bars, ceiling to floor, tracing the intonation of "Will you be there?", giving an imaginary "you" and an imaginary "there" a spatial presence. This is the one mechanism in his work that starts from sound rather than print: pronunciation and intonation, not spelling, determine the shape. Seen in W18 (Will You Be There? (Echo), 2015, thin hexagonal bars of brass), recurring at Konrad Fischer 2020-21, where an Echo extends from ceiling to floor.

**Takeaway:** draw the sound wave of the title and stand it up in brass; the question becomes a room-height object. [C28046]

## M9 -- The floor star: ray dimensions set by printed words

The star-shaped works lie on the floor in solid metal profiles of copper, brass or lead, and the dimensions of the upper viewing sides of the individual star rays are determined by the dimensions of the printed title-giving words, their number by the shape of the star to be formed. The watchable behaviour is a phrase encoded in geometry at your feet: you walk around a star whose rays are long or short because the words that named it are long or short, and the version count is in the title itself -- zweifach, dreifach -- so the system is now explicit rather than hidden. Seen in W19 (Die Welt erzaehlt, zweifach, sternfoermig, 2020), W20 (Schneefall erzaehlt, dreifach, sternfoermig, 2022), and Strassen nach dem Regen, zweifach, sternfoermig (2021); the 2026 Tucci Russo show continues the form with floor stars in copper, brass, lead and steel whose dimensions derive from the letters of titles.

**Takeaway:** put the star at our feet and size its rays by the words; the title is the plan, the floor is the page. [C28047]

## M10 -- The mirror and the face in the wall

Mirrored glass multiplies readings: no privileged vantage point, each viewpoint gives a different reflection, and figurative associations arise only when looking and reading at once. The watchable behaviour is the viewer finding faces where there are none: the reflections of spherical yews in a mirrored railing can be read as "faces in the wall", the mirror surfaces on the upper part of leaning cardboard groups multiply the four legible letters (e, n, i, c) into unreadable glints, and the work changes as you walk past it. Seen in W06 (mirrored glass construction 150 x 400 x 20 cm with 16 spherical and 5 pyramid-shaped yews; the forms reduce to circle and triangle, the circle reappearing as a segment in the curved railing of mirrored lamellas) and W04 (pieces of mirror and steel resting on the upper part of both groups, the same size as the face of the bar they sit on).

**Takeaway:** put a mirror where the text should be; every viewpoint reads a different work. [C28048]

## Aesthetic logic

Klingelhoeller's logic is language taken out of the book and given weight, lean, height and shadow: a sentence is cut from cardboard and piled on the floor, a word is turned into a drawer, a spoken question is drawn as a sound wave and stood up in brass, a title's printed words size the rays of a star lying at your feet. The materials are deliberately limited and everyday -- paper, corrugated cardboard, plaster, mirrored glass, steel, granite -- in the Minimal-art tradition, and the forms are deliberately modest: leaning profiles, half-open drawers, flat steel shadows, coloured chain links. What changes across forty years is not the material but the transfer rule: in the 1980s the title is a metaphor the work literalises (fruit for the fruit of labour); in the 1990s the title is a material the work is built from (cardboard letters, mute in form); from 2005 the title is a measurement the work is built to (word lengths to drawers, printed words to star rays, sound waves to brass bars). Against Schuette's modelled figure -- the hero sunk in mud, the strangers with luggage, the bound pair under glass -- Klingelhoeller's answer is the figure removed entirely: no body, no face, only the language that would have named them, made solid enough to lean on, open, walk around, and read in a mirror. The work is always horizontal, always in between -- between here and there, between reading and looking, between the word and the thing. [C28049]
