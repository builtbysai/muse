# Francois de Troy: Study Notes

**Site:** Francois de Troy (9 January 1645, Toulouse -- 1 May 1730, Paris), French portrait painter; the Toulouse provincial who came to Paris, learned portraiture from Loir and Lefebvre, and spent fifty years painting the two courts of France at once -- Louis XIV's at Versailles and James II's in exile at Saint-Germain-en-Laye -- becoming the most sought-after portraitist of his time, the painter who could make any woman look beautiful without altering her features, and ending as Director of the Academie Royale, the institution his master's master never joined.

## The life

Francois de Troy was born in Toulouse on 9 January 1645 -- the day is disputed by a baptism variant, 28 February 1645, in museum-digital data quoting an older English Wikipedia revision (see C1436/C1486) -- into a family of painters. His father was a painter in Toulouse; his name is the first of the study's unresolved disputes: "Antoine Troy" (English Wikipedia, citing Robert-Dumesnil), "Antoine Nicolas de Troy" (French Wikipedia and Wikidata), "Nicolas de Troy" (Italian Wikipedia; Sotheby's; NiceArtGallery) -- and the Malliot manuscript calls him "Jean de Troy", a fourth variant from a source wrong on the reception year (see C1435, C1428, C1477, C1478). The French Wikipedia's note 5 explains the chain: the Mercure de France wrote that his father was "N. de Troy", which Dezallier d'Argenville expanded to "Nicolas de Troy" in his Abrege (vol. 4, p. 219) -- the "Nicolas" may be a misreading of an initial, but Breme and Sotheby's still use it, so the study gives the name as disputed and does not settle it. His mother was Astrugue Bordes; his grandfather Jean Ier de Troy was a painter-glazier (peintre-vitrier) in Toulouse (French Wikipedia only). His elder brother Jean de Troy (4 April 1638 -- 1691) was also a painter, professor of drawing at Toulouse, moved to Montpellier after 1674 and founded an academy there; he stayed provincial while Francois went to Paris (see C1441 for the brother's death date, 25 June vs 25 April 1691).

Francois was taught the rudiments by his father, "and perhaps also by the more worldly Antoine Durand" -- the hedge is in every source, and the Durand link stays conjectural (see C1452). The date he left Toulouse for Paris is disputed three ways (see C1437, C1430, C1479): 1662 (Malliot's manuscript; Lambert 1751 says he was sent to Paris aged 17), 1665 (Sotheby's, following Breme: "settled in Paris in 1665"), or aged 24, about 1669 (Robert-Dumesnil). The Web Gallery of Art says only "some time after 1662". The study uses none of the three silently; the Paris arrival is "the 1660s, the year disputed".

In Paris he studied portrait painting under Nicolas-Pierre Loir (1624-1679) first, then under Claude Lefebvre (1632/33-1675), the famous portraitist. The order Loir-then-Lefebvre is consistent across the French Wikipedia (quoting Malliot), Sotheby's and the Web Gallery of Art. The French Wikipedia notes that Lefebvre's success as a portraitist pushed de Troy to become a portraitist himself. In the 1670s he became friendly with Roger de Piles, who introduced him to Dutch and Flemish painting -- the conduit for the Van Dyck and Rembrandt models, and for the colourism Breme's monograph calls "l'apotheose du coloris". De Piles, the theorist of colour against Le Brun's line, is the intellectual patron of de Troy's whole manner.

In 1669 (1668 in Sotheby's, following Breme -- see C1438/C1426) he married Jeanne Cotelle. She is described three ways in the sources (see C1439): daughter of the painter Jean Cotelle the elder (French Wikipedia), sister of the painter of decorative interiors Jean II Cotelle (Sotheby's), and sister-in-law of his master Nicolas-Pierre Loir (English Wikipedia, Web Gallery of Art). The three descriptions are compatible -- she can be all three at once -- but no single source states more than one, and the Loir link implies Loir married another Cotelle daughter, which no source read states outright. The marriage welded the Troy family to the Cotelle family; the brother-in-law, Jean Cotelle the younger, is a candidate for #590.

His Academie career, as assembled from the French Wikipedia (Montaiglon) and Sotheby's (Breme): approved (agree) 4 April 1671, the session commissioning two portraits of "Messieurs de Metz et Perot" (French Wikipedia only, citing Montaiglon tome I, pp. 360-361 -- see C1482); received (recu) 6 October 1674 as a history painter, with the reception piece Mercury Cutting Off the Head of Argus (Mercure coupant la tete d'Argus), now at the Ecole Nationale Superieure des Beaux-Arts, Paris (Malliot calls it "Mercure endormant Argus", Mercury putting Argus to sleep, and misdates the reception to 1694 -- both errors flagged, see C1444/C1445). Adjunct professor 1692 (agreed). Professor: 1693 (Sotheby's/Breme), 1697 (French Wikipedia, Montaiglon tome III, p. 214), or 1698 (English Wikipedia) -- three dates in print, the ladder undisputed, the year not (see C1442/C1476/C1484). In November 1696 he was suspended from the Academie for one month following a dispute over the drawing of an eye -- French Wikipedia only, citing Montaiglon (see C1451/C1483). On 28 November 1699 he presented to the Academie his portrait of Jules Hardouin-Mansart. In January 1705 he moved from the professors' class to the conseillers-professeurs class. On 7 July 1708 he was named Director of the Academie, remaining until 4 July 1711; the Academie then placed him in the class of former directors, a class it created to honour him. Predecessor Jean Jouvenet, successor Corneille Van Cleve (English and French Wikipedia agree). In 1722 he was adjunct rector (Sotheby's/Breme; not mentioned in either Wikipedia article).

Note the paradox that structures his whole career: he was received as a HISTORY painter, yet he made his living and his reputation as a portraitist. He exhibited at the Salon of 1699 (well received) and the Salon of 1704, where he showed The Feast of Dido and Aeneas.

The turn came in 1679-1680. In 1679 he was commissioned to paint the Swedish ambassador Nils Bielke, his first important commission. In 1680 he painted Maria Anna Victoria of Bavaria, shortly after her marriage on 7 March 1680 to Louis, Grand Dauphin, heir to the throne. Louis XIV had chosen him for the Dauphine's portrait, and the French Wikipedia (quoting Malliot) says he travelled to Bavaria for it. From that point his career as a court painter was assured (Sotheby's: "from that time his career as a painter of the court was insured"). His clients included Madame de Montespan, her son by the king Louis Auguste, Duke of Maine, and the Duke's wife Louise Benedicte de Bourbon; by the end of 1680 he was concentrating on portraits of the king's legitimised children by Montespan: the Duc du Maine (1670-1736), Mademoiselle de Nantes (1673-1743), the second Mademoiselle de Blois (1677-1749), and the Comte de Toulouse (1678-1737). In 1696 Bossuet commissioned him to paint the three sons of the Grand Dauphin -- the Duc de Bourgogne (1682-1712), the Duc d'Anjou, later Philip V of Spain (1683-1746), and the Duc de Berry (1686-1714) -- as a gift for the Grand Duke of Tuscany. The city of Paris also commissioned him: in 1682 a large allegorical group portrait for the birth of the Duc de Bourgogne (since destroyed), and again in 1709, 1716, and twice in 1725 for very large portraits of the same type. Only the ex-voto of 1725 survives, painted with his son Jean-Francois de Troy, now in the church of Saint-Etienne-du-Mont, Paris.

He worked continuously in court circles for almost fifty years and was admired for capturing the upper classes and their preoccupation with manners and fashion. The sources repeat, in near-identical words, that he had the ability to make any woman look beautiful, which made him sought after by all women (English Wikipedia, citing Robert-Dumesnil; French Wikipedia). Malliot's version: "Without altering their features, he had the art of adding graces and nobility to them."

In the 1690s de Troy became the principal painter to the court of King James II in exile at Saint-Germain-en-Laye (the Italian Wikipedia dates the appointment to 1690; see C1448). James II had been in residence there from 1688, after the Glorious Revolution. De Troy painted the King, the Queen (Mary of Modena), the young Prince of Wales (James Francis Edward Stuart), and the aristocrats who had remained faithful to them. He was the master there of Alexis Simon Belle (1674-1734), his best student, who painted de Troy's own portrait about 1703 (his reception piece at the Academie, 4 August 1703; now Versailles, MV 5873). Between 1698 and 1701, a period of peace between France and Great Britain, Jacobites could cross the Channel carrying portraits of James Francis Edward Stuart and his sister Princess Louisa Maria. De Troy was then James II's only court painter and needed Belle's help to produce the many portraits commissioned from him. This workshop production, with several versions of the same portrait, is central to how his Jacobite portraits circulated: the National Portrait Gallery's Richard Talbot, Earl of Tyrconnell (NPG 1466, 1690-1691) exists in versions in London, Dublin (National Gallery of Ireland, three versions), and Rudding Park, and it is not established which, if any, is the original (see C1446/C1471). The Tyrconnell is listed by the NPG as "attributed to Francois de Troy" -- Breme and Malcolm Rogers both favoured de Troy in 1992, and he "remains the likeliest candidate", but there has been "no general agreement" on the painter, and Belle and Hyacinthe Rigaud were also suggested. The study presents it as attributed, not secure.

Through the legitimised princes he became attached to the court of the Duc and Duchesse du Maine at Sceaux, which they acquired in 1700. The Duchesse, Louise-Benedicte de Bourbon, was lively, fascinated by the arts and sciences, and spent her husband's fortune on entertainments; in 1703 she founded an imaginary order of chivalry, the Ordre de la "Mouche a Miel" (Order of the Honey Fly), of which she was "Reine des Abeilles" (Queen Bee). Sceaux became famous for wit and extravagance, its "Nuits de Sceaux" (sixteen Grandes Nuits between April 1714 and May 1715). De Troy's Sceaux works, per Sotheby's (following Breme): individual portraits -- the Duc du Maine 1692 (destroyed, known through a workshop version), 1693 (known through a print by Pierre Drevet), and 1715 as Grand Maitre de l'Artillerie; the Duchesse du Maine 1694 (Musee des Beaux-Arts, Orleans); The Astronomy Lesson of the Duchesse du Maine, c. 1705-1710; and The Feast of Dido and Aeneas, 1704, exhibited at the Salon of 1704. More than fifty figures, most of them portraits from life of the Sceaux court in mythological guise: the Duc du Maine as Aeneas (plumed helmet, golden cuirass, long blue cloak), the Duchesse as Dido reclining on a day bed, their children as Ascanius/Cupid and infants in the arms of wet-nurses, Malezieu as master of ceremonies, Genest, the Comtesse d'Estrees, the Marechale de Villars, and the first President de Mesmes among the musicians in the distance. De Troy painted himself at the foot of a monumental column wrapped in drapery, in antique costume, holding a portfolio of drawings and a porte-mine, looking out at the viewer (the identification rests on Claude-Marin Saugrain's 1716 description, quoted via Breme/Sotheby's -- see C1450). The Mercure de France of May 1730, reporting his death, called it a work of astonishing composition and "the ultimate endeavor in painting and a masterpiece of art". Sotheby's dates the execution to April-August 1704, a few weeks after the March 1704 birth of the last child shown.

Apart from his son, his students included Andre Bouys and John Closterman. Bouys is confirmed -- "studied in Paris under Francois de Troy" (WGA); agree 1687/88, recu 1689 on two portraits of his contemporaries, the painter Charles de La Fosse and the sculptor Etienne le Hongre (both now Versailles); conseiller 1707; he engraved de Troy's work. Closterman rests on a Wikipedia list only, with no second source found this run (see C1480); the study does not lean on the pupilage without one. His son Jean-Francois de Troy (27 January 1679, Paris -- 26 January 1752, Rome) studied under him, was received at the Academie Royale on 28 July 1708, became one of France's leading history painters, invented the "tableaux de modes" (paintings of fashionable contemporary life), and was Director of the French Academy in Rome from 1738. Wikipedia says his July 1708 admission came "certainly not without a little help of his father who was then the director of that institution" -- editorial wording, not a documented fact; the admission is evidenced, the "help" is inference (see C1481). Father and son must not be confused -- a confusion that online aggregator sites still make (see C1454, C1456, C1460): works dated after Francois's death, among them Time Unveiling Truth (1733, National Gallery NG6454) and The Judgement of Paris (1734), belong to Jean-Francois.

Francois de Troy died in Paris in 1730, aged 85. The day is disputed: 1 May 1730 (English and French Wikipedia, Benezit, the 1863 "Etat civil de quelques artistes francais", Lambert 1751) versus 21 November 1730 (Italian, Spanish, and Russian Wikipedia) -- see C1440/C1415. The Mercure de France issue of May 1730 already carried an obituary notice of his death (cited in Sotheby's lot 264), which supports the May date and is hard to reconcile with a November death. The study uses 1 May 1730 and notes the 21 November variant. He was also an engraver: among his engravings is one of the funeral in 1683 of Maria Theresa of Austria, wife of Louis XIV.

Unlike Vouet, Le Brun, Mignard and La Fosse, no source this run records de Troy travelling to Italy. One low-quality site claims it is "widely accepted that Francois de Troy spent time in Italy" -- no other source read mentions any Italian journey (see C1447). His classicism, like Le Sueur's, was learned at second hand -- from Lefebvre, Loir, de Piles and prints. The study does not imply a Roman sojourn.

## The evolution: history painter by training, portraitist by inheritance, group portraitist by mastery

Three phases, all evidenced by the works. The through-line is a narrowing that becomes an expansion: a painter who enters the Academie as a history painter, is forced by a teacher's death into portraiture, and then makes portraiture big enough to hold history inside it -- fifty sitters in one mythological feast.

**1. Toulouse and early Paris, to c. 1675 -- the history painter.** The early known work is history, religious and mythological painting, plus tapestry designs for Madame de Montespan. The Web Gallery of Art calls the early mythological pictures "unexceptional" -- Susanna and the Elders and Lot and his Daughters (both Hermitage) are listed there among his early works, but the 1721 Lot and his Daughters is attributed by Didier Aaron to Jean-Francois, and the attribution is unresolved, so both are excluded from the catalogue (see C1458). The reception piece, Mercury Cutting Off the Head of Argus (W01, 1674, ENSBA Paris), is the phase in one object: a history subject, painted to enter the Academie as a history painter, with no visual description found in any source read. A portrait of Louis-Francois Lefevre de Caumartin dated 1668 (Chalons-en-Champagne, Joconde) sits uneasily against Sotheby's claim that a pair of portraits of c. 1680-85 are "two of the earliest recorded portraits by the artist known today" -- if the 1668 attribution stands, the superlative fails (see C1453). The phase ends with Lefebvre's death in 1675: de Troy "changed his direction to become a portrait artist, aiming at commissions from Lefebvre's former clients" (English Wikipedia; WGA). The teacher's death is the hinge of the career.

**2. The portrait turn, 1675-c. 1698 -- the court portraitist.** The two 1679-80 court commissions are the pivot: Nils Bielke, the Swedish ambassador (W02, 1679), the first important commission; then Maria Anna Victoria of Bavaria, the Dauphine (W03, 1680), commissioned by Louis XIV, painted shortly after her marriage to the Grand Dauphin on 7 March 1680 -- Sotheby's: from that time his career as a painter of the court was insured. From here the catalogue is almost entirely portraiture, and the history painter in him is absorbed into portraiture as mythological guise. The single sitter dominates: bust or three-quarter length, in action or with an attribute -- Charles Mouton (W05, 1690, Louvre), seated, legs crossed, playing the lute/theorbo, the WGA text noting it "suggest[s] an awareness of Italian seventeenth-century portraiture" while his more typical portraits of women follow the free movement of the Flemish tradition; Richard Talbot, Earl of Tyrconnell (W06, 1690-1691, NPG 1466, 1156 x 889 mm VERIFIED), in armour with the Garter James nominally invested him with in November 1690 -- the Garter inaccurately painted, buckle on top instead of bottom, the dark blue ribbon now nearer the pale blue of the Saint-Esprit; Elisabeth Charlotte, Duchess of Orleans, with a Moorish page (W04, 1680), the subordinate figure as a device for displaying rank; a couple as Venus and Paris (W07, 1691, Louvre, 150 x 120 cm REPORTED); Lady Mary Herbert as Diana (W10, c. 1692, Coughton Court, 125 x 98.5 cm REPORTED), a Catholic Jacobite-line noblewoman painted in the guise of the goddess of the moon and of chastity. The figure carries the meaning; the setting is minimal. The Saint-Germain work runs alongside the Versailles work from the 1690s: the same hand paints the Sun King's court and the exiled king's court, and the same sitters recur in versions -- Princess Louisa Maria Stuart (W16, 1700, inscribed on the reverse "Fait a S Germain / Par f de Troy / En 1700", 70 x 58 cm REPORTED), half length in a blue dress holding a posy of flowers; Prince James Francis Edward Stuart (1701, Scottish National Gallery, 76/77 x 64 cm), "probably commissioned by James's mother to mark his thirteenth birthday in 1701", the National Galleries of Scotland praising "its exquisite rendering of plush velvet and silver thread".

**3. The group portrait and the late years, 1698-1730 -- the court in miniature.** The mature work embeds the figure in a social scene: couples in mythological guise (W07), mothers with children (W12, the Count of Brionne's wife and son, 1697; W18, the Duchess of La Ferte-Senneterre with the Duke of Anjou on her lap and the Duke of Brittany, 1710; W19, the Countess von Cosel and her son as Venus and Cupid, c. 1712-1715, North Carolina Museum of Art), and finally the two great allegorical groups -- The Feast of Dido and Aeneas (W14, 1704, 160 x 202 cm REPORTED), more than fifty figures, most of them portraits from life, exhibited at the Salon of 1704, and The Astronomy Lesson of the Duchesse du Maine (W17, c. 1705-1710, 99.5 x 130 cm REPORTED for the Wadsworth Atheneum version; a second signed version, 96 x 128 cm, at Sceaux), where the Duchesse, recognizable by her small stature, large mouth and heavy make-up, wears a satin dress trimmed with gold embroidery and a blue velvet-silk cloak with fleurs-de-lis, gestures toward a large book beside an armillary sphere, while Malezieu sits facing her on a stool, not an armchair, to mark the difference in rank, and the Abbe Genest stands at the doorway holding up a magnifying glass, a joke on Malezieu's poor sight ("Mal aux yeux"). The scene is set in a vestibule at the entrance of a library shown in perspective, with some of the 3,000 volumes of the Duc's library visible, a barometer on the bookcase, and a clock whose face is supported by Time and Love. The late Ex-Voto (W20, 1725/1726, Saint-Etienne-du-Mont) returns to the large civic group portrait, the format of his first city commission in 1682, painted with his son. The late mythological portraits continue to the end: Portrait of a Lady as Ceres, c. 1724; Portrait of a Lord Accompanied by a Black Page, c. 1725, Quimper.

What changed, in one line per register. Light: no source read gives a technical account of a change in lighting across the career, so no lighting doctrine is claimed. What the sources do record: Sotheby's characterises his mature manner as "dynamic compositions, an encompassing clair-obscur, warm coloring, fluid application of impasto, a preference for delicate glazes and a free touch"; the Astronomy Lesson is "warm colours in thick impasto, with a soft brush". A move from the darker, more sculptural single-figure portraits of c. 1690 (Mouton, Talbot) toward the warmer, more brightly lit interior scenes of c. 1704-1710 (Dido, Astronomy Lesson) is visible in the subjects and in the contemporary descriptions, but the sources do not state it as a lighting doctrine, and it is recorded here as an observed tendency, not a verified technical shift. Palette: Sotheby's describes "warm coloring" as characteristic. Specific colour notes: the Duc du Maine as Aeneas wears "a plumed helmet, a golden cuirass and a long blue cloak" (W14); the Duchesse du Maine wears "a blue velvet-silk cloak with fleurs-de-lis" over gold-embroidered satin (W17); the Duchesse du Maine state portrait (W11, 1694, 227.4 x 158 cm REPORTED) pairs a brown-and-silver gown with a deep blue mantle. Blue mantles, gold embroidery, warm flesh and warm grounds recur in the mature descriptions. No source supports a claim of a palette shift from cool to warm or the reverse, so none is made. Figure: single sitters, bust or three-quarter length, in action or with an attribute (Mouton with his lute, Talbot in armour with the Garter, the Duchess of Orleans with her page) to the figure embedded in a social scene -- couples in mythological guise, mothers with children, and finally the 50-plus-figure allegorical group and the conversational interior, where rank is shown by furniture (stool vs armchair), by who gestures and who enumerates, and by a joke figure at the door. Scale: scale grows with the career and with the sitter's rank. Early portraits are cabinet to three-quarter scale (Talbot 115.6 x 88.9 cm; Lady Mary Herbert 125 x 98.5 cm; Louisa Maria 70 x 58 cm). The mature state portrait reaches 227.4 x 158 cm (W11, reported) and the group allegories 160 x 202 cm (W14). The late Ex-Voto is described as a "very large" portrait of Parisian dignitaries, though no dimensions were found. The one counter-example is the Cleopatra (W08) at a reported 39 x 50 cm, which is either a small cabinet version or a mis-recorded size (see C1463). Authorship: authorship becomes collaborative and then dynastic. The 1690s Saint-Germain work was produced at a volume that required help: by 1698-1701 de Troy "was then James II's only court painter and needed the help of Belle, his best student, to produce the many portraits commissioned from him". The late Ex-Voto (W20) is explicitly co-painted with his son Jean-Francois. Attribution in the record is correspondingly unstable: the Talbot is only "attributed to" de Troy (W06); the La Fontaine is "attributed to" (see C1473); the Louisa Maria exists in versions credited to father and to son (see C1464); and the whole father/son boundary is the central hazard of this catalogue, with Time Unveiling Truth, the Judgement of Paris, Lot and his Daughters and the Death of Lucretia all circulating under the wrong de Troy on commercial sites.

## M1 -- The portrait historie: the likeness is the contract, the myth is the flattery

De Troy's driving mechanism is the "portrait historie": a true, recognisable likeness of a named living person, dressed and posed inside a mythological or historical role -- Venus and Paris, Diana, Ceres, Cleopatra, Dido and Aeneas. The likeness is the contract; the myth is the flattery. The Sotheby's essay on The Feast of Dido and Aeneas states the rule plainly: at this level of high society no painter would risk mixing up a Princess of the Blood with a lady's companion or a simple servant, so every one of the fifty-plus figures had to carry its own physiognomy, and rank was signalled by nothing but the mythological pretext and the placing. The viewer must know the story of Dido and Aeneas to put each sitter in the right social position. The same mechanism works in the single portrait: a duchess becomes Diana or Ceres, a countess becomes Venus with her son as Cupid, a couple becomes Venus and Paris. The sitter is recognisable -- the Duchesse du Maine is identifiable in The Astronomy Lesson by her small stature, wide mouth, and heavily rouged cheeks, per Sotheby's -- and simultaneously elevated into myth. The mechanism is what lets a portraitist be a history painter without leaving portraiture: the myth is a costume the sitter rents for the sitting, and the likeness is what the sitter takes home.

**Takeaway:** put the likeness in costume -- a named living person, recognisable from their small stature, wide mouth and rouged cheeks, dressed as Diana or Dido or Ceres, and let the myth do the flattery while the likeness does the work: the viewer must know the story to know who is who, and the costume must never hide the face it is flattering.

## M2 -- The flattering brush: coloris, warm colouring, impasto and glazes

Contemporaries praised him above all as a colourist. Dominique Breme's 1997 monograph article is titled "Francois de Troy ou l'apotheose du coloris" (the apotheosis of colouring). Dandre Bardon, quoted in the French Wikipedia, said de Troy gathered in himself the talents of several celebrated painters: he painted eyes like Guido (Reni), noses like Van Dyck, and mouths like Correggio. Malliot adds that his works were "full of intelligence and finesse" and charmed by their colouring, and that he earned the highest praise for his portraits of women because, without altering their features, he added graces and nobility to them. Sotheby's (lot 264, following Breme) gives the technical summary: abandoning the stiffness of the effigies produced at the beginning of Louis XIV's reign (Beaubrun, Nocret, Elle), de Troy opened himself to Flemish portraiture, originating with Rubens and particularly the elegant aesthetic of Van Dyck. "Dynamic compositions, an encompassing clair-obscur, warm coloring, fluid application of impasto, a preference for delicate glazes and a free touch are characteristic of this artist." The Web Gallery of Art agrees on the models: a style of portraiture based on Flemish and Dutch models that included Van Dyck and Rembrandt. The Artcurial note on the Portrait of a Lady as Ceres (c. 1724) points to the same qualities in a single picture: the changing, moire reflections of white and gold-yellow drapery, the sheaf of wheat under the model's hand, the subtlety of the flesh tones, and the nuances of sky and clouds. The mechanism is a brush that flatters by construction: warm colouring, fluid impasto, delicate glazes, a free touch -- the paint itself is the compliment, applied thick where the light falls and glazed thin where the skin must glow, and the sitter's beauty is a property of the surface, not of the sitter.

**Takeaway:** flatter with the paint, not the pose -- warm colouring, fluid impasto, delicate glazes, a free touch, the eyes painted like Guido, the nose like Van Dyck, the mouth like Correggio, and let the brush add the graces and nobility while the features stay exactly as they are.

## M3 -- Rank is furniture: the social geometry of the group portrait

In de Troy's mature work, rank is not worn, it is sat on. The Astronomy Lesson is the mechanism in one room: the Duchesse sits in an armchair (fauteuil) as befitted a princess of the blood, in daytime clothes with unpowdered hair, confirming points of astronomy with Nicolas de Malezieu, seated opposite on a stool (tabouret, marking his lower rank), while the Abbe Charles-Claude Genest appears in the doorway holding a loupe. The furniture is the hierarchy, made visible: armchair against stool, the one who gestures against the one who enumerates, the joke figure at the door who sees the whole arrangement and comments on it with a magnifying glass. In The Feast of Dido and Aeneas the same geometry scales to fifty figures: the Duc as Aeneas addresses the Duchesse as Dido reclining on a day bed at right; the Prince de Dombes enters at left as Ascanius/Cupid, led by Malezieu as master of ceremonies; in the foreground right, a wet-nurse tends the Comte d'Eu and the infant Duc d'Aumale; in the distance a music concert, Jean-Antoine de Mesmes among the musicians. Rank is signalled by nothing but the mythological pretext and the placing -- who reclines, who enters, who tends, who plays in the distance. The viewer must know the story of Dido and Aeneas to put each sitter in the right social position, and must know the court to know why the stool matters. The mechanism is social geometry made legible: the picture is a seating plan, and the seating plan is the picture.

**Takeaway:** seat the rank -- an armchair for the princess and a stool for the tutor, the one who gestures against the one who enumerates, a joke figure at the door with a magnifying glass, and let the furniture, the placing and the mythological pretext carry the whole hierarchy, so the picture reads as a seating plan and the seating plan is the subject.

## M4 -- The pose solved on a stand-in: the sitter laid over the pose

Sotheby's lot 65 (The Astronomy Lesson) records a concrete working method: in a preliminary drawing of the Duchesse, de Troy uses another figure to stand in for her while he works out the pose for the finished painting. The pose is solved on a substitute body; the sitter's identity is then laid over it in the painting, recognisable from her small stature, wide mouth, and rouged cheeks. De Troy used a model for the pose, the Duchess sitting only for the study of her face (Didier Aaron, citing a preparatory drawing sold at Drouot, 18 April 1984, no. 81). The same separation runs through his drawing practice: the Beaux-Arts de Paris holdings give the mechanical detail -- "Etude pour un portrait de femme en Venus": black chalk (pierre noire) with white chalk heightening on beige paper, 0.367 x 0.264 m, a preparatory sheet for "Portrait de femme en Venus desarmant l'amour", dated 1698-1700; "Etude pour un portrait d'homme a mi-corps avec reprise d'une main": black chalk with white chalk heightening on blue paper, 0.263 x 0.229 m, the young man half-length, three-quarter view, wearing a wig and a barely sketched justaucorps, the left hand reworked to specify its movement; "Etude de deux mains pour le portrait de Jules Hardouin-Mansart": black chalk with white chalk heightening on beige paper, 0.255 x 0.423 m -- the hands, by their expressive gesture, are one of the major stakes of the state portrait; the two raised index fingers seem to accompany an argument addressed to an interlocutor to be convinced. The mechanism is a split: the pose is a problem solved on any body, the identity is a face and a pair of hands laid over it afterwards. The hands are the proof -- a hand study is a portrait of a gesture, and the gesture is what the sitter is saying.

**Takeaway:** solve the pose on any body, then lay the sitter over it -- work the gesture out on a stand-in, let the sitter sit only for the face, and make the hands the argument: two raised index fingers accompanying a point addressed to someone who must be convinced, the pose a problem and the identity a layer laid on top.

## M5 -- Drawing first: black chalk, white heightening, and the abandonment of trois crayons

De Troy's drawings were prized by collectors; Malliot says connoisseurs esteemed and sought them, and that for their fine finish they yielded nothing even to Van Dyck's. The Beaux-Arts de Paris holdings (French Wikipedia, citing Brugerolles, "Portraits dans les collections de l'Ecole des Beaux-Arts", Carnets d'etudes 36, 2016) give the mechanical detail: black chalk (pierre noire) with white chalk heightening, on beige or blue paper, the figure existing first as a contour in motion and only second as a painted body. And the holdings record a change of system: the Venus study of 1698-1700 "belongs to his second stylistic period, during which he progressively abandoned the 'trois crayons' (black, red, and white chalk) to favour black chalk alone". The mechanism is a simplification with age: three chalks become two, then effectively one -- black chalk for the drawing, white chalk for the light, the red chalk dropped. A Christie's drawing lot (lot 4454458) shows the same practice late in his career: "Deux etudes pour un portrait de femme a mi-corps (recto); Trois etudes de mains (verso)", black and white chalk on brown paper, watermarked with grapes, 227 x 330 mm, dated by Breme to about 1720 from the costume worn by the model at right, and comparable to the double portrait at the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Grenoble. The drawing is the design, the painting the colouring-in -- and the colouring-in gets simpler as the drawing gets surer. Young painters did not come to him for colour; they came for the likeness, and the likeness is drawn before it is painted.

**Takeaway:** draw it in black chalk and light it in white, and drop the red -- three chalks becoming two as the hand gets surer, the figure existing first as a contour in motion on beige or blue paper, the hands studied as arguments, and only when the drawing is finished does the colour wash in over it.

## M6 -- The workshop as a production line: portraits as political currency

At Saint-Germain-en-Laye (1698-1701) the workshop scaled up: with Jacobite portraits in heavy demand, Belle, his best student, helped produce the many portraits commissioned, which is why several versions of the same Jacobite portrait exist and the original is often undetermined. The first childhood portrait of the young pretender was "arranged by Belle's teacher, Francois de Troy" (Christie's, citing Edward Corp). Between 1698 and 1701, a period of peace between France and Great Britain, Jacobites could cross the Channel carrying portraits of James Francis Edward Stuart and his sister Princess Louisa Maria -- the portraits were smuggled as likenesses of a king, and the likeness was the politics. The mechanism is production at the speed of demand: one court, two painters, portraits as political currency, the master arranging the composition and the best pupil multiplying it. The versions are the evidence: the Tyrconnell exists in London, Dublin (three versions) and Rudding Park, and "it is not established which, if any, of these portraits is original"; the Astronomy Lesson exists in two period versions, neither distinguishable as the prime version (see C1449); the Louisa Maria exists in versions credited to father and to son (see C1464). The workshop is not a failure of authorship; it is the system working. A portrait that must cross the Channel in a Jacobite's luggage cannot wait for the master to finish it alone -- it must be made in numbers, by more than one hand, and the numbers are the point.

**Takeaway:** make the portrait in numbers -- one court, two painters, the master arranging the composition and the best pupil multiplying it, the likeness smuggled across the Channel as political currency, and let the versions be the evidence: when the demand is the subject, the original is the one you cannot tell from the copies.

## M7 -- The group portrait as a court in miniature: fifty figures, one myth

The Feast of Dido and Aeneas (1704) is the mechanism at full stretch: more than fifty figures, most of them portraits from life, in a single mythological feast, painted April-August 1704, a few weeks after the March 1704 birth of the last child shown, exhibited at the Salon of 1704 (12 September - 8 November). The Duc du Maine as Aeneas, in plumed helmet, golden cuirass and long blue cloak, addresses the Duchesse as Dido, reclining on a day bed at right. The Prince de Dombes enters at left as Ascanius/Cupid, led by Nicolas de Malezieu as master of ceremonies. In the foreground right, a wet-nurse tends the Comte d'Eu and the infant Duc d'Aumale. In the distance a music concert. And de Troy painted himself at the foot of a monumental column wrapped in drapery, in antique costume, holding a portfolio of drawings and a porte-mine, looking out at the viewer -- the painter inside his own picture, as a draftsman, holding the tools of the trade. For the picture he made "quite a few drawings" to prepare the masterpiece; four survive: a study for the three women behind the Duchesse's bed; a simple drawing for the figure of the Marquise de Villars lifting her veil; a preparation for two figures behind the set table (location unknown); and a sheet of strongly executed studies for the musicians in the gallery. The mechanism is the court compressed into a myth: the whole Sceaux circle, fifty people who know each other, each recognisable, each placed by rank, inside one story that everybody in the room knows -- Dido and Aeneas, the queen and the hero, the feast that ends in leaving. The city commissions are the same mechanism in civic dress: the large allegorical group portraits for the city of Paris (1682, 1709, 1716, twice in 1725), of which only the Ex-Voto of 1725 survives, painted with his son. The group portrait is the form that lets a portraitist be a history painter without leaving portraiture: the history is the pretext, the court is the subject, and the painter signs it by painting himself into the corner with his portfolio.

**Takeaway:** compress the whole court into one myth -- fifty figures, most of them portraits from life, each recognisable, each placed by rank, inside a story everybody in the room knows, and paint yourself into the corner with your portfolio, the draftsman among his sitters, the picture a feast and a seating plan at once.

## M8 -- Two courts at once, and the Academy chair: the painter who served a king with a throne and a king without one

De Troy's career is the only one in the chain that holds two courts at once: Versailles and Saint-Germain, the Sun King and the king with no kingdom. From 1679 he is Louis XIV's choice for the Dauphine; from the 1690s he is James II's principal painter in exile, painting the King, the Queen, the young Prince of Wales and their faithful aristocrats, while still the most sought-after French portraitist of Louis XIV's court. The two courts are not a contradiction; they are the same job twice -- the flattering likeness, the portrait historie, the rank made legible -- performed for a king who has everything and a king who has lost everything, in the same years, by the same hand. And then the closing irony of the chain: he ends up running the institution Vouet never joined. Named Director of the Academie on 7 July 1708, remaining until 4 July 1711, preceded by Jean Jouvenet and succeeded by Corneille Van Cleve, the Academie creating a class of former directors to honour him. Vouet taught Loir; Loir taught de Troy; de Troy directed the Academy that excluded Vouet. The mechanism is institutional: the portraitist who never painted a ceiling, never went to Rome, never held the post of Premier peintre du Roi -- Mignard held that, and de Troy never did -- ends up holding the chair that governs French painting, because the portrait trade, practised for fifty years at two courts, is the institution by then. The painter who served two kings governs the painters.

**Takeaway:** serve two kings at once -- the same flattering likeness for the king with a throne and the king with no kingdom, the same hand at Versailles and at Saint-Germain -- and let the portrait trade become the institution: the man who never painted a ceiling and never went to Rome ends up directing the Academy his master's master never joined.

## The aesthetic logic

De Troy's aesthetic is colour over line, flattery over monument, and the group over the single figure -- in that order. The construction is Flemish: Van Dyck and Rembrandt by way of Roger de Piles, "dynamic compositions, an encompassing clair-obscur, warm coloring, fluid application of impasto, a preference for delicate glazes and a free touch" (Sotheby's, following Breme), against the stiff effigies of the early reign of Louis XIV (Beaubrun, Nocret, Elle). The flattery is the system: eyes like Guido, noses like Van Dyck, mouths like Correggio (Dandre Bardon), the ability to make any woman look beautiful "without altering their features", adding "graces and nobility" to them (Malliot) -- the likeness is the contract and the paint is the compliment. The group is the destination: the single sitter of 1690 (Mouton with his lute, Talbot in armour) becomes the couple in mythological guise (Venus and Paris, 1691), becomes the mother with children (Brionne, 1697; La Ferte-Senneterre, 1710; von Cosel, c. 1712-1715), becomes the fifty-figure feast (Dido and Aeneas, 1704) and the conversational interior (Astronomy Lesson, c. 1705-1710), becomes the civic group (Ex-Voto, 1725). He works by drawing (black chalk heightened with white, the trois crayons abandoned for pierre noire alone), by the stand-in (the pose solved on a substitute body, the identity laid over), and by the workshop (Belle as the production pair, the versions multiplying, the original undetermined). The through-line is the likeness as a social fact: a portrait is not a picture of a person, it is a person's place in a room, a court, a family, a kingdom -- and when the kingdom is lost, as at Saint-Germain, the portrait is the kingdom, carried across the Channel in a Jacobite's luggage. The reputation curve runs with it: the most sought-after French portraitist of his time, "the only true rival in France of Hyacinthe Rigaud and Nicolas de Largilliere" (Sotheby's 2012 -- auction-house characterisation, see C1485), Director of the Academie 1708-11, then the long eclipse by his own son -- Jean-Francois, the history painter, the inventor of the tableaux de modes, the Director of the French Academy in Rome -- whose works now circulate under his father's name on commercial sites, and whose father must be rescued from him, one misattribution at a time. The study's duty is to keep the two de Troys apart: the father who painted the likeness, and the son who painted the life.

## The network

**Teachers:** his father (name disputed, see C1435/C1428/C1477/C1478), who taught him the rudiments in Toulouse; Antoine Durand, "perhaps also" a teacher (conjectural, see C1452); Nicolas-Pierre Loir (study #586), under whom he studied portrait painting first, and whose sister-in-law Jeanne Cotelle he married; Claude Lefebvre (1632/33-1675), under whom he studied second, whose death in 1675 is the hinge of his career -- he changed direction to portraiture to capture Lefebvre's former clients. **The intellectual patron:** Roger de Piles (1635-1709), friend from the 1670s, "who introduced him to Dutch and Flemish painting" -- the conduit for the Van Dyck and Rembrandt models, and the theorist of colour against Le Brun's line. **No Italian trip:** unlike Vouet, Le Brun, Mignard and La Fosse, no source records de Troy travelling to Italy (see C1447); his classicism was learned at second hand, from Lefebvre, Loir, de Piles and prints. **The family workshop:** brother Jean de Troy (1638-1691), painter and drawing teacher, who stayed provincial and founded an academy at Montpellier; wife Jeanne Cotelle (married 1668/69), daughter of Jean Cotelle the elder, sister of Jean Cotelle the younger, sister-in-law of Loir; brother-in-law Jean Cotelle the younger (1642 or 1646-1708), Lefebvre's pupil, painter of the Versailles garden views; son Jean-Francois de Troy (1679-1752), his pupil, received at the Academie 28 July 1708, Director of the French Academy in Rome 1738-1751, the Rococo turn of the dynasty. **Pupils and the line forward:** Alexis Simon Belle (1674-1734), his best student, who worked with him at Saint-Germain 1698-1701, painted his portrait c. 1700/1703, and became principal painter to the Jacobite court after James II's death in 1701 -- the workshop line runs de Troy to Belle to Aved (Jacques-Andre-Joseph-Camelot Aved, 1702-1766, Belle's pupil); Andre Bouys (c. 1656-1740), confirmed pupil, whose reception portraits of 1688/89 are of Charles de La Fosse and Etienne le Hongre; John Closterman (1660-1711), Wikipedia list only (see C1480). **Patrons:** Louis XIV (chose him for the Dauphine, 1679-80); Madame de Montespan and the Maine line (the Duc and Duchesse du Maine, Sceaux from 1700); Bossuet (the three sons of the Grand Dauphin, 1696, as a gift for the Grand Duke of Tuscany); the city of Paris (1682, 1709, 1716, twice in 1725); James II and Mary of Modena at Saint-Germain (from the 1690s); Nils Bielke, Swedish ambassador (1679, the first important commission -- the doorway to the #590 top pick); Elisabeth Charlotte, Duchess of Orleans (1680, with a Moorish page; again 1694). **Academie colleagues:** Jean Jouvenet (predecessor as Director), Corneille Van Cleve (successor), Charles de La Fosse (Director 1699-1702; the NiceArtGallery succession error, see C1475), Antoine Coysevox (successor to La Fosse). **Rivals:** Hyacinthe Rigaud (1659-1743) and Nicolas de Largilliere (1656-1746) -- Sotheby's pairs the three as the Golden Age of French court portraiture, but both phrasings are auction-house copy, not scholarship (see C1485); all three painted Elisabeth Charlotte (Mignard 1675/78, de Troy 1680/94, Rigaud 1713). Pierre Mignard (1612-1695), the elder rival, Vouet's pupil, Premier peintre du Roi after Le Brun -- the top court post de Troy never held; de Troy held the Academie directorship Mignard never held. Charles Le Brun: no evidenced personal link to de Troy found this run (dead 1690, before de Troy's directorship); the hold stands on its own terms. **No documented contact** in the sources read between de Troy and the Orleans Regent Philippe II (son of the duchess he painted), or between de Troy and Jouvenet beyond the directorship succession; do not invent either.

**Adjacent artists (the map):** Hyacinthe Rigaud (1659-1743) -- the rival who outlived them all, whose state portrait of Louis XIV (1701) is the image de Troy's flattering court manner is measured against; Pierre Mignard (1612-1695) -- Vouet's pupil, the elder portraitist whose sitters de Troy inherited, Elisabeth Charlotte passing from Mignard (1675/78) to de Troy (1680/94); Alexis Simon Belle (1674-1734) -- not an adjacent but the continuation, the best pupil who took the Saint-Germain court over when the master could not meet the demand, painted the master's portrait, and became principal painter to the Jacobite court after 1701.

**Top pick for #590 is David Klocker Ehrenstrahl (23 September 1628, Hamburg -- 23 October 1698, Stockholm).** Held as the next top-pick contender after de Troy (per the #588 brief and leg 3). The chain is real and evidenced: de Troy's first important commission, in 1679, was the portrait of the Swedish ambassador Nils Bielke; Ehrenstrahl was the Swedish court painter in Stockholm -- the man whose court Bielke served. Follow the ambassador home. Ehrenstrahl is the Hamburger who arrived in Stockholm in 1652 at Carl Gustaf Wrangel's invitation, studied in Italy 1654-61 (Venice, Rome, via Florence, Marseille, Paris and London on the way back), became court painter (hovkonterfejare) to Queen Dowager Hedvig Eleonora in 1661, was ennobled in 1674 taking the name Ehrenstrahl, became court intendant in 1690, and painted the great ceiling allegory The Great Deeds of the Swedish Kings in the Riddarhuset (House of Knights) 1670-75, repeated at Drottningholm 1695-97. Pupils Mikael Dahl, David von Krafft (his nephew and successor as court painter) and his daughter Anna Maria. He wrote about his own work in Die vornehmste Schildereyen (1694). The "father of Swedish painting" -- the court portraitist as institution-builder, the northern mirror of de Troy.
