# Helen Karpeles Kennedy: Study Notes

**Site:** Helen May Karpeles Kennedy, b. Thursday 17 May 1888, 33 Lancaster Gate, Bayswater, London, England; d. Wednesday 24 March 1976, Suffolk, England, age 87. English folk dancer; founding Honorary Secretary of the English Folk Dance Society (1911); member of Cecil Sharp's women's dance team from 1910; the dancer who taught the revival its steps five hours a day and then spent sixty-two years as the wife of the man who ran it -- the founder who receded from the record the moment the institution she helped found began to keep one. The exact place of death is not established in any source read (Suffolk is established; Waldringfield was the retirement home, not a stated death place), and the cause of death is not established in any source read; no obituary for her was located. [C34889]

## 1. Lancaster Gate, Hamilton House, Canning Town: the unpromising start

Helen May Karpeles was born on Thursday 17 May 1888 at 33 Lancaster Gate, Bayswater, London, the fourth daughter of Joseph Nicolaus Karpeles, a Hamburg-born tea merchant and stockbroker who had become a naturalised British subject in 1881, and his wife Emily, nee Raphael, the London-born daughter of a foreign banker. Both parents were of Jewish ancestry but non-practising. Her older sisters were Lucy (b. 1882), Florence (b. 1884) and Maud (b. 1885); a brother, Arthur, was born in 1895, soon after which the family moved to 87 Westbourne Terrace, Paddington, where they stayed until Emily's death in 1914. Helen followed her sisters to boarding school at Hamilton House, Tunbridge Wells, Kent, and probably left school in 1907. By then Maud had begun volunteer work with the Invalid Children's Aid Association at the Mansfield House Settlement in Canning Town, and in due course Helen joined her: visiting disabled children at home in the East Ham and Barking areas, and running a weekly Guild of Play meeting at which the children sang action songs and made up dances, Maud at the piano. The Guild of Play, founded by Bermondsey Settlement workers under Grace Kimmins, aimed to restore "the art of wholesome play" to children in extreme poverty. Nothing in this beginning predicts a folk dancer. It predicts a volunteer -- and the whole of Helen's career keeps that shape: unpaid work, done with other people, for children and for the public, with the dancing as the means rather than the end.

**Takeaway:** She enters the story as a settlement volunteer, not as an artist. Every later phase keeps the volunteer terms: unpaid, collective, done by showing rather than by writing. [C34893]

## 2. May 1909: Stratford, Sharp, Neal, and the instant conversion

In May 1909 Maud and Helen attended the Shakespeare Festival at Stratford-upon-Avon and happened upon folk song and folk dance competitions. Thinking the dances might inspire their children's settlement work, they watched the whole programme and met Cecil Sharp, one of the judges. The other judge was Mary Neal. The sisters were, in the phrase every account repeats, instantly entranced. The afternoon matters for three reasons, and the study should keep all three in view. First, it is a conversion dated to a single afternoon: spectator in the morning, adherent by the evening. Second, it is a succession as much as a discovery: the woman Helen was about to replace in Sharp's work -- Neal, whose Esperance girls had been dancing Kimber's Headington morris in London since Christmas 1905 -- was standing beside Sharp, adjudicating, at the founding moment. Third, it is a watching, not a performance: the sisters watched the whole programme. The record is careful on this point and the study must be too -- Stratford 1909 was not a team performance, because no team existed yet. What they carried home was not a repertoire but a conviction: that these dances, learned properly, could be taught onward, to children first and then to anyone.

**Takeaway:** One afternoon, two judges, one conversion. The founding moment of Helen's career contains its whole structure: she succeeds Neal, she learns from Sharp, and she learns by watching first. [C34894]

## 3. September 1909: the Polytechnic evening classes

When Sharp opened his School of Morris Dancing at the South Western Polytechnic at Chelsea in September 1909, Maud and Helen enrolled in the evening classes he also ran there, open to the public. The Chelsea school matters because it was Sharp's machine for making dancers and teachers at scale: the classes provided, in Schofield's phrase, "a corps of dancers and teachers who became the foundation of the English Folk Dance Society", and the corps included Helen Kennedy (Douglas's sister), Marjory Sinclair, Maud and Helen Karpeles, Peggy Walsh and Maggie Muller. Helen's learning here was embodied and collective: she learned the steps in her own body, in the evening, alongside strangers, in a physical-training department run by Dorette Wilkie, where the gym tunic -- loose, high-waisted, flat shoes -- freed the legs in a way ankle-length dresses never had. The school also supplied the other half of her education: the method. Sharp's was analytical -- every step learnt exactly as recorded from the traditional dancers, from a handbook, taught by qualified teachers, so the dance could be reproduced accurately anywhere. Neal's was intuitive -- watch the traditional performers, copy them, absorb rhythm and style as a whole. Helen learned the Neal way (by watching at Stratford, by attending classes) and then worked inside the Sharp system for the rest of her active career. She lived the split the revival argued about.

**Takeaway:** The Polytechnic is where the spectator becomes a practitioner. The step is learnt exactly, by the body, in a room full of other learners -- and the tunic, not the theory, is what lets the legs move. [C34895]

## 4. The Folk Dance Club: Vim on the parquet

The sisters so enjoyed the classes that in the autumn of 1909 they started an informal Folk Dance Club at their home in Westbourne Terrace. Maud's unpublished autobiography (VWML MK/7/185, p. 20) describes the routine: the girls removed all rugs and furniture from the drawing room and ruined the highly polished parquet floor with "a plentiful supply of vim" -- a scouring powder, presumably to give the floor grip -- "without protest from their long-suffering parents". The detail is the mechanism. A domestic interior was dismantled weekly and rebuilt as a practice floor: furniture to the walls, a floor appearing, dancers filling the cleared space, and at the end the room reassembling. The club was the sisters' own teaching vehicle, and it is the most Helen-specific thing in her whole career: not Sharp's school, not the Society, but a room in her parents' house that she and Maud turned into a school. It produced the women's folk dance team of 1910-1911, illustrated Sharp's lecture-demonstrations, gave the public performances, and -- through Helen Kennedy, Douglas's sister, already a member -- brought Douglas Kennedy into Helen's life. It is also where the scale of the thing is visible: from a drawing room to a 500-person public performance in eighteen months, and from a drawing room to a national society in two years.

**Takeaway:** The club is a room that empties itself. Grip matters -- the floor must hold the step -- and the whole revival, at its start, fits in a drawing room. [C34896]

## 5. Bampton, Whit Monday 1910: the watching trip

On Whit Monday, 16 May 1910, Maud and Helen went with Sharp to watch the Bampton morris dancers, and four days later he wrote to Maud (letter MK/3/1, 19 May 1910) that he was keen to recruit both sisters to his teaching team -- "to rope you and your sister into my staff of teachers". The trip's significance is the letter, and the letter's significance is that Sharp was recruiting teachers, not performers. The sisters were being hired -- unpaid, as everything in this story is unpaid -- to carry the dances onward: to demonstrate them, to teach them, to try out the new ones he was recording. The study must not call Bampton a performance. It was a watching trip, like Stratford, and the pattern is now unmistakable: Helen learns by watching, and then she is asked to teach what she has watched. The Bampton side itself stays a group in the record -- no individual Bampton dancer is named in any source read in connection with Helen -- and the tie is to the side as a watched body, not to any person.

**Takeaway:** Watch, then teach. The recruitment letter is the hinge of her career: from pupil to staff in four days, on the strength of having watched well. [C34897]

## 6. Queen's Hall, 31 May 1910: grace, skill and discipline

A few weeks after Bampton, on 31 May 1910, members of the South Western Polytechnic performed at a concert at the Queen's (Small) Hall, where their "grace, skill and discipline provoked universal admiration" (*Daily Telegraph and Courier*, 1 June 1910). The quotation is a group review, not a personal review of Helen, and the study must not present it as one -- the performance was by the Polytechnic's students, a group that included the Karpeles sisters among others. But the date matters: within nine months of first seeing a folk dance, Helen is performing one in public, in a London concert hall, to a newspaper's praise. The women's team that emerged from the club "performed at several public events in 1910 and 1911", and the Queen's Hall night is the first of them on the record. The speed is the point. Sharp's system could take a spectator in May 1909 and put her on a concert stage in May 1910, because the system was built for exactly that: exact steps, learnt by the body, demonstrated in public, taught onward the same week.

**Takeaway:** Nine months from spectator to concert hall. The praise belongs to the group -- and that is the point: the system, not the soloist, is what performs. [C34898]

## 7. Five hours a day: the teaching staff

By 1910-1911 Maud and Helen "were soon teaching Morris, sword, and country dancing classes five hours a day as members of his teaching staff" -- the sentence is joint, about the sisters together, and the split of hours between them is not recorded, so this study cites it as joint, not as Helen's alone. Five hours a day is the measure of the peak: not a hobby, not a weekend pastime, but a working day spent teaching other people's bodies to do what hers had learnt to do. The teaching was by demonstration: the sisters danced, the learners copied, and the dance moved from one body to the next. They also tried out the new dances Sharp was recording, including the sword dances from Grenoside and Flamborough in August and December 1910 -- the Chelsea dancers "provided a group of willing volunteers to try out the complexities of these dances, and assist Sharp with a new notation style". The first long-sword display by Sharp's dancers was in December 1910, the short-sword debut in February 1911. Helen's body, in these years, is Sharp's laboratory: the place where a collected dance is tested to see whether it can be taught.

**Takeaway:** Five hours a day, by showing. The teacher is the textbook: the dance is transmitted body to body, and the trying-out is part of the teaching. [C34899]

## 8. Portman Rooms, 3 April 1911: 500 people, 60 pounds

On Monday 3 April 1911 the Folk Dance Club gave a successful public performance at the Portman Rooms, Baker Street, to raise funds for the Invalid Children's Aid Association. The *Westminster Gazette* had noticed it on 29 March; Wikipedia adds that the event raised 60 pounds, that the contralto Mattie Kay sang, and that Sharp lectured. The audience was more than 500 -- a planned performance for friends had turned into a show for a hall full of strangers, such was the interest in the dance form. The night gathers the whole early career into one room: the club performing, Sharp lecturing and accompanying on the piano, a professional singer on the same bill, and the money going to the settlement children whose need had started the whole thing in 1907. The charitable purpose never leaves the story. The Portman Rooms night is still a fundraiser for the Invalid Children's Aid Association, four years after the sisters first visited disabled children in Canning Town. The dance has grown from a teaching aid for those children into a public art -- and the proceeds still go back to them.

**Takeaway:** The drawing room becomes a hall of 500, and the money still goes to the children. Scale changes; the purpose does not. [C34900]

## 9. The EFDS founding, 6 December 1911: a society, not a club

On Wednesday 6 December 1911, at a meeting at St Andrew's Hall, Newman Street, London, Cecil Sharp moved that "a Society, to be called The English Folk Dance Society, be established, having its headquarters in London". The motion was carried and a committee appointed: Maud Karpeles, her sister Helen, Ralph Vaughan Williams, Alice Gomme (Lady Gomme), Sir Archibald Flower, Hercy Denman, Charlotte Sidgwick, Perceval Lucas, George Wilkinson, and Cecil Sharp as Honorary Director. Peggy Walsh was Secretary; Captain William Kettlewell was Honorary Treasurer; Lady Mary Trefusis accepted the presidency in 1912. The Folk Dance Club dissolved to make way for the Society, and Helen was elected its first Honorary Secretary. The shift from club to society is the shift from a room to an institution: a committee, a president, a treasurer, a journal (Lucas edited the first two), a prescribed repertoire, grade levels, examinations. Helen's own move in the same shift is from teacher to institution-builder: she now holds the Society's pen, not just its steps. She is still unpaid, still working as one half of a sister pair, and still dancing -- the secretary is also on the demonstration team. The phase is short, under three years, because marriage ends it.

**Takeaway:** The club becomes a society with a committee and a journal, and Helen becomes its first secretary. The dancer takes up the pen -- and keeps dancing. [C34901]

## 10. First Honorary Secretary: the pen, not the steps

Helen held the Honorary Secretaryship from the founding until Maud took it over from her -- Maud's tenure is dated 1921 in Cecil Sharp's People and 1922-1930 in Wikipedia, so Helen's end date is c. 1921/22 by inference only, and this study states no year as fact. What the role entailed day to day is not recorded in any source read: no minutes, no correspondence, no description of her desk. What is recorded is the machine she was helping to run. From 1912 Sharp offered Elementary and Advanced Folk Dance Certificates: candidates had to dance in *any position* in any of six country dances, six Morris set dances, a Morris jig, and any position of a longsword dance -- "any position" meaning the examiner could place you anywhere in the set and you had to know the dance from there. Holding a certificate was explicitly *not* a test of teaching capacity; a separate list of "approved" teachers was kept at the EFDS office. Helen ran the paperwork of this machine -- membership, correspondence, the office that held the lists. The confusion to avoid is the Trefusis one: the EFDSS Wikipedia article calls Lady Mary Trefusis "the first secretary of the society", but Knevett's committee list names Helen as Honorary Secretary and Peggy Walsh as Secretary, with Trefusis as President. Use President for Trefusis.

**Takeaway:** She ran the office that held the lists. The certificate machine demanded any-position knowledge -- know the dance from every slot, not just your own -- and the secretary was the person who kept the machine's records. [C34902]

## 11. The demonstration team: the dance as lecture illustration

Sharp's teaching depended on a demonstration team: a small trained side that performed the dances live while he lectured, so the audience saw the thing being described at the same moment it was described. The Karpeles sisters' Folk Dance Club "gave demonstrations to illustrate Sharp's public lectures"; the EFDS demonstration team of 1911 included Helen May Karpeles, A. Claud Wright, Lily Roberts, and Douglas Kennedy, the youngest member at 17. Helen was a member of Sharp's women's dance team from 1910, performing at public events in 1910-1911; the men's morris side made its first appearance only in February 1912 (Wilkinson, Lucas, Butterworth, Tiddy, Wright, Paterson, Douglas Kennedy), and the filmed "Rose" and the Kinora Morris solo were the men's team and Butterworth respectively -- not Helen. She is evidenced in the Kinora *country-dance* spools, dancing with Sharp, Butterworth and Maud. The team is a teaching technology: the dance is the diagram. A piece can stage the demonstrator/audience split -- six dancers inside a ring, a ring of watchers outside it, the watchers gradually joining in -- because that is literally how the revival taught: the same dance repeated so the audience could learn it.

**Takeaway:** The dance is the diagram. A small set performs while the lecturer talks, and the audience learns by watching the thing itself. [C34903]

## 12. The Kinora films, 1912: six reels, five dances, one minute

Probably in 1912 -- all sources hedge the date, and Heaney's Kelmscott reasoning (the only date on which the Demonstration Team is known to have been present and dancing in the Oxford area in 1912 was 20 June, when a meeting was organised at Kelmscott) is a likelihood, not a certainty -- the EFDS Demonstration Team was filmed on a Kinora, a hand-cranked "what-the-butler-saw" home-movie machine: about 640 tiny black-and-white photographs arranged radially on a spool, each held back momentarily by a ratchet as the spool turns, the film lasting about a minute at roughly ten and two-thirds frames per second at a hand-cranked, uneven speed. Seven spools were made by Gilman of Oxford (933-939): 933 Butterworth dancing (side view), 934 Maud Karpeles dancing, 935 Maud Karpeles and Helen Kennedy dancing, 936 missing, 937 Sharp, Butterworth, Karpeles and Kennedy dancing a country dance, 938 Butterworth (the Bodleian's film, MS Eng. misc. e. 664), 939 Maud Karpeles dancing. Six survive; the five dances on them are one country dance ('Hey Boys Up Go We', Sharp's interpretation of a seventeenth-century social dance), three solo morris jigs, and one double jig. They are the earliest known film of English folk dance and the only known moving pictures of Sharp. They open the BFI's 2012 two-DVD set *Here's a Health to the Barley Mow*. The films were also filmed twice more, and failed twice: in August 1912 the side was filmed dancing "The Rose" in Stratford-upon-Avon High Street for Pathe's Animated Gazette, and "the pictures of 'The Rose' were unfortunately spoilt by the acceleration being too great"; a second attempt in Paris in June 1913 "was a failure". Those were the men's side; Helen does not appear in them.

**Takeaway:** A one-minute loop, a fixed camera, a repeating figure -- and the loop is imperfect, hand-cranked, wobbling, with dancers occasionally colliding and laughing it off. The dance survives because someone cranked a handle. [C34904]

## 13. 'Lumps of Plum Pudding': the sister dance on film

Of the five dances on the Kinora reels, one belongs to Helen and Maud together: 'Lumps of Plum Pudding', a double jig danced by the two sisters on spool 935, "film 3" in the Cecil Sharp's People account, viewable in the compilation at the link the Helen profile gives. It is a Sherborne/Bledington jig; its tune was given to Sharp by Mason with the jingle "Lumps of plum pudding and pieces of pie / My mummy gave me for telling a lie". The other solos on the reels are identified in Folktrax as 'Princess Royal' and 'Jockey to the Fair'. The double jig is the form this study keeps returning to, because it is the form of the sisters' whole career: two dancers, one dance, each carrying half of it. The jig is also the solo turn in the Morris tradition -- one dancer performing the stepping and capers alone, often dancing near and across a pair of clay pipes laid crosswise on the floor, a tiny stage the feet must not touch -- and the double jig is that turn done by two. In the film the sisters dance together in a medium that lasts a minute and repeats: the earliest moving image of English folk dance is, among other things, a film of two sisters dancing together.

**Takeaway:** Two dancers, one dance, each carrying half of it. The double jig is the sisters' career in miniature -- and it is the oldest moving image of the tradition. [C34905]

## 14. Paris, June 1913: ten minutes before going on

At a folk festival in Paris in June 1913, Douglas Kennedy and Helen Karpeles became engaged "in the 10-minute interlude before going on stage". The sentence, from the Pinewoods Camp history, is the whole romance in one line: an engagement fitted into the gap between two performances, by two people whose courtship had been conducted inside a demonstration team. Douglas had come into Helen's life through the club -- his sister Helen was already a member, he was invited through her, and he performed with the club at Kensington Town Hall on 1 December 1911, joining the EFDS at its inaugural meeting a week later. He was 17 then, the youngest member of the 1911 team; he performed with the new men's demonstration team at the Suffolk Street Galleries on 27 February 1912, not yet 19. The Paris engagement is also the last fully documented moment of Helen's first career. After it: marriage, war, children, and a record that goes quiet for decades. The ten-minute interlude is worth lingering on, because it is the hinge between the two halves of her life -- the dancer engaged in the wings, the wife stepping out of the frame.

**Takeaway:** Engaged in a ten-minute gap between performances. The courtship happened inside the team, and the engagement is the hinge between her two lives. [C34906]

## 15. September 1914: the wedding and the disputed day

Helen married Douglas Neil Kennedy in Kensington in September 1914, soon after he had enlisted in the London Scottish Regiment (14th Battalion, London Regiment). He was 21, she was 25 -- or 26, if her birth date of 17 May 1888 is right, which would make her 26 on 29 September 1914; the profile's "25" is inconsistent with its own birth date, and the inconsistency is carried here, not smoothed. The exact day is disputed between the two Cecil Sharp's People profiles: the Helen profile says 29 September 1914, twice; the Douglas profile says 28 September 1914, once. This study prints 29 September with the split flagged. What is NOT part of this wedding, and must be struck from the record that the #802 study built, is the detail that Maud was maid of honour and Sharp walked Helen down the aisle. That passage belongs to Lily Roberts Conant's wedding -- 15 December 1917 at Helen Storrow's home in Boston, where "Maud Karpeles was her maid of honor. Cecil Sharp walked her down the aisle" (Pinewoods Camp history, read in full by the coordinator). No source supports it for Helen, and this study does not use it. The correction matters beyond accuracy: the #802 study's doorway tie for Helen rested in part on a wedding detail that was never hers, and the tie must stand on the facts that are hers -- the Stratford meeting, the classes, the club, the team, the Society, the films -- which it does, amply.

**Takeaway:** 29 September, with 28 September carried as the split. And the aisle detail is Lily's, not Helen's -- a correction the record owes her, because her real wedding needs no borrowed poetry. [C34907]

## 16. The war years: silence in the record

What Helen did during the war years 1914-1918 is not recorded in the sources consulted. Douglas served with the 14th Battalion London Scottish, then the Royal Defence Corps, reaching captain and being awarded the MBE. Maud followed Sharp to America in the summer of 1915 and spent 1916-1918 collecting with him in the Appalachians. Sharp himself sailed for the USA on RMS Lusitania in December 1914. Helen stayed in England, married, and disappears from the record for four years. The silence is a finding, not a gap to fill: the record of the revival in these years is written through the men who went to war and the woman who went to America, and the woman who stayed home and married is not written about at all. The study does not invent a wartime Helen -- no teaching, no nursing, no committee work -- because no source gives her one. The one dated wartime fact that touches her is second-hand: in the summer of 1915 Sharp cabled England from Maine for another teacher for the EFDS summer school, and Douglas gave his wife Helen credit for recommending Lily Roberts -- the recommendation that sent Lily to America, to Helen Storrow, to Pinewoods, and to the life that would make her the camp's owner. Helen's one documented wartime act is a recommendation made on someone else's behalf.

**Takeaway:** Four years, no record. The silence is the finding: the revival's history is written through the people who travelled, and Helen stayed home. Her one wartime act is recommending someone else. [C34908]

## 17. Two sons: John and Peter

Helen and Douglas had two sons. The elder, John N. Kennedy, was born in the April quarter of 1917; he served as a Sub-Lieutenant, RNVR, and was lost with HMS Glowworm on Monday 8 April 1940, when the destroyer was sunk by the German cruiser Admiral Hipper off Norway and all but 31 of her company were lost. The Helen profile calls him "David"; the Douglas profile calls him John; the naval casualty list gives "KENNEDY, John N, Sub Lieutenant, RNVR, MPK", and John is correct -- the "David" is most likely a slip from Douglas's grandfather, the Scottish singer David Kennedy (b. 1825). The younger son, Peter Douglas Kennedy, was born on Saturday 18 November 1922 in London and died on Saturday 10 June 2006, aged 83: folklorist and folk-song collector, EFDSS staff from 1948, BBC *As I Roved Out* presenter in the 1950s, editor with Alan Lomax and Shirley Collins of *Folk Songs of Britain*, founder of the Folktrax label, EFDSS Gold Badge 2005, his archive at the British Library. ## 20. Square Dances of America, 1939: the late import

In 1937, while visiting the United States to direct a Pinewoods Camp summer school, Douglas Kennedy began collecting American square dances; he introduced them to England at a summer school in 1938, and in 1939 he and Helen published *Square Dances of America* together. The collecting trip was Douglas's; the book is joint; the credit must be split exactly that way. The square dance is a different machine from the longways set Helen had danced since 1909: a square of four couples, eight dancers, with a caller directing the set -- the set is a box, the caller is a voice that issues figures, and the couples respond. The Kennedys then taught and performed the dances with the Square Dance Band, and the book codified them -- calls, music and instructions -- for English dancers. It is Helen's one evidenced publication that imports a new dance form, and it arrives nearly thirty years after she helped found the Society that would publish it: the founder, late in life, co-authoring the book that brings a foreign form home.

**Takeaway:** A square of four couples, a caller, a box instead of a corridor. Thirty years after founding the Society, she co-authors the book that imports a new dance into it. [C34912]

## 21. The bands: Square Dance Band and Folk Dance Band

In 1939 Helen and Douglas, with Nan and Brian Fleming-Williams, formed the Square Dance Band (drum, guitar, fiddle, concertina), which "set the standard and pattern for all subsequent folk-dance band playing". The band was the performing and teaching vehicle for the American square dances Douglas had collected in 1937 and introduced in England in 1938. After Peter Kennedy joined the EFDSS staff in 1948, the Society pursued a "folk dancing for all" policy, introducing American-type folk dances with a caller along with dance bands in the style of the London-based Folk Dance Band, whose members included Helen and Douglas Kennedy, with Peter Kennedy on drums. The bands are the third form of her career, after the team and the books: the dancer becomes a musician's collaborator, playing in the ensemble that keeps the dancers' time. She is recorded, in 1961, playing English concertina and three-hole pipe and tabor -- the traditional morris musician's instruments -- which closes the circle: the woman who learned the dances by watching the musician now plays the musician's part herself. No source shows her teaching folk dance in Scotland or in Suffolk; Suffolk appears only as retirement boating at Waldringfield.

**Takeaway:** From dancer to band member: the woman who learnt by watching the musician ends up playing the musician's instruments. The band is the third form of the career. [C34913]

## 28. The two Helen Kennedys: the name trap

When Douglas married Helen Karpeles in 1914 there were two Helen Kennedys: his wife, and his older sister Helen Dorothy Kennedy (b. Friday 5 July 1889, Edinburgh; d. Monday 13 October 1975, Pembury Hospital, age 86), a skilled Highland dancer who had enrolled at Chelsea Physical Training School in September 1907, begun learning morris with Sharp in early 1909, taught physical training at Tunbridge Wells, worked in France during the war assisting Daisy Daking's rehabilitation work, was appointed occasional inspector of folk dancing in elementary schools in 1920, and married Stanley North in December 1920 -- after which she was, retrospectively in the literature, "Helen Kennedy-North". The study's rule is simple: where a source says only "Helen Kennedy", do not assign it to Helen Karpeles Kennedy without a second anchor. The trap is also the point: the revival's record confuses its women with each other, and the confusion is itself evidence of how thinly they were recorded.

**Takeaway:** Two Helen Kennedys, 1914-1920, and the record confuses them. Where a source says only "Helen Kennedy", do not assign it -- the confusion is itself the evidence of how thinly these women were recorded. [C34920]

## 29. The set of six and the common figures: a rectangular machine

Most Cotswold Morris dances are danced by a set of six dancers in a rectangle -- two parallel lines of three, facing each other across the set, plus a musician and often a Fool -- and the dance is built as an alternation: a block of stepping in place or travelling, then a *figure* (a traced floor pattern), then stepping again, then the next figure, until the dance ends. The step is the engine: the double-step (right-left-right-right, left-right-left-left, a skipping, hopping step) and the single-step (two hops on each foot in turn) never stop while the dance runs; Sharp's system is step-first, learn the step exactly, then the figures ride on top of it. The figures are a small shared vocabulary: *foot up* (advance and retire), the *hey* (a figure-of-eight weave), *whole rounds* (the whole set circling), *cross-over* (lines passing through each other), *back-to-back*, *galleys* and *capers* (turning jumps that end a figure). Each dance is a fixed sequence of these figures -- "The Rose", for example, runs opening, half-hey, whole-hey, half-hey, whole-hey, half-hey, whole-hey, with different capers on each repeat. In handkerchief dances each dancer holds a white handkerchief in each hand and the arm movements trace patterns in the air, turning a small gesture into a large white trace; in stick dances each dancer carries a short wooden stick and strikes it against a partner's stick on set beats, a clash that is both rhythm and figure. Helen taught from this repertoire on Sharp's staff; she did not create it, and no source names a particular Morris dance as danced by her personally. The dances listed in the 1912 certificate requirements are the repertoire she taught and was examined in as a system, not a record of her performances.

**Takeaway:** Six bodies, a rectangle, a fixed pulse, a small alphabet of figures. The set is a closed machine that dissolves into a pattern and re-forms -- and the handkerchief makes the air itself the drawing. [C34921]

## 30. Progression, the hey, and the longways corridor

An English country dance is danced in a *longways* set: couples in two parallel lines, men on one side facing their partners on the other, the end nearest the music called the *top* (or Presence) and the other end the *bottom*. Most longways dances are *progressive*: the same sequence of movements (the Complete Figure) is repeated over and over, but each repetition leaves the dancers in changed positions -- the active couple moves one place down the set, the couple below moves up, and the figure repeats with new partners. The dance has no end built in; it repeats "for an indefinite number of times" until the active couple reaches the bottom and waits, or the music stops. Partners are temporary: you dance the figure with one couple, then the next, then the next. The *hey* is the weave inside this corridor: dancers move in single file along a set track, passing one another on alternating shoulders -- right, left, right, left -- tracing a figure of eight, with no hands given, the weave done by timing and by reading the other dancer's shoulder. The same mechanism appears in Morris (the whole hey of the six-dancer set) and in country dance, which is why Sharp's dancers could move between the two families. Three small partner figures recur across Sharp's country dances: *siding* (face your partner throughout, four steps forward passing by the left shoulder and four back by the right, without holding hands), *arming* (link right arms at the elbow and go once around each other, then fall back; repeat with the left arm), and *casting* (turn outward and dance up or down *outside* the set, leaving the corridor, travelling around its edge, re-entering one place on). Each is a different way of relating to one other body: pass without touching, link and orbit, leave and return.

**Takeaway:** The corridor is the stage; progression is the conveyor; the hey is the braid. The same figure repeats with different people in it -- sameness of path, change of partner -- and that is the whole dance. [C34922]

## 31. The longsword ring and the rose: a chain that must not break

In the northern longsword dance, six (Kirkby Malzeard) or eight (Flamborough) dancers each hold a long, rigid sword by the hilt, the point resting on the next dancer's shoulder or held in the next dancer's hand, so the swords form a continuous ring. The figures are performed with the ring intact: the dancers walk round, clash swords, and at the climax interlock the blades into a *lock* -- and then display it as a *rose*, a hexagonal star of crossed swords held up for the audience. The dance is a piece of engineering: a flexible ring that must never break, ending in a rigid geometric display. Helen taught sword dancing on Sharp's staff -- the Chelsea dancers tried out the Grenoside and Flamborough sword dances for him in August and December 1910, and the first long-sword display by Sharp's dancers was in December 1910 -- but she did not collect the dances; Sharp's *Sword Dances of Northern England* (1911-1913) is his collecting. The 1912 certificate required any position of either the Kirkby Malzeard longsword dance (for six dancers) or the Flamborough Sword Dance (for eight). The rose is the payoff a generative piece wants: six or eight straight lines snapping into a hexagon, a chain that stays connected while it moves and then freezes into a star.

**Takeaway:** A ring of linked bodies that deforms without ever breaking, then locks into a star. The rose is the freeze-frame the whole dance is built to earn. [C34923]

## 32. The Kinora loop: a one-minute dance

The Kinora is not just the record of Helen's dancing; it is a form. A fixed camera, a short loop, a repeating figure -- and the loop is imperfect: hand-cranked speed wobbles, frames jerk, the dancer occasionally blurs out of focus or stumbles against a partner (Sharp and Butterworth collide in one spool and share a chuckle). The film lasts about a minute -- roughly ten and two-thirds frames per second at a hand-cranked, uneven speed -- and shows short sequences of country dancing together: Sharp, Butterworth, Maud Karpeles and Helen Kennedy. A piece can adopt the Kinora as its form: a one-minute loop, a fixed frame, a repeated figure, with a slight hand-cranked unevenness in the tempo and an occasional stumble that the dancers laugh off and recover from. The medium is the message: the dance survives because someone cranked a handle. And the Kinora is also a warning about the record: the Pathe filming of "The Rose" in Stratford High Street in August 1912 was "spoilt by the acceleration being too great" and no trace survives, and the Paris filming attempt of June 1913 "was a failure". What survives of Helen dancing is what a hand-cranked toy camera happened to catch, on a day that is itself only "probably" 20 June 1912 at Kelmscott.

**Takeaway:** The loop is the form: fixed frame, one minute, a repeated figure, a wobbling tempo, a stumble laughed off. What survives of her dancing is what a toy camera happened to catch. [C34924]

## 33. The certification machine: any position

The EFDS, founded 6 December 1911, was not just a club but a standardising institution: a prescribed repertoire, grade levels, and examinations. From 1912 Sharp offered Elementary and Advanced Folk Dance Certificates, and the requirement that matters most for a generative piece is the *any-position* rule: candidates had to dance in any position in any of six country dances, six Morris set dances, a Morris jig, and any position of a longsword dance -- the examiner could place you anywhere in the set and you had to know the dance from there. Holding a certificate was explicitly *not* a test of teaching capacity; a separate list of "approved" teachers was kept at the EFDS office. Helen was the Society's first Honorary Secretary (1911; Maud took the role over in 1922), which is to say she ran the paperwork of this machine -- membership, correspondence, the office that held the lists. The mechanism is interchangeable parts: dancers who must know every position, not just their own, so that the set holds whatever slot is filled. A piece can stage it literally: six agents, and at a signal one is dropped into a new slot and must pick up the figure from there without breaking the set; or a grading ritual, the same dance danced three times with the dancers permuted, the set scored on whether it holds.

**Takeaway:** Know the dance from every slot, not just your own. The set holds because its parts are interchangeable -- and the secretary kept the list of who was approved. [C34925]

## 34. Imitation versus notation: two ways to learn a dance

The revival split over how a dance is transmitted, and Helen lived on both sides of the split. Mary Neal's "intuitive method": traditional performers demonstrate, learners watch and copy, absorbing rhythm, style, and fluency as a whole. Sharp's "analytical method": every step is learnt exactly as recorded from the traditional dancers, from a handbook (*The Morris Book*, *The Country Dance Book*), taught by qualified teachers, so the dance can be reproduced accurately anywhere. Helen learned the Neal way -- she and Maud learned by watching the Stratford competitions and by attending Sharp's evening classes -- and then worked inside Sharp's system, as a teacher on his staff demonstrating dances to illustrate his lectures. The two modes give a generative piece its cleanest mechanism: *copy-the-demonstrator* (one agent dances, the others follow a beat behind, errors creeping in and being corrected) versus *read-the-book* (agents snap to exact notated positions, no drift) -- or a piece that starts as imitation and tightens into notation, the dance getting more exact as the system "learns" it. The split is also the story of her succession: Neal's method was the one Sharp rejected, and Helen is the person who carried Sharp's method forward while having learnt by Neal's.

**Takeaway:** Two transmissions: copy the demonstrator, or read the book. She learnt one way and taught the other -- and a piece can start loose and tighten into notation as it learns. [C34926]

## 35. The living-room school: the room that empties itself

The Karpeles sisters' Folk Dance Club met in their parents' house at 87 Westbourne Terrace, London, and the routine, from Maud's autobiography, is the most concrete image in Helen's whole career: the girls would remove all rugs and furniture from the drawing room and ruin the highly polished parquet floor with "a plentiful supply of vim", without protest from their long-suffering parents. A domestic interior was dismantled weekly and rebuilt as a practice floor. The club grew from this room into a 500-person public performance at the Portman Rooms on 3 April 1911 (raising 60 pounds for the Invalid Children's Aid Association), and by December 1911 it had dissolved into the EFDS. The Vim detail gives a tactile rule: grip matters; the floor must hold the step. A piece can stage the room itself: a stage that *is* a room -- walls, a rug, a piano -- that the dance gradually clears, furniture sliding to the walls, a floor appearing, dancers filling the cleared space, and at the end the room reassembling. The living-room school is the cleanest Helen-specific spine in the whole study, because it is the one mechanism that belongs to her and Maud and to nobody else: not Sharp's school, not the Society, but a room in her parents' house that two sisters turned into a school.

**Takeaway:** The room empties itself, the floor appears, the dance fills it, the room reassembles. The whole revival, at its start, fits in a drawing room -- and grip matters. [C34927]

## Aesthetic logic: the system underneath

Helen Karpeles Kennedy's aesthetic is not in anything she made, because she made almost nothing that survives under her own name. It is in what she *did*, and what she did was always the same thing in different rooms: take a dance that exists somewhere else -- in a village, in a book, in a musician's hands -- and move it, intact, into the bodies of the people in front of her. The logic has four parts. First, transmission is the work: the dance is not composed, it is carried, body to body, and the carrier's skill is exactness -- the step learnt exactly, the figure repeated exactly, the set holding its shape whatever slot you are dropped into. Second, the room is the instrument: settlement hall, drawing room, concert hall, committee room, family home, boat -- what changes across her life is the room, and the dance adapts to each one without changing its steps. Third, everything is done with someone else: first a sister, then a husband, never alone -- the double jig, the joint book, the band, the marriage are all the same form, two people carrying one thing. Fourth, the record is the enemy: the revival wrote its history through collectors and directors and authors, and Helen was none of those, so the record thins exactly where her life thickens -- after marriage, after 1922, in the war years, in the quarrel. The aesthetic logic of a piece built from her, then, is a system that transmits exactly, in a room that keeps changing, always in pairs, with the record of it always slightly out of reach -- a dance that is learnt by watching, taught by showing, and filmed, if at all, by a hand-cranked toy that wobbles.

**Takeaway:** Transmission is the work; the room is the instrument; everything is done in pairs; the record is always slightly out of reach. A piece from her should transmit exactly, in a changing room, two at a time, on a wobbling loop. [C34928]

He is also the only adjacent figure who is Maud's antagonist -- the director her sister fought as secretary -- which makes him the load-bearing figure of the family triangle, and the doorway into the directorship half of Helen's life: the half that is not the sister at all, but the household that ran the Society for thirty-seven years.
