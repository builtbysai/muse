# Francois Morellet: Study Notes

**Site:** Francois Morellet, born 30 April 1926 at Cholet, France, died 10 May 2016 at Cholet, aged 90 -- French abstract painter, sculptor and light artist, the man who let chance decide inside a system he had already fixed: squares and triangles permuted by a stated rule, forty thousand squares coloured by the odd and even numbers of a telephone directory, pi deciding the angle of ten neon lines, white neon flashing at four interfering rhythms, a perfect sphere built from nothing but straight steel rods, whole building facades covered in a red grid on blue, and, late in life, a neon loop that slumped across the floor and gave up. He called himself the monstrous child of Mondrian and Picabia, said his systems were the producers of his works and he was not responsible for whether they were brilliant or ugly, and co-founded GRAV, the group that took Vasarely's idea that the sole artist was outdated and turned it into a collective programme with labyrinths, street actions and games for the spectator (C6001, C6003, C6004, C6104).

**Doorway:** Hop from the Vasarely study: from the elder who programmed the grid to the younger man who answered him in 1957 and let chance decide inside it -- Francois Morellet, GRAV co-founder beside Le Parc, whose neon and programmed paintings turn Vasarely's system into a game of rules.

## The life: from the toy factory to the neon that gave up

Francois Charles Alexis Albert Morellet was born on 30 April 1926 in Cholet, France, and died on 10 May 2016 at his home in Cholet, ten days after his 90th birthday (C6001, C6015). He lived and worked in Cholet for the entirety of his life (C6002, C6006, C6013). The family business was the Morellet-Guerineau TOY factory, not paint -- a correction to the brief, on every source consulted; Morellet returned to Cholet in 1948 and ran it until 1975, Levy Gorvy alone saying 1976 (C6002, C6006, C6013, C6017). The factory years matter: the business gave him financial independence and taught him production techniques that fed directly into his art, and his industrial life 350 km from Paris is the reason he gives for co-founding GRAV -- he needed a permanent audience and a group to exhibit with rather than remaining alone (C6003, C6014). His father loved artists, "even more than art itself, as his collection was not brilliant" (C6003).

He began making still-life paintings at 14 while studying Russian literature in Paris, took lessons at Jean-Denis Maillart's studio in 1941, and describes himself as an autodidact; "self-taught" is the standing description in every gallery and museum biography (C6001, C6003, C6007, C6008, C6013, C6014). He began painting in 1946 in a semi-figurative style; his first abstract drawings date from 1948, and in 1949, influenced by the tribal arts at the Musee de l'Homme, he made paintings close to abstraction -- but "it was only in 1950 that I started doing abstract geometrical paintings" (C6003, C6009). His first solo exhibition was at Galerie Creuze, Paris, in 1950 (C6007, C6008). In 1950 he visited Brazil, where he discovered Concrete art and the work of Max Bill through Almir Mavignier in Rio de Janeiro, when he contemplated moving there with his wife; Mavignier showed him reproductions of Bill's work in a journal featuring a retrospective at Sao Paulo's Museum of Modern Art. "Through his work, I learned the rules of concrete art, dictated in 1932 in Van Doesburg's manifesto, which converted me" (C6003). In 1952 he discovered Muslim linear art on the walls of the Alhambra in Granada, "a shock, which would influence my work on the line and my love for the 'all-over'" (C6003). From 1953, repetitive patterns and networks of superimposed "trames" incited the eye to imagine the continuation of lines and shapes beyond the edge of the painting; he stopped framing his works in 1952 (C6003).

The 1957 exchange with Vasarely is the doorway of this study and is told in full in the D1 verdict above: Vasarely wrote proposing to "program" babies through the toy factory, slipping abstract geometric images into their prams; Morellet refused on 2 July 1957, calling the factory "a milieu perfectly hostile to the arts (even to Renoir)", while agreeing that "the painter creator and interpreter is of another era" (C6005). Vasarely was also one of the first to buy Morellet's works and tried to convince the influential Denise Rene to represent him in Paris (C6005). The Molnars -- above all Francois Molnar -- were the first to make Morellet understand the importance of writing; their collaboration began in 1958, when Francois Molnar wrote the essay for Morellet's first solo show after his turn to geometric abstraction, at Galerie Colette Allendy, 6-24 May 1958 (C6005). Degruson counts more than 636 texts written by Morellet over 59 years, and an oeuvre of more than 5,800 works; he was still answering a student's email interview on 26 April 2016, fifteen days before his death (C6005).

In 1960 Morellet co-founded the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel with, among others, the Molnars. The Centre's members at creation were Hugo Demarco, Hector Garcia Miranda, Horacio Garcia Rossi, Julio Le Parc, Vera and Francois Molnar, Francois Morellet, Sergio Moyano, Servanes, Francisco Sobrino, Joel Stein and Yvaral (C6005). The Molnars moved away from the project in 1961, leaving six members: Horacio Garcia Rossi, Julio Le Parc, Francois Morellet, Francisco Sobrino, Joel Stein and Yvaral (C6005). The group then became GRAV, Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel (C6016). How the group came together, in Morellet's own account: "The three Argentine artists, Horacio Garcia Rossi, Julio Le Parc and Francisco Sobrino, first visited Vasarely, who pointed them to Francois and Vera Molnar, then to me and to Vasarely's son Yvaral. I contacted Joel Stein -- an old friend and accomplice since 1949 -- so he could also join the group" (C6003). Vasarely is the godfather of the group, never a member: neither 1961 GRAV text carries his name, while his son Yvaral was a co-founder (C6019). GRAV "picked up on Victor Vasarely's concept that the sole artist was outdated" (C6004). Before GRAV, in April 1960, Piero Manzoni invited Morellet and several collaborators to the "Motus" exhibition at his Galleria Azimuth in Milan, where the works were shown without the artists' individual names indicated, at Morellet's instigation (C6014, C6016).

GRAV's main aim was to merge the members' individual identities into a collective and individually anonymous activity, linked to scientific and technological disciplines and based around collective events called Labyrinths (C6004). Its 1961 texts: "Propositions sur le mouvement" and, for the Paris Biennial, the flyer "Assez de mystifications" (Enough Mystifications); "Propositions generales", signed by Garcia Rossi, Le Parc, Morellet, Sobrino, Stein, Yvaral (C6010). The manifesto targets, per Degruson: the unique and isolated artist; the cult of personality; the myth of creation; overestimated aesthetic or anti-aesthetic conceptions; elaboration for the elite; the production of unique works (C6005). Its programme: to confront the public with artwork outside the museum, intervening in urban spaces with subversive games, politically charged flyers and playful questionnaires, turning spectators into participants (C6011). The 1963 manifesto states it plainly: "We want to free the viewers from this apathetic dependence that makes him passively accept, not only what one imposes on him as art, but a whole system of life... We want to make him participate. We want to place him in a situation that he triggers and transforms" (C6004). The first Labyrinth, at the third Paris Biennale in 1963, invited viewers to walk through twenty environmental experiences -- wall reliefs, light installations, mobile bridges -- meant to invoke different viewer reactions; GRAV defined types of audience interaction from "perception as it is today" through "visual activation" and "active involuntary participation" to "active spectatorship" (C6004). Morellet's own contributions: the first room entirely covered with wallpaper reproducing his 1960 "Repartition aleatoire de 40.000 carres"; room three holding a 100 x 100 cm work of 64 white bulbs with four lighting rhythms; the last room holding his first neon work, four 80 x 80 cm panels with four white neon lights facing each other, "the alternated lighting was very bright, quite unbearable for the visitors"; a large 240 cm diameter sphere outside the labyrinth on the great stairs of the Musee de la Ville de Paris (C6003). On 19 April 1966 GRAV staged "Une Journee dans la rue" (A Day in the Street) in Paris: gifts at Chatelet Metro at 8 am, changeable structures on the Champs-Elysees at 10 am, habitable kinetic objects at midday, a giant kaleidoscope at 2 pm, moveable paving slabs at Montparnasse at 6 pm (C6004). The Situationist International criticised GRAV's installations for requiring the viewer to fulfil pre-existing options devised by the artists; Joel Stein later acknowledged that audience interaction with GRAV installations "can become a sort of entertainment, a spectacle in which the public is one of the elements in the work" (C6004). GRAV dissolved by agreement in November 1968, on the recognition that it was impossible to maintain the rigour of a joint programme (C6004).

Morellet began working with neon at the same time as GRAV's founding, 1960-1961, and neon became a central material from 1963 (C6001, C6002). His reasons, in his own words: "With my friends from GRAV, we wanted to end the conventional artistic practice of canvases, paintbrushes and easels. In my quest for new artistic materials, neon had all the qualities I liked: it was perfectly linear; the lighting allowed rhythms of brutal flashing; it had an industrial origin; its use in public advertising was very vulgar at the time, which I liked; and there was a neon producer in Cholet, who was very efficient and enthusiastic" (C6003). In 1966 he wrote of light sources as "plastic material": "Only habit and traditions have prevented the direct sources of artificial light (bulbs, neon tubes) from taking up their rightful place in the artistic canon" (C6002). His neon works of 1963 were "provocative, vulgar and unsellable. I had to wait 20 years to sell my first one! Today, they are stylish, expensive and very trendy" (C6003). He met Dan Flavin at the opening of "Kunst Licht Kunst" in 1966 at the Stedelijk Van Abbemuseum, Eindhoven; Flavin's long work with green fluorescent tubes was "fabulous" (C6003).

His abstract ideal was tempered by the charming nihilism of Marcel Duchamp; for him a work of art referred only to itself (C6001). His titles are generally sophisticated, show Duchampian word play, and describe the "constraints" or "rules" he used to create the work (C6001). Like John Cage in music and the Oulipo group in literature, he used rules and constraints established in advance to guide the creation of his works while allowing chance a role in some compositions; this dialectic between rigid rule-based procedure and chance situates his work within post-conceptual art (C6001). His own formulation: "There is nothing aesthetic about my interest in systems or mathematics. The use of systems allows me to develop works whose compositions are dictated by the 'rules of the game,' independently from my taste, and without final choice from my side. My systems fix the rules of the game and mathematics execute the composition and give the final visual form. In fact, I always try to do the least amount possible!" (C6003). And: "Because I always wanted to distance myself from my works. Since the 1950s I've tried to eliminate all trace of sensibility in the making and reduce as much as possible the number of subjective decisions in the conception of my works. My systems, associated by chance, are the producers of my works, and if one finds them brilliant or ugly, I am not responsible" (C6003). His own classification of his systems, published in 1974: Juxtaposition, Superposition, Hasard (Chance), Interference and Fragmentation -- an exercise in demystifying and rationalising his work, which he said, pursued further, would have resembled Borges' animal classification (C6003). He called himself the "monstrous child of Mondrian and Picabia" (C6014).

The chance turn came in the late 1950s through the "Duo-Collages" of Jean Arp and Sophie Taeuber-Arp, discovered through his friend Ellsworth Kelly, which led him to introduce chance as a central principle, creating works based on random numbers found in his local phone directory or the infinite decimals of pi (C6002). From 1968 Morellet became interested in architecture and public spaces (C6007); commissions named in the sources include the Centre Culturel in Compiegne, a section of La Defense in Paris, and the Kroller-Muller Museum Park in Otterlo (C6007, C6017). In 1992 he summed up his work in "Relache no. 1", combining all the materials he had ever used: painted canvas, neon tubing, adhesive tape and strips of metal (C6017).

Exhibition spine: Documenta, Kassel, 1964 (with GRAV), 1968 and 1977; The Responsive Eye, MoMA, New York, 1965; Venice Biennale 1970 and 1990 (C6002, C6013). First major solo museum exhibition: Stedelijk Van Abbemuseum, Eindhoven, 1971, touring Europe, with the works displayed at each venue in different orientations -- horizontal, vertical or upside down (C6002). North American retrospective 1984-85, touring the Albright-Knox Art Gallery (Buffalo), Musee d'art contemporain (Montreal), Brooklyn Museum and Center for the Fine Arts (Miami) (C6002, C6013). Retrospectives: Nationalgalerie, Berlin, 1977; Centre Pompidou, 1986 and 2011; Galerie nationale du Jeu de Paume, Paris, 2000-2001; Musee des Beaux-Arts, Nantes, 2007 (C6002, C6013, C6018). The 2011 Centre Pompidou show, "Reinstallations", resituated his installations from 1963 to the present as a cohesive oeuvre, beginning with his contributions to GRAV's two most important labyrinths (C6012). Dia Art Foundation presented a major survey in 2017, the first in the US in more than three decades (C6013). More than 130 solo exhibitions in his career -- an estate/gallery figure, not an independently counted total -- and 11 solo shows in 2016 alone, his 90th-birthday year (C6003, C6009, C6017). He received the Peter C. Ruppert Prize for Concrete Art in Europe in 2008 (C6006). The Louvre commission: in 2010 he redesigned the bay windows and oculi of the Lefuel staircase (W20); he became the second living artist to create a permanent installation at the Louvre, though other sources phrase it as one of only three contemporary artists with a permanent installation there -- both phrasings circulate (C6008, C6013, C6006). The Centre for International Light Art (CILA) in Unna, Germany, staged a retrospective of his light art, 24 September 2016 - 29 January 2017, the last exhibition curated by the artist himself shortly before his death (C6001). His work is held by the Centre Pompidou, Dia Art Foundation, MoMA, LACMA, Tate, the Seoul Museum of Art, the Kunsthaus Zurich, the Nationalgalerie Berlin and others (C6002, C6013).

## M1 -- The rule in the title: one stated rule is the whole picture

Carre inscrit dans un carre (W03, 1954) is the mechanism in its purest form: a white square panel, oil on wood, 80 x 80 cm, carrying two thin black outlines only -- an upright square inscribed inside a diamond, a square turned 45 deg, its points touching the panel edges, nothing else on the panel (C6055). The viewed image shows exactly that: the rule is the picture, and the picture is the rule carried out (C6055). Carres et triangles rouges et bleus (W02, 1953) is the same module in two flat colours: a square blue ground carrying one large red diamond made of a central red square with a red triangle on each of its four sides, points touching the panel edges (C6055). The 80 x 80 cm oil-on-wood format recurs through the whole 1950s group (C6053, C6055). 16 carres 96 triangles (W01, 1952) is the serial version: the alternation of black and white triangles and squares obeys a rigorous serialization (C6069, prose only). Repartition de 16 formes identiques (W04, 1957) is the permutation version: 16 identical L-forms slotted together on an 80 x 80 cm panel, some versions following a legible symmetrical puzzle logic, others looking disorganised and prefiguring the chance works of 1958 (C6053, prose only). The titles do the describing: they state the constraint, and the painting is the constraint executed.

**Takeaway:** write the rule in the title and carry it out, nothing more. One rule, one panel, no motif, no shading, no decision left to taste.

## M2 -- Trames: the field with no motif

4 doubles trames, traits minces 0-22.5-45-67.5 (W06, 1958) is the Trames mechanism at its purest: four double grids of thin lines at 0, 22.5, 45 and 67.5 deg, "a work consisting of no more than several sets of parallel black lines intersecting on a white ground, generating no motif" (C6057, prose only). From 1953, repetitive patterns and networks of superimposed "trames" incited the eye to imagine the continuation of lines and shapes beyond the edge of the painting (C6003). The supplementary image of "36 trames de tirets pivotees au centre" (1960, oil on wood, 80 x 80 cm, viewed 1000x1007) shows the mechanism at maximum density: a dense black-on-white field of pivoting dash-grids forming concentric rings around a small central circle (C6055). The same device scales down to tape: "4 trames 30, 60, 120, 150 partant des 4 angles du mur" (1977, reactivated 2021, matt adhesive tape 5 cm wide, 356.2 x 891.5 cm, viewed 1000x750) -- black tape lines crossing a white gallery wall at four angles, a visitor standing at left for scale, the Trames as architecture (C6055). The Alhambra arabesques he first saw in 1952 are the named origin of the line networks (C6051, C6053).

**Takeaway:** lay sets of parallel lines over each other at fixed angles and refuse to draw anything else. The field has no motif, no centre, no edge -- and the eye keeps finding diamonds and stars that were never drawn.

## M3 -- Chance from a found number source: the phone book decides

Repartition aleatoire de triangles suivant les chiffres pairs et impairs d'un annuaire de telephone (W05, 1958) is the first official chance work: triangles distributed by the odd and even numbers of a telephone directory (C6053, C6057, prose only). The 1958 picture "marked the beginning of his mature period" and was followed throughout his career by works informed by "games of logic and chance" (C6057, C6063). Repartition aleatoire de 40 000 carres (W08, 1960) is the landmark: a grid of forty thousand squares, marked square by square from the top left to the bottom right; in Morellet's own account via Tate, he drew lines to make 40,000 squares, then his wife or sons read numbers from the phone book aloud, he marked each square for an even number and skipped the odd ones, then painted the marked squares blue and the blank ones red (C6052, C6064). The image viewed is the 1961 version of the same system in grey and black (1200x1499), not the 1960 MoMA red/blue painting: a square field of tiny grey and black squares in apparently random distribution, reading at a distance as television static -- no motif, no centre, no edge hierarchy (C6055, C6068). The date is 1960 by MoMA, 1961 by DAN Galeria and the Mayor Gallery; 1960 is used as the MoMA collection date (C6052, C6068, C6069). A wallpaper version of the same system lined a room in GRAV's labyrinth at the 1963 Paris Biennale (C6065, C6060). The influence named is not Cage but the Duo-Collages of Sophie Taeuber-Arp and Jean Arp, made "according to the laws of chance", which Morellet knew through Ellsworth Kelly (C6057).

**Takeaway:** fix the grid, then let a found number source fill it. The phone book is not a metaphor -- it is the random-number generator, read aloud square by square, and the static it makes is the picture.

## M4 -- Programmed chance: pi decides the lines

6 repartitions aleatoires de 4 carres noirs et blancs d'apres les chiffres pairs et impairs du nombre pi (W07, 1958) is where pi enters the work: six chance distributions of four black-and-white squares, the placements decided by the odd and even digits of pi (C6060, prose only). Pi stays for the rest of the career -- the late "Declinaisons de pi" (Versions of pi) series dates from 1998, and the 2016 "5 neons au hasard de pi" works are its last statement (C6051, C6055). 10 lignes au hasard (W15, 1971) is the same rule in lines: ten lines crossing the picture plane, their angles determined by the digits of pi (C6069, C6063, prose only). The supplementary image of "5 neons au hasard de pi n. 1" (2016, white neon tubes and acrylic on canvas, 116 x 118 cm, viewed 1200x1199) shows the rule in its final year: five white neon tubes crossing a black panel at pi-determined angles, ends overshooting the panel edges (C6055). And "pi piquant neonly n. 9 1=6 deg" (2007, blue neon, 196 x 192 cm, viewed 1200x878) shows pi angles as a wall drawing in light: blue neon sticks forming a loose zigzag on a white wall (C6055). "Since 1960, he has also consistently followed the concept of 'programmed chance,' which is always based on a predefined system of order (the picture format and the number, color, and form of elements). This order is then 'filled' with a structure generated by chance" (C6109). The system fixes the rules of the game and mathematics executes the composition; chance fills a predefined order (C6106, C6109).

**Takeaway:** let the digits of pi choose the angles. The rule is fixed and printed in the title; the composition is not the artist's choice, and that is the point.

## M5 -- Neon as line and rhythm: the light machine

Neons 0-45-90-135 avec 4 rythmes interferents (W10, 1963) is the first neon work: four 80 x 80 cm panels, four white neon lights facing each other on the four walls of a room, flashing at four interfering rhythms, shown in the last room of GRAV's 1963 labyrinth (C6003, C6058, C6060, prose only). The related later variant viewed, "Neon 0-90 avec 4 rythmes interferents" (1965, white neon tubes on black painted panel, 80 x 80 cm, viewed 1000x667), shows a black square panel with a white neon grid -- three vertical and three horizontal tubes crossing at right angles, some lit, some dark -- the on/off rhythm frozen in a single photograph (C6055). 16 lampes - allumage avec 4 rythmes superposes (W11, 1963) is the same machine in bulbs: white bulbs on painted wood panel with electromechanical system, 80 x 80 x 15 cm (C6055, C6070). The viewed image shows a black square panel on a white gallery wall carrying 16 white bulbs in a 4 x 4 grid, several lit, several dark, with the electromechanical control box and its wiring exposed on the floor below, cables running up the wall -- the mechanism is on show, not hidden (C6070). The bulbs are "split into four units that light up at regular rhythms but at slightly different speeds" (C6070). The 80 x 80 cm panel size is the same module as the 1950s paintings -- the system keeps its format while the medium changes from paint to light (C6053, C6055). Why neon: "It was perfectly linear; the lighting allowed rhythms of brutal flashing; it had an industrial origin; its use in public advertising was very vulgar at the time, which I liked; and there was a neon producer in Cholet" (C6065). In 1966 he wrote of light sources as "plastic material": "Only habit and traditions have prevented the direct sources of artificial light (bulbs, neon tubes) from taking up their rightful place in the artistic canon" (C6002).

**Takeaway:** neon is a line that can switch off. Put four rhythms in one room and let them interfere -- the pattern is the interference of the clocks, and the flashing is the work.

## M6 -- The sphere made of straight lines

Sphere-trame (W09, 1962) is the first break out of the plane: the same grid logic built in steel rods (C6054). Fabricators welded circular stainless steel grids together, progressively lengthening toward the centre to form a perfect sphere; the sphere hovers above the ground and, as movement activates the grid patterns, "it affects the eye as a shimmer" (C6054). Straight rods in a calculated structure end up forming a curve (C6061). The Detroit version is 203.2 cm overall (C6054); the Sainsbury Centre version is 45 x 45 x 45 cm, made as a multiple in an edition of 200 by Galerie Denise Rene, editions in varying sizes made between 1962 and 1966 and "now his best known works" (C6067). The viewed images confirm the same object twice: the Detroit sphere hanging in a gallery (1813x1996), a huge lattice ball of steel rods with its grid shadow thrown on the parquet floor, and an outdoor version at Kleiner Werth, Wuppertal (1280x853), a steel grid sphere standing among trees and shrubs (C6054, C6061). The date flag: DIA dates its example "between 1962 and 1989" (design 1962, fabrication later); Sainsbury dates the work 1962; 1962 is used as the design date (C6054, C6067, C6051). Grille se deformant (W13, 1965-69) is the kinetic cousin: three metal grids on a stand, raised, driven by motors so the grids deform against each other -- a kinetic object, not a picture, the grid itself moving (C6068, prose only; the 1963 date in one gallery listing is unverified and not used).

**Takeaway:** build the curve out of straight rods and let the grid shimmer. The sphere is never drawn or modelled -- it is a cubic grid clipped to a round envelope, and it breathes as you walk past.

## M7 -- The spectator put to work: the building is the canvas

Reflets dans l'eau deformes par le spectateur (W12, 1964) is the participation mechanism in one object: a web of neon lamps whose light is reflected on a water tank, and the spectator manipulates a lever to disturb the reflection (C6069, C6060, prose only). The same show logic appears in "Mona Lisa Distorted" (1964), where an electric fan agitates a reproduction of La Joconde on a cotton sheet, and "RED" (1964), where a button detonates an electronic flash behind the word ROUGE (C6060). The viewer is a participant, not a spectator -- the GRAV programme in miniature (C6004). Trames 30-87-93-183 (W16, 1971) is the mechanism at facade scale: painted on the surrounding walls of the Plateau La Reynie, Paris, near the site where the Centre Pompidou was to be built, red lines on a blue background (C6057). The viewed image (600x441, thumbnail-size) shows two Paris building facades entirely covered in a red grid on a blue ground -- intersecting sets of parallel lines at the titled angles, wrapping corners and window openings without respecting them. The painting treats the building as a flat picture plane; the architecture is the canvas (C6057). Desintegrations architecturales (W17, series from 1971) is the same confrontation as a programme: grids, lines and disintegrations laid over facades, walls and interiors so the building's own geometry is interrupted and re-read. "My 140 Architectural Disintegrations follows the same principle, a confrontation between my systems and architecture" (C6062). More than 100 such works stand in public space in France (C6056). The 1963 labyrinth already staged the idea at room scale: twenty environmental experiences, with GRAV defining types of interaction from "perception as it is today" to "active spectatorship" (C6004).

**Takeaway:** give the spectator a lever and the building becomes the picture plane. The work is not finished until someone disturbs it -- and the grid must interrupt the architecture, not decorate it.

## M8 -- The system sags and jokes

Lamentable n. 2 (W19, 2005) is the late turn in one object: white neon, 400 x 192 x 186 cm (C6055). The viewed image (1000x1500) shows a single tall, thin white neon loop in a bare white room, hung from the ceiling and slumped across the floor -- the top half a narrow vertical loop, the bottom half collapsed into a flat triangle on the concrete. It reads as a neon line that has given up: the "lamentable" joke is visible, not just titled (C6055, C6059). A second source calls the work "Lamentable", 2006, "a large, flexible neon tube bent into an irregular, seemingly haphazard shape" (C6059); the date is 2005 for n. 2 (GalleriesNow) against 2006 in general accounts, both recorded (C6055, C6059). No End Neon (W18, 2001/2002, also shown as "NoendneoN", a palindrome title) is the same loosening in another direction: slender argon tubes intersecting a gallery wall at slanted angles, "governed by a continuous system, and in the mind's eye it is easy to conceive of the work extending, indefinitely, into space" (C6058, prose only). The subtitle alludes to Piet Mondrian's "Pier and Ocean" group (C6058). Neons avec programmation aleatoire-poetique-geometrique (W14, 1967) is the joke as a by-product: three squares crossed by diagonals, neon tubes in groups with shifted on/off rhythms, among whose shapes the letters N-O-N, N-U-L, C-O-N, C-U-L may form. "As I like swearwords and provocation, this was an opportunity to associate my rough poetry with my rigorous geometry to make people smile" (C6003, prose only). The supplementary image of "Relache n. 2 blanc" (1992, acrylic and oil on canvas, lacquered aluminium, neon tubes, canvas strips, 340 x 290 cm, viewed 1000x1333) shows the same loosening: white panels and frames tipped askew, a pale neon line crossing them (C6055). Colour returns in the Acrobatie arcs: "Acrobatie n. 1" (2010, blue argon tubes and red neon tubes, 268 x 279 cm, viewed 1000x1333), two red arcs and blue straight tubes arranged like abstract limbs on a white wall (C6055, C6070). By the mid-1980s, in counterbalance to the strict dogma associated with minimalism and abstraction, he began using wordplay in his titles rather than prescriptive mathematical formulas (C6002).

**Takeaway:** let the system sag. After fifty years of obedience, the grid slumps, runs off the wall, and spells swearwords by accident of the program -- and the joke is generated by the system, never pasted on.

## The aesthetic logic: the least amount possible, and the joke at the end

Morellet's logic runs in one direction for sixty years, and it is the opposite of the expressive gesture: from the hand-made picture toward a system that makes the picture, and from the panel toward the room, the street and the building. It starts with a rule stated in the title and carried out on an 80 x 80 cm panel (M1), moves to line sets that refuse to make a motif (M2), to chance admitted inside the rule -- a phone book read aloud, pi deciding the angles (M3, M4) -- to neon replacing paint and time entering through flashing rhythms (M5), to a sphere built from straight rods (M6), to the spectator given a lever and the building treated as a canvas (M7), to the system at last sagging, slumping and joking (M8). At every step something is taken away -- the outline, the motif, the artist's taste, the hand, the unique work, the upright panel -- and something is handed over: first to the rule, then to chance, then to the machine, then to the spectator, then to gravity. "My systems fix the rules of the game and mathematics execute the composition and give the final visual form. In fact, I always try to do the least amount possible!" is the theory; "My systems, associated by chance, are the producers of my works, and if one finds them brilliant or ugly, I am not responsible" is the ethic; the Louvre windows (W20) are the destination -- the strictest system installed in the most decorated building in France, playing the 19th-century grillwork against its own skewed copy (C6003, C6062).

Against Vasarely the contrast is exact and is the doorway of this study: Vasarely's motor is a patented alphabet of coloured units permuted by assistants into endless serial pictures, the pattern moving although nothing moves; Morellet's motor is a rule fixed in advance with chance admitted inside it, the composition executed by mathematics and the picture made by a system the artist refuses to own. Vasarely dematerialised the picture into a system you complete by walking past; Morellet dematerialised the artist into a rule you can read in the title. The two men met in the middle through GRAV: Vasarely pointed the Argentines to Morellet and to his son Yvaral, GRAV picked up Vasarely's concept that the sole artist was outdated, and the group turned the elder's programme -- art for all, in the street -- into labyrinths, street actions and games where the spectator triggers and transforms the work (C6004, C6006). The 1957 letter is the hinge and the correction of this study: the elder wrote to the younger proposing to program babies through a toy factory, and the younger answered that the factory was "a milieu perfectly hostile to the arts (even to Renoir)" -- he would take the programme, not the prams (C6005). Against Le Parc and Soto the contrast is one of temperament inside the same group: Le Parc's light and Soto's wires make spaces you walk through, while Morellet's neon and grids make rules you watch execute -- the player of the game beside the builders of the room. Morellet met Soto in 1956 and became close (C6107), and the two were paired in a joint exhibition, "Francois Morellet / Jesus Rafael Soto -- Optical and Movement", at Espace Louis Vuitton Beijing in 2023 (C6107). The social programme never left either side of it: the 1963 labyrinth was for walkers, the 1966 Day in the Street was for passers-by, the Architectural Disintegrations were for the buildings of France, and the Louvre windows were for everyone climbing the stairs (C6004, C6056, C6062).
