# Ken Hinckley: Study Notes

**Site:** Ken Hinckley is Senior Principal Research Manager at Microsoft Research, where he leads the EPIC group -- Extended Perception, Interaction, and Cognition -- at Microsoft Research, Redmond. He has worked at Microsoft Research continuously since 1997, nearly 30 years at the same lab, investigating novel input devices, device form-factors, and modalities of interaction; his CV names sensing techniques, cross-device interaction, and pen computing as the work he is best known for. He takes a B.S. in Computer Science from Worcester Polytechnic Institute with High Honors (1991), an M.Sc. (1993) and a Ph.D. (1996, thesis "Haptic Issues for Virtual Manipulation", advisor Randy Pausch) from the University of Virginia; the doctoral work was done with UVA's Department of Neurosurgery on two-handed spatial interfaces and physical props for neurosurgical planning. He was inducted into the CHI Academy on Sunday 27 April 2014 at CHI 2014 in Toronto; his UIST 2000 paper "Sensing Techniques for Mobile Interaction" won Best Paper and the UIST 2011 Lasting Impact Award, and "Sensing Techniques for Tablet+Stylus Interaction" won Best Paper at UIST 2014. He is also a published short-fiction writer (Nature Futures, including "The Ostracons of Europa", Nature vol. 499, Wednesday 3 July 2013) and a contributing member of the Microsoft Courier team around 2009, whose interaction details remain confidential to Microsoft. His birth date is not established in any source read -- Wikipedia alone says "born 1969", with no day, month, or place, and no primary source read confirms even the year. No university affiliation is stated in any source read. One discrepancy is flagged, not settled: AD Scientific Index lists his affiliation as "Microsoft Research Montreal", while every Microsoft Research page read places him and the EPIC group in Redmond. [C36920]

**Relevance:** Hinckley's work asks what a system can know about a hand before the hand acts -- which of several simultaneous channels (pen tip, pen hover, touch, grip, palm, approach trajectory) carries the command, and what the system infers from posture and approach rather than from a drawn mark. The evidence is "Pre-Touch Sensing for Mobile Interaction" (CHI 2016), in which a self-capacitance screen senses fingers above the display and grip around the edges before contact, and "Pen + Touch = New Tools" (UIST 2010), in which a finger hold changes what the pen means -- together they make the hand's state, not the mark, the input. [C36921]

**Doorway:** The 453rd doorway. Singh ends on the stroke that already knows: when the system supplies the consistency and the hand supplies only the sketch, where does the drawing end and the interpretation begin -- and what does a stroke already know? Hinckley answers the branch before the stroke: what the hand already knows before it draws. His systems read the approach, the grip, the palm, and the division of labour between the two hands, so the interpretation starts before any mark exists -- the hover is already a signal, the grip is already a mode, the hold is already a phrase. The open question carried forward is: if the system can read the hand before it acts, where does the intention live -- in the hand that approaches, in the sensor that watches, or in the rule that decides what the approach meant? [C36922]

## M1 -- The approach is the interface (Pre-Touch Sensing, CHI 2016)

Subject: A mobile screen that senses fingers above the display and grip around its edges before any contact, and reshapes itself in response to the approach. The hardware is a Fogale Sensation self-capacitance touchscreen, a 16x9 sensor matrix, 14-bit capacitance per cell, sampled at 120 Hz; a fingertip is sensed about 35 mm above the screen and a flat palm about 5 cm away; a single frame is processed in about 35 ms. The fingertip extraction pipeline interpolates the raw 16x9 map to 180x320 (Lanczos 4), thresholds out sensor noise, isolates the fingertip from the rest of the hand, finds local maxima with a 6.5x4.6 mm window moved 3.3 mm horizontally and 2.3 mm vertically, combines maxima within 1.5 mm, and picks the highest value inside a 5 mm radius circular mask; maxima at the screen edge are treated as grip, not fingertip; thumb vs. finger is decided from rotated bounding boxes, aspect ratio, and yaw angle. Fingers resting on the back of the device are not detected -- a stated limit. [C36924]

Visual principle: Nearness is a visible state, not a binary event. In the paper's Ad-Lib video player the default state has no visible UI at all -- just the video content -- and when a hand approaches, the interface "pours itself out" around the finger: core playback controls fade in over 100 ms and expand to full size, the full UI fades in over 200 ms, and on withdrawal it fades out over 1.2 s -- "withdraw gracefully, like a good waiter". The presentation depends on grip, approach direction, and finger count: a bimanual index-finger approach brings the full controls, centred, with a vertical volume slider on the side the hand approached from; a one-handed thumb approach brings a reduced control set in a fan-shaped layout near the edge, with timeline and volume morphing into dials; a second thumb brings advanced options sliding in; a pinch-to-zoom posture makes the controls fade out and a gestural guide appear instead. In the Calm Web Browser the overlay is alpha-blended with the thresholded, interpolated finger image itself, so links and controls are revealed in the region under the hand and "feather off" along the finger's contours rather than popping in. The legible image is a surface that breathes -- elements swelling into legibility as a hand nears and dissolving as it withdraws -- with the hand's own shadow as part of the composition. [C36925]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): Three strategies, stated explicitly in the paper. (a) Anticipatory reactions: the interface reacts to the approaching hand before contact, on fixed schedules -- the paper tried proximity-proportional fading and rejected it, because fading controls in proportion to finger distance was less predictable and more distracting than fixed-time animations; comfort and convenience explicitly trump efficiency ("the ad-lib interface is not about efficiency"). (b) Retroactive interpretations: the pre-touch trajectory just before contact disambiguates what the touch means. The exact rule for ballistic vs. fine tap: a fine-adjustment phase is a trajectory at altitude below 10 mm above the screen AND within 15 mm of the touch-down location for 250 ms before contact; a ballistic dive (no fine phase) dispatches the tap to the large target under it, while a fine adjustment dispatches it to the small target within 7.7 mm of the finger-down event -- a 5x5 mm target requires fine adjustment, a 40x40 mm target is acquired by a pure ballistic dive. The same distinction applied to swipe: a ballistic swipe is a flick or scroll, while a swipe preceded by a fine acquisition phase is a text selection, dispensed immediately -- removing the tap-and-hold timeout (typically 1,000 ms) that selection normally requires. (c) Hybrid touch+hover gestures: a thumb holds an icon to select it and a second finger brought into range brings up a context menu (Copy, Delete, Rename, Share) directly under the finger, its opacity proportional to the second finger's altitude; touch down on a command to complete it, lift to cancel; grip sensing detects one-handed use, so the menu falls back to tap-and-hold with the thumb and arcs in a fan shape toward the thumb side. The state variables are finger height, approach velocity, approach direction, grip configuration, and finger count. [C36926]

Artistic opportunity: No gallery piece treats the space above the canvas as an input. The hover field is a third dimension the viewer already inhabits with their hand; making it visible turns the approach itself into the drawing. The piece can show what the hand was about to do before it did it -- anticipation made visible -- and the paper's own design judgment is portable: predictability beats responsiveness, so the interface answers on a schedule, not on the finger's every twitch. [C36927]

**Takeaway:** The finger's approach is a continuous analog signal (height, speed, grip, posture) and the paper deliberately throws most of it away, committing to fixed 100/200 ms animations and fixed locations. The rule to carry is the retroactive one: a touch is classified by the 250 ms that preceded it -- ballistic or fine -- and the classification, not the contact point, decides what the touch means. The interface that answers on a schedule is the honest transfer; a piece that chases the finger would be a worse piece. [C36928]

## M2 -- Pen writes, touch manipulates (Pen + Touch = New Tools, UIST 2010)

Subject: A note-taking and scrapbooking surface on a Microsoft Surface tabletop, prototype "Manual Deskterity", where the pen writes, touch manipulates, and the combination yields new tools. The division of labour is stated as a rule: unimodal pen always leaves ink; unimodal touch moves, zooms, selects; multimodal pen+touch is interpreted as a command, never as ink. One touch-hold integrates three steps that are normally separate: object selection, mode switching of the pen from ink to a contextual command acting on the selected object, and phrasing of the pen and touch inputs into one unitary command (Buxton's chunking and phrasing). What holds the phrase together is the paper's term, the muscular tension of the nonpreferred hand holding the object: "salient kinesthetic feedback" that ties selection, mode, and phrase together with no button, persistent mode, or visible widget. Releasing the object returns the pen to ink, always; the paper states there is "no possibility of getting stuck in a tool mode". [C36929]

Visual principle: The hand's division of labour is the composition. The evidence base is an observational study with a paper notebook: 8 people (6 male, 2 female, age 9-53, all right-handed), pasting and annotating clippings for a hypothetical short film in a 7"x10" notebook, with pens, glue, tape, scissors, and print-outs of 20 web pages; behaviours B0-B9 recorded, including the pen tucked between fingers to interleave writing and moving clippings (B1), the nonpreferred hand holding a clipping while writing about it even when glued down (B3), thumb and index finger framing a clipping to focus attention (B4), and all users forming piles of "interesting" clippings while holding other items in the nonpreferred hand (B7). The picture is a desk of materials with a surround of clippings and tools, and the held object is always the centre of the action. Holding is visible as muscular tension made into a rule: while touch holds an object, the pen's meaning changes -- the same stroke copies, cuts, or staples depending on what is held. [C36930]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): A modal state machine with the held object as context. The tools, each a rule: Stapler -- finger-tap-select multiple items, then hold an item and tap it with the pen; the tapped item moves to the top of the stack and represents the whole pile (test users rated it one of their favourites). X-acto knife -- hold the object, pen stroke starts outside the object, crosses through its interior on any path, and finishes outside; on exit, cut and scrap piece both appear (a user asked "How would it know which half I want?" -- both halves remain). Tearing -- hold with one finger, cross with another; tears along the line connecting entry and exit; test users did not find tearing photos particularly useful, and the paper's lesson is that users do not expect analogous pen+touch and touch+touch gestures to do the same thing. Carbon copy -- hold an item with a finger, "peel off" a copy with the pen; once the pen drags away by a minimum distance (value not stated in the paper), a semi-transparent copy appears attached to the pen and becomes opaque as dragging continues, annotations copied too. Ruler -- hold an object with thumb and index finger; an animation increases the object's transparency and adds a dotted line around its border, and the pen stroke is then constrained to the object's border; the object becomes "mostly transparent" at test users' suggestion. Brushing -- hold a photo and stroke the pen on the canvas; the stroke uses the photo as a brush, each subsequent stroke while still holding mapping to a common viewport within the photo. Tape curve -- while drawing a stroke, holding a finger on the stroke turns the remaining portion into a tape curve (straight lines and variable-radius curves in one tool); lifting the finger reverts immediately to freeform ink. Bezel menu -- a semi-transparent strip along the top edge; novice mode taps an icon to create an object, expert mode drags through the item onto the canvas to create and place it in a single transaction. Page flipping -- swipe across the top-right corner for next page, top-left for previous; drag downward from the corners to scrub through pages; a page curl in the corners is the affordance. The implementation is a custom infrared LED pen, activated during contact via a tip switch, "much brighter than hand/finger contacts, so it is trivial to identify as the brightest spot in the image"; touches with a large contact area are treated as incidental, and the paper states "Our approach does not solve it". There is no formal evaluation: the paper says so explicitly ("we do not present a detailed evaluation in this manuscript"); feedback came from 11 test users, informally. [C36931]

Artistic opportunity: The gallery's interactions are almost all single-pointer. A two-handed piece -- one pointer that holds and phrases, one that acts -- makes the viewer's own hands the subject. The mechanism is the division of labour itself, and it is visible in every gesture: the hold is a decision the viewer can see themselves making. The paper's grammar is the transfer, not any single tool: hold = select + mode + phrase, release = return to ink, and every tool is one sentence in that grammar, differing only in what the pen does while the hold lasts. [C36932]

**Takeaway:** The hold is a phrase, and the phrase is the whole system -- selection, mode, and timing in one muscular act, with no widget anywhere. The piece to build is not a stapler or a knife; it is the grammar, with the four verbs (copy, cut, staple, brush) as its vocabulary. The rejected variant is the warning: pen dragging selected items was tried and abandoned because users frequently intended to write on a selected item but dragged it by mistake -- the hold must precede the act, or the grammar collapses. [C36933]

## M3 -- Speed is zoom (SDAZ, UIST 2000)

Subject: Browsing a large document by rate-based scrolling in which the view automatically zooms out as scrolling speeds up, so the perceptual scrolling speed in screen space remains constant, and zooms back in as scrolling slows. The paper's statement of the problem: in rate-based scrolling a small handle movement can fling the user through a long document and lose all spatial context. The solution makes the loss visible and then repairs it. [C36934]

Visual principle: Speed and scale are one variable. As velocity rises the document recedes into an overview, landmarks sliding past at a constant perceived rate; as velocity falls the detail returns. The visual event is the smooth, continuous trade between how fast you move and how much you can see. In the web-browser prototype, headings and images act as semantic-zoom landmarks and stay legible while plain text is rendered as horizontal lines when zoomed out; a mouse-button press shows a pink slider, distance from the press point is the scroll speed, and release animates back to base scale. In the map viewer (a synthetic map generated from Perlin noise), a drag from a relative position sets direction and speed; a joystick variant failed in a reported way -- first-time users tilt the stick to maximum, causing a sudden speed-up and zoom-out. [C36935]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): A coupled control law, Eq. 1: scale = constant / speed; if the speed is below a threshold, scale = 1 (no zoom). The naive form fails twice, and each failure has a named fix. First, at the moment the mouse starts moving, the scale drops abruptly (Fig. 3); the revised formula, Eq. 2, is scale = s0 ^ ((dy - d0) / (d1 - d0)) -- scale changes exponentially with mouse movement (dy = distance moved from the press point, d0/d1 the movement range, s0 the minimum scale) -- and speed = v0 / scale, so the scale changes at a constant rate rather than jumping. Second, the document "swells" when the user reverses direction or releases the button: the rate crosses zero, so the system zooms back in suddenly; the fix is a delay and maximum limit on the rate of change of scale, applied during zoom-in only, with no delay for zoom-out (Fig. 5). The asymmetry is deliberate: zooming out must stay responsive, zooming in must not startle. The user controls only speed and direction; the system handles zoom. The paper's own tradeoffs (Table 2): appropriate for intermediate-size information spaces, spatial data, repetitive visits, self-centering absolute devices, expert users; less appropriate for small or huge spaces, symbolic or abstract data, one-time visits, relative pointing devices, novices. Evaluation: 7 users of moderate computer experience; in the web task (find 20 images in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland), completion times were approximately equal to scroll bars despite years of scroll-bar familiarity (per-subject seconds are not transcribed in the paper text and are not quoted here), and 6 of 7 preferred automatic zooming; in the map task, preference was 4 of 7 for automatic. Some users reported dizziness from the constant text flow; dexterous users and frequent video-game players did better. A circling-around-target overshoot is reported: zooming in on going back makes the target appear to move away. [C36936]

Artistic opportunity: A document that is also a landscape, navigated by a single gesture whose speed sets its own scale. No gallery piece couples velocity to zoom as a law; the closest pieces treat zoom as a separate control. Here the zoom is not a control at all -- it is the consequence of hurrying, and the overview is the reward for it. The two fixes are the honest part of the paper and the part to carry: the naive version fails visibly (onset jump, reversal swell), and the exponential form plus the zoom-in-only delay are what make the law feel like physics rather than a trick. [C36937]

**Takeaway:** Take a parameter the user already controls (speed) and let the system derive a second parameter (scale) from it, so one input does two jobs -- the design move Hinckley returns to everywhere in this corpus. The law is scale = constant / speed, with the exponential onset and the zoom-in-only delay as the two repairs that make it survivable. A piece that only implements the law without the repairs would repeat the paper's own failures. [C36938]

## M4 -- The touch has a signature (Sensor Synaesthesia, CHI 2011)

Subject: Hand-held devices that combine touch and motion sensing so that each modality disambiguates the other: tilt-to-zoom, soft vs. hard taps, gentle vs. forceful swipes. The paper's frame is "synaesthesia" because one sense (touch) is read through another (motion). Implementation: a Windows Phone 7 device (accelerometer sampled at 30 Hz) and a Zune HD (60 Hz), both capacitive multi-touch plus 3-axis linear accelerometer; a photo browser hosts all techniques except tilt-to-zoom, which is in a map application. [C36939]

Visual principle: A touch is not a point; it is an event with a body behind it. Touching the screen induces secondary accelerations and angular velocities in the motion sensors -- the device feels how it was touched, not just where. The legible image is a surface that reacts differently to a tap that lands softly and one that lands hard: in the paper's example, a soft tap on a calendar opens the day view, a hard tap navigates directly to the current appointment. Force is a visible quality of the mark, not a hidden parameter. In tilt-to-zoom the feedback is a perspective box that is axis-aligned at the start and distorts with tilt, its vanishing point above the screen when zooming in and below when zooming out. [C36940]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): Two directions, both executable. Touch-enhanced motion -- a touch starts a listening window and motion is the verb. Tilt-to-zoom: hold the thumb tip on the point to zoom about and tilt the device; zoom centre is the touch (x,y); sliding the thumb beyond a minimum distance triggers pan, holding the thumb still and then tilting transitions to zoom; top edge away from the body zooms out, top edge toward the body zooms in (slightly more than half of pilot users preferred this mapping; some preferred the opposite); rate-based, zoom speed a function of the change in forward-back tilt angle from the orientation at touch-down, which is reset to the current tilt on every touch; dead-band of plus/minus 5 degrees around the starting orientation before zooming starts. Pivot-to-lock: touch the screen anywhere and pivot the device to a new viewing orientation to lock the current landscape or portrait orientation; a padlock icon appears as feedback, the lock persists after the finger lifts, and users criticised auto-unlock -- "auto unlock is a horrible feature". Tip-to-select: frame a region with two thumbs, then quickly tip the device away and back -- it must depart the current orientation by at least plus/minus 15 degrees in any direction and return within a short time-out (value not stated) -- then the two fingers manipulate opposite corners of the marquee, and lifting the fingers crops the region. Hold-and-shake: hold an icon or photo and shake to apply a command (e.g. delete, a red square labelled "Deleted" appears), recognised by 3 successive peaks in the motion signal within 2 s; hold and shake again on the same spot to undo. Motion-enhanced touch -- motion before or during contact changes what the touch means. Soft-vs.-hard tap: the difference between the accelerometer peaks in the first 200 ms of the touch, separation greater than 0.3 g is a hard tap; soft tap activates, hard tap "drills into" the secondary target; the paper's rule is that two soft taps equal one hard tap; feedback is the tile animating in proportion to the force ("splat" for a hard tap); hard tap was criticised as "too hard on my fingers". Swipe-vs.-hard-drag: a gentle swipe scrolls or pans, a drag with a hard onset grabs the item and rearranges it without entering an "edit" mode. Context of touch: gyro traces for thumb vs. index-finger vertical swipes are distinct, the gyro more sensitive than the accelerometer and in the device frame; a proof-of-concept user-specific recogniser matches templates by sum of Euclidean distances within an error threshold. Evaluation: 10 Microsoft volunteers, about 10 minutes per technique, 7-point Likert scales plus rank ordering; tilt-to-zoom and pivot-to-lock resonated most, 5 of 10 explicitly mentioning one-handed interaction as the best thing about tilt-to-zoom; hold-and-shake was the only technique nobody chose as favourite or runner-up (too much effort and time); ranking differences were not significant (Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared(5) = 16.74, p > 0.05). Four learning steps were observed: the device reacts to motion; holding the screen starts the listening; generate the motion in synchrony with the touch (some users tilted before the finger touched down); learn the tilt mapping. [C36941]

Artistic opportunity: Force as a first-class visual variable. A piece in which a soft touch leaves a pale, wide mark and a hard touch a dark, narrow one -- and in which holding still while tilting the world zooms around the held point -- makes the body's effort part of the picture. The gallery has pressure in a few pieces; none classifies the touch by what the device felt when it landed. The honest constraint to carry is that a web page cannot read the paper's accelerometer the same way: the browser proxy (contact area and approach speed) must be disclosed in the build notes, and the gate should retire the prompt if it requires the paper's hardware itself. [C36942]

**Takeaway:** Touch is not an event, it is a window: "holding the screen starts listening" turns a touch into a 200 ms, 2 s, or until-release listening interval in which motion is the verb. The 0.3 g hard-tap threshold and the 15-degree tip are the same move at two scales -- a motion quantity becomes a mode switch, with no widget at all. The piece is the classified touch, not the sensor. [C36943]

## M5 -- Grip is a mode (Sensing Techniques for Tablet+Stylus, UIST 2014)

Subject: A tablet and stylus that read how they are held -- tripod writing grip vs. tuck grip vs. palm grip, which hand grips the tablet, pen barrel tilt and roll -- and use the grip to disambiguate intentional touch from accidental contact and to summon contextually appropriate tools. The pen is a flexible capacitive grid of 7x30 sensing elements covering the whole barrel, wrapped in heat-shrink tubing, in a 3D-printed case holding a miniature Wacom electromagnetic pen; it carries a MEMS gyroscope, accelerometer/magnetometer, and a 2.4 GHz transceiver; inertial sensors stream at 130 Hz and the 7x30 capacitance map at 30 Hz. The pen is 19 cm long and 14 mm in diameter, designed wireless but tethered for power because the battery could not be changed without damaging the grip sensor. The tablet case covers the entire back and sides with rigid printed-circuit boards, 44x26 capacitive sensing elements, streaming at 25 Hz. [C36944]

Visual principle: The hand's shape is the interface's state. A palm resting on the screen is not noise to be rejected; it is information -- the system reads the grip to know what the hand intends. The legible image is a surface that knows which parts of the hand are tool and which are rest: the pen's grip selects the tool, the palm's contact is drawn as a resting shadow rather than erased, and the tool changes when the grip changes, with no menu ever appearing. The paper's own feedback is part of the picture: a brief "radar circle" animation, centred on the palm-down location, fading as it expands, so the user is not left wondering whether the palm triggered something occluded by the hand. The tools are visible only while held: a circular magnifier loupe, a grey dashed ellipse that turns bold red when the airbrush sprays, a small palette that exists only while a nonpreferred finger is held down, a thumb menu fading in slowly at the gripped edge and freezing mid-fade when a stray thumb lands on it, a vellum layer curling over the screen on handoff. [C36945]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): A grip classifier plus a contact-motion gate. Four grips are recognised -- Writing, Tuck, Palm, No Grip -- by one-vs.-all Support Vector Machines outputting a probability distribution over the four; features are designed to be invariant to pen roll angle and grip height on the barrel (the raw 7x30 map fitted into a 7x10 normalised image, plus stylus yaw and pitch, the normalised grip histogram, the number of lit pixels, and the pixel sum). Training data: 9 right-handed participants, about 15 minutes per user, 1,200 samples per user per grip, 32,400 samples total. Accuracy: 88% overall, user-independent, in 10-fold cross-validation on the training set; in a follow-up with 9 additional right-handed users (8,100 samples), Writing 93%, Tuck 93%, Palm 77% (Palm low because several users held the pen very lightly, giving inconsistent contact); Writing vs. non-writing (Tuck or Palm) distinguished with 97% accuracy. The observational base is 9 people (4 female, 1 left-handed) on a 10-inch Windows 8 tablet, behaviours B1-B12, including: users only put the pen down when they anticipated not needing it for a prolonged time (B1); all users exhibited the Tuck grip, many also the Palm grip (B2); Tuck for quick transient touch, Palm for a longer sequence (B3); users extended fingers to touch from Tuck and Palm grips but never from the Writing grip (B5); users floated the palm above the screen, splayed fingers "crab-like", or perched the thumb on the bezel rim rather than letting it stray onto the touchscreen (B9 -- the paper's comment: "unnatural and potentially fatiguing accommodations" caused by the system's inability to tell intentional from unintentional touch). Palm rejection is reframed as context sensing: do not classify the touch, classify the context. The bump signal -- when the user plants the hand, the pen's motion sensors show a characteristic hard-contact profile, detected as a peak (second-order finite difference on the three combined axes of accelerometer or gyro) exceeding a minimum threshold within a 10-sample window -- is identified within a maximum of 56 ms after the touch-down event in the worst case. The rule: for as long as the detected palm contact persists, any new touch landing within a 300 pixel radius is flagged as a "palm"; any new touch away from the palm and without a coincident bump on the pen is a true intentional touch; palm contact is tracked, not rejected outright, so it can also be used as input (e.g. to orient menus or anchor objects). The tools, each an exact rule: Magnifier vs. full-canvas zoom -- stow the pen (Tuck or Palm) and bring two fingers into contact; if the stylus shows a bump coincident with the touch, the Magnifier appears, and if not, the hand is the bare nonpreferred hand and the system does standard two-finger pan/zoom; a light touch is an explicit workaround, and users leveraged it deliberately to choose pinch-to-zoom instead of the Magnifier. Drafting tools -- hold the pen in the Writing grip and touch down one finger of the nonpreferred hand (single touch without a corresponding bump); a small palette appears offering Airbrush (default) and Compass; the tools are spring-loaded, lasting only while the finger is held down; the Airbrush sprays where the nonpreferred finger is (needed because inertial sensing cannot locate the pen tip in x,y), with the pen's azimuth and elevation angles defining the spray ellipse (the conic section), grey dashed ellipse as preview, bold red dashed ellipse while spraying; the Compass constrains the pen to circular arcs centred on the nonpreferred finger's location. Virtual barrel button -- a candidate tap is a bump signal from the finger tap at the same time as a new contact on the capacitance map, accepted only when the stylus is not moving (users hold the pen still when lifting the index finger to tap), and the stylus must remain in a new moving (or not moving) state for at least 100 ms. Thumb menu -- grip sensing on the tablet case detects which hand holds the tablet and summons the menu at the appropriate side; it fades in over a 1.5 s interval and fades out after 350 ms if the user lets go; a thumb is judged unintentional if it lands coincident with a new hand grip on the corresponding back portion of the case, and the fade-in then freezes if the thumb overlaps the menu. Tablet handoff -- sensors detect both users grasping the device and the tablet level, and a special annotation layer "peels over" the tablet like a transparency or sheet of vellum (page-curl animation); the other user may annotate but not copy or navigate; it is not triggered when one user holds the display with two hands, because then the tablet is angled toward the user, not level. Evaluation: 8 people (3 female, all right-handed, 23-51 years old, mean 37), 60-90 minute sessions; palm detection was rated the most useful capability ("it's like magic", "this system is very cool, hopefully someday I can use it"); the virtual barrel button was the lowest ranked feature. Stated limits: the pen is tethered; all techniques need a minimum-force motion threshold and very soft touches are an inherent challenge; observations and techniques are for right-handed users, not extended to left-handers, walking or one-handed use. [C36946]

Artistic opportunity: The piece that watches how it is held. The viewer's grip -- something they never think about -- becomes the mode switch, and the mode switch is visible in the drawing itself: the same stroke is ink in one grip and a blade in another. Nothing in the gallery reads the hand's posture as input, and the palm-as-shadow (classified, not rejected) is the paper's own insight and is visually unlike anything in the gallery, where palms simply do not exist. The same honesty constraint as M4 applies: the paper's hardware grip sensing is not available to a web page, so the piece must use disclosed proxies (pointer type, contact shape, stylus tilt and pressure where the Pointer Events API reports them, number of simultaneous contacts), and the gate should retire the prompt if it requires the paper's hardware itself. [C36947]

**Takeaway:** A contact is never judged alone; it is judged by what the hands were doing in the roughly 100 ms around it -- the 300 px palm radius, the 56 ms bump window, and the 100 ms stillness rule are the same idea at three scales. The grip classifier's numbers are the transferable part (Writing 93%, Tuck 93%, Palm 77%, writing vs. non-writing 97%): the system does not need to be certain about which grip, only about whether the hand is writing. The palm is context, not noise. [C36948]

## M6 -- Two hands, one task (Symmetric Bimanual Interaction, CHI 2000)

Subject: A two-handed task in which each hand is assigned an identical role -- tracking a pair of targets, one with each hand -- as opposed to the usual asymmetric division (one hand frames, the other acts). The apparatus is deliberately minimal so the only difference between conditions is perception: two pens on a Wacom Intuos 12x18 inch tablet sampled at 60 Hz, a 21 inch display at 1280x1024, two red 20x20 px square targets moving along pseudorandom trajectories, each hand with a white 20x20 px cursor. The conditions are Separated (two red square targets, two white cursors, perceived as two tasks) vs. Integrated (a single red rectangle 20 px wide and as long as the distance between the two targets, with a white line drawn between the two cursor positions and the cursors hidden -- the same motor actions required, a different visual perception, one integrated task), crossed with distance between targets (100 px, Singular Attention, both points in the focal field, vs. 840 px, Divided Attention) and speed (Slow 1 px/frame vs. Fast 2 px/frame). Design: 8 right-handed volunteers, within-subjects, order counterbalanced, 7 blocks per condition with the first as practice, 6 blocks x 4 attention x speed combinations, trials of 45 s each, 384 total non-practice trials, the same 4 precomputed pseudorandom trajectories used for all blocks and conditions. [C36949]

Visual principle: Symmetry is a visible coupling, not a property of one hand. In the Integrated condition the two targets are connected by a red rectangle and the two cursors by a white line, so the pair reads as one body; in the Separated condition the same two hands read as two tasks. The legible image is two forms moving in parallel, joined by a visible link, with the parallelism itself as the picture: when the link is tight the pair moves as one body, when it loosens the two drift into sequence. [C36950]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): Two identical agents each tracking a moving target, and a parallelism metric adapted from Masliah's m-metric -- for each time step (1/60 s), %ER is the actual magnitude of movement toward the target divided by the movement required to reduce the error to zero, per hand, and parallelism is the ratio of the two hands' %ER values, larger value as denominator, averaged over the trial, bounded 0-1, where 1 means both hands simultaneously reducing error by the same amount. Results: overall parallelism 0.31 -- the paper's comment is "not very high", the hands are mostly not moving together even in a symmetric task. Integrated gives 12% more parallelism than Separated (F(1,6) = 7.28, p < 0.05); Singular beats Divided (F(1,6) = 108, p < 0.01); Slow beats Fast (F(1,6) = 46, p < 0.01). Total RMS error: visual integration is not significant (F(1,6) = 4.8, p = 0.06); Singular is superior (F(1,6) = 109, p < 0.01) and Slow is superior (F(1,6) = 87, p < 0.01). Symmetry: overall the left hand has 8% higher RMS error than the right, not significant (p = 0.07); RMS of the left hand is significantly higher than the right in Separated but not in Integrated (F(1,6) = 7.6, p < 0.05). The per-condition chart values in Figs. 2-5 were read off rendered images and are approximate, not text-quoted (for example parallelism about 0.45 separated vs. 0.54 integrated at slow/singular, falling to about 0.15 vs. 0.18 at fast/divided). The paper's conclusion: lack of visual integration causes asymmetric performance; divided attention and task difficulty cause more sequential (less parallel) performance but not more asymmetric error; parallelism is not a requirement for symmetric error performance; in symmetric tasks there is no tendency for the motor system to devote more resources to the dominant hand when attention is divided. [C36951]

Artistic opportunity: The gallery has many single-hand pieces and a few asymmetric two-hand ideas (M2); none has two equal hands. A piece with two mirrored drawing arms whose coupling the viewer controls -- and whose parallelism is the score -- makes coordination itself the content. The viewer is not drawing a picture; they are conducting a pair. The paper quietly limits the ambition, and the limit is the honest part: 0.31 overall parallelism means even in the best condition the hands are mostly taking turns, which is why the later tools (Pen+Touch, Stitching) are built around sequential phrasing (hold, then act) rather than true simultaneity. [C36952]

**Takeaway:** Visual integration is what makes two-handed input feel like one action -- the integrated bar is the whole point, because perception, not motor demand, is what integrates the task. The coupling parameter and the live parallelism score are the transferable mechanism: two identical agents, one visible link, and a number that says whether they moved together or apart. [C36953]

## M7 -- The frame is rotated (Kinesthetic Reference Frames, UIST 1999)

Subject: Two-handed input in which the input space of the hands and the output space of the display do or do not correspond -- the kinesthetic reference frame (where the hand feels itself to be) vs. the visual frame (where the mark appears). The theoretical spine is Guiard's Kinematic Chain model: the nonpreferred hand sets a frame of reference in which the preferred hand acts (for right-handers, the left hand orients the paper, the right hand moves the pen). The question is whether the frame needs to be felt in the body (kinesthetic) or whether it is enough to see it on screen (visual). The experiment separates the two by moving a ToolGlass -- a semi-transparent palette the left hand moves and the right hand clicks through -- across three spatial mappings, crossed with visual vs. nonvisual feedback. [C36954]

Visual principle: A mark that does not land where the hand went. The task is Kabbash's colorised connect-the-dots: 12 squares per set; the left hand moves a ToolGlass (50% transparent, 4-colour palette, 50x50 px segments) with no button press; the right hand controls the cursor; squares are 40x40 px; the start square is black with a white border and the goal square is one of red, green, blue, or yellow, the correct colour required to proceed; distances are 200 px and 500 px Euclidean. The legible image is the error itself -- the gap between intended and actual position, drawn as a visible offset vector that shrinks as the hand learns the new frame, and the drawing that results is the record of that learning. [C36955]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): Three spatial mappings crossed with visual feedback. Unified (U): both hands in the same space, common origin, 1.5 inch constant offset between cursors (the right hand moves relative to the frame defined by the left). Separated (S): the tablet divided into halves, each hand with its own origin, offset only in the horizontal direction. Relative (R): a cardboard template with two openings 6.5x10 inches, spaced 4.25 inches apart and centred; clutching required (lift the puck and put it down to reposition; the origin changes every time). Crossed with Visual (v): ToolGlass and cursor always visible, vs. Nonvisual (n): ToolGlass shown only when within proximity of twice ToolGlass size of the right cursor and not drawing, disappearing once drawing starts. Design: 12 right-handed participants, about 1.5 hours each, 6 conditions within-subjects, counterbalanced; 5 blocks per condition x 2 sets of 12 squares; 720 sets total; 3 warm-up sets per condition; 11 outlier trials (more than 3 SD) removed, about 1%, maximum 4% in any condition. Results (ANOVA on completion time per 12-square set): kinesthetic frame F(2,10) = 64.87, p < .0001; visual feedback F(1,5) = 59.00, p < .001; distance F(1,5) = 360.97, p < .0001. Hypotheses: H1 supported -- Uv, Sv, Rv did not differ significantly (with vision, the spatial mapping does not matter); H2 supported -- Rn and Sn differed significantly from the other means, Un did not differ from Uv (without vision, only the Unified frame survives; body-relative kinesthetic cues suffice). At 200 px only Rn differed; at 500 px Sn, Rv, and Rn differed. Mean times per 12-square set, read off Fig. 5 and approximate: Uv 24.1 s, Un 25.4 s, Sv 24.7 s, Sn 30.3 s, Rv 28.1 s, Rn 34.4 s, with clutching Rv 35.4 s and Rn 45.6 s. The conclusion, in the paper's own terms: visual feedback on the screen is sufficient for Guiard's reference principle, and an absolute spatial kinesthetic reference is not essential -- vision is the dominant channel and masks kinesthetic deficiencies; with body-relative kinesthetic cues (Unified) users cope without visual feedback, while with frequent origin changes (Relative) users become increasingly dependent on visual feedback, especially as movement magnitude grows. [C36956]

Artistic opportunity: A piece about adaptation rather than execution. The drawing is not the point; the shrinking gap between hand and mark is. No gallery piece deliberately misaligns the hand and the picture and then shows the hand learning its way back. This is the weakest of the passing mechanisms -- the visual consequence is a drawing made under a handicap, and the piece's interest depends on the viewer's willingness to adapt -- and it is ranked last for that reason. [C36957]

**Takeaway:** The frame can be purely visual and still work -- which is why the later pen+touch tools can rely on a finger hold being visible on screen (the pink highlight, the dotted ruler border) rather than felt. The control experiment behind the whole bimanual programme is also a warning for the piece: every clutch breaks the frame, and the user must rebuild it from vision alone, so the error trace must always be visible or the piece is only a handicap. [C36958]

## M8 -- The stroke that spans the seam (Stitching, AVI 2004)

Subject: A pen gesture that starts on one display, crosses the gap, and continues onto a second display -- stitching the two devices together so content can flow across them. The paper's problem is spontaneous device sharing between devices that do not know each other's network addresses: the gesture is the address. No menus, no network setup, no explicit pairing. [C36959]

Visual principle: Continuity across a break. The stitch is a single unbroken gesture that refuses to stop at the edge: it leaves one surface, crosses the void, and lands on the other, and the crossing is what connects them. The legible image is the seam itself -- a fold or gap in the canvas -- and the thread that crosses it, carrying a form from one side to the other. The feedback is visible on the receiving screen: a short chirp, a semi-transparent blue shadow in the shape of the selected photos, an animated cone whisking files between screens, and a remote menu hanging off a transparent blue cone back to the origin screen if the pen is held still for 0.5 s. The seam is not a defect in the picture; it is the picture's hinge. [C36960]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): An envelope is the time interval during which the pen is in range and moving above a predetermined speed threshold. The recognition criteria, exact: the first envelope must end near the first screen's border and last more than 250 ms; the second envelope must start near the second screen's border and last more than 100 ms; the second envelope must start after the first ends, but no more than 1.5 s after it (supporting stitching up to about 75 cm, arm's reach); the direction of the pen on exit vs. entry must match within plus/minus 20 degrees; recognition is eager, as soon as the first 100 ms of the second envelope is observed, before the gesture finishes. Geometry: fit a line to the pen coordinates on each screen; record p0 (exit point on device 1), p10 (entry point on device 2), p11 (recognition point), alpha0 (angle at p0); alpha = (alpha0 + alpha1)/2, offset = tan(alpha) x bezel width, and PB is the displacement of p1 along device 2's edge by the offset in pixels; empty space between devices is ignored. Commands and operands: remote postfix menus are implemented (stitch first, then choose the command from the remote menu); implemented commands are copy/move photos, persistent shared workspace, expand image across screens, and Gallery; the default with an operand is move, not copy -- pilot users repeatedly copied back and forth creating clutter, so the default was changed. Operands are a tap on a single photo or a lasso of several; selected photos are outlined in orange and scaled slightly larger; selection cools after 3 s (highlights turn blue, no longer an operand). Coexistence: dragging-state stitching uses speculative execution (assume drag, undo if a stitch is reported); tracking-state stitching requires the pen tip within 2 cm of the screen surface (a palm resting helps) and avoids speculative execution. Proxemics: intimate (devices together, tiled displays), personal (up to about 75 cm), social (beyond arm's reach -- the Transporter: drag a photo to the screen edge, dwell, a collapsing blue square animation, then the photo is transported), public (over 4 m, not supported); orientations supported are face-to-face, shoulder-to-shoulder, corner-to-corner (90 degrees). Architecture: the stitching server is typically hosted on one mobile device and only that address is needed to bootstrap; the server extends to sets of up to 16 devices, but StitchMaster supports pairs only; cooperative stitching is noted, one user drawing the first half of the stitch and a second user completing the second half on another device. Evaluation: 12 participants from the Microsoft usability pool, run in pairs of strangers (1 female-female, 3 male-female, 2 male-male), 2-3 minutes practice, about 1 hour sessions; all 12 created the persistent shared workspace on their first or second try with a no-operand stitch; all but 2 first attempted stitching by dragging (not tracking); "I would use this software if it were available" averaged 6.7/7 and "Combining the screens ... was a compelling capability" 6.8/7; in 3 of 7 sessions participants asked "Do they have to be right next to one another?" and moved the tablets 15-40 cm apart. The most human finding is establish-and-relax: once users trust the connection, the gesture shrinks to 3-5 cm stitches onto the other screen, the pen held near the top like a pointing stick. [C36961]

Artistic opportunity: The seam as a verb. The gallery has pieces with folds and divisions, but none in which the act of drawing across a boundary is itself the connection -- the stroke as a needle, the seam as something to be sewn. The transfer is visible: a form drawn on the left arrives, changed by the crossing, on the right. The piece must be a single canvas with a fold, not two panels -- Hans rejected a two-panel form for No.840, so the seam is a fold line down the centre of one sheet. [C36962]

**Takeaway:** Stitching is Pen+Touch's grammar with the hold replaced by a bezel: the crossing is the phrase. The 250 ms / 100 ms / 1.5 s / 20-degree envelope is the same hold+threshold+window shape, but the "hold" is a continuous motion across empty space. And the gesture shrinks with trust -- 3-5 cm and a pointing stick -- which is the piece's second act: the seam loosens where it has been sewn often. [C36963]

## M9 -- Closeness is a stage (Micro-mobility and F-formations, UIST 2012) -- REJECTED

Subject: Devices and people that share more as they draw nearer -- micro-mobility (tilting and angling a device toward another) and F-formations (how people arrange themselves to share), with engagement increasing in stages as a function of proximity. The theory is Hall's proxemics (intimate 0-50 cm, personal about 1 m, social about 4 m, public over 4 m) and Kendon's F-formations: people arrange themselves in a formation with an inner o-space (the joint activity space), a surrounding p-space ring (the people, i.e. group membership), and an outer r-space (buffer). The evidence base is a design study of 10 paired participants, videotaped, with foam-core mock-ups (slate 30.5x21 cm, reader 15x21 cm, phone 6x12 cm); behaviours B1-B8 include devices moving in and out of o-space as the topic changes (B3), incidental tilting not being a cue alone but needing the F-formation context (B4), and gestures at another person's display being brief, typically 1-2 s (B7). The four techniques are all active only when the users are sensed to be in an F-formation and the recipient holds the tablet up in o-space: Tilt-to-Preview (hold a finger on content and tip the slate by at least 10 degrees; a transient semi-transparent copy appears on all devices in the formation, sliding in from the sender's side); Face-to-Mirror (hold the tablet vertical, angle greater than 70 degrees, no touch needed; users said over 70 degrees "required extreme tilt"); Portals (tilt the slate by more than 10 degrees toward another device, a tinted edge appears along the shared screen edge, drag an item through the edge and release to transfer it -- moved, not copied -- to the other device); Cross-Device Pinch-to-Zoom (a pinch enlarging content beyond one slate's visible area expands onto the surrounding tablets, creating a larger cross-device virtual canvas). The hardware is Asus EP121 slates (1.16 kg, 312x207x17 mm), Phidgets motion sensors, Qualcomm QSRCT 8 GHz radios (distance sensing 10 cm accuracy at 90% confidence, maximum range 15 m), and two overhead Kinect depth cameras at 200 cm height tracking a 220x94 cm area per camera; the F-formation detection pipeline filters depth components, normalises height, finds heads as the topmost depth band and shoulders as the second, disambiguates facing with a third band plus head shift, and classifies side-by-side / L-shaped / face-to-face with hysteresis -- error rates not formally evaluated, proof of concept only. Evaluation: 6 participants, all male, 29-50 years old; Portals ranked favourite, then Tilt-to-Preview, then Cross-Device Pinch-to-Zoom, then Face-to-Mirror last; copy-vs.-move semantics confused users. [C36964]

**Takeaway:** REJECTED -- duplicate-adjacent to No.9110. The mechanism is real (distance maps to engagement stages, orientation gates which stages are reachable), but its visible outcome -- two forms approaching and merging -- is the core visual of No.9110 (metaballs, approved V4), and the stage differences would read as labels on a merge rather than as distinct visual systems. What survives into the study is the social threshold: gestures at another person's display are brief, 1-2 s, which is why the techniques are transient by design -- a rule about brevity, not about merging. [C36965]

## The arc: who does the work

The 30 years read here trace one sustained argument: the device should read the hand, and the hand should not have to say what it already means. The early work (1994-1998, Virginia and neurosurgery) asks what the hands can do together -- passive real-world props (a doll's head, a cutting plane) as tangible handles for 3D medical images, cooperative bimanual action, two-handed virtual manipulation -- and the control experiment (UIST 1999) establishes that the nonpreferred hand's frame can be purely visual and still work. The sensing programme (1999-2005) moves the question from the hands to the device: a PDA that knows how it is held, tilted, and whether anyone is looking at it, with a context server of shared state (Holding, LookingAt, Flat, Moving, Shaking, Walking, Proximity) and small rules over it -- a voice memo that starts when the device is raised to the mouth like a phone (Holding + Close proximity at about 8 cm + tilt toward the user, all holding for 0.1 s), a screen that rotates when tilted past a 5-degree gray zone and held 0.5 s, tilt scrolling with a dead-band of 4 degrees and a transfer function vfb = K . sgn(dAfb) . max(|dAfb| - dAmin, 0)^alpha with K = 0.2 and alpha = 1.6, auto-on when Holding + LookingAt + Portrait persists 0.5 s. The pen work (2005-2014) gives the hand a language: the pen writes, touch manipulates, the hold phrases them into tools, the grip selects the tool, the palm is context. The cross-device work (2003-2021) scales the same grammar from hands to bodies: a 10-degree tilt is the hold, the F-formation is the context, the o-space is the selection. The late work (2016-2026) closes the loop: pre-touch reads the approach before contact, posture-aware pen+touch reads how the tablet is held, and the newest papers (ImaginationVellum, Intent Tagging, Texterial, Proscenium) ask what the hand means when the system can finish its sentences. One grammar runs through all of it: a sustained contact or posture (the hold) opens a listening window, a measured quantity crossing a threshold inside that window selects the command, and releasing ends it. The question moves from "what can the hand do" to "what does the hand already mean" -- and the answer is always a number: 250 ms, 0.3 g, 10 degrees, 56 ms, 300 px. [C36966]

## The aesthetic logic

Hinckley's systems share a visual grammar that is directly portable: state that exists only while a hand holds something, and state that is deliberately invisible the rest of the time. The pink highlight around a selected object, the dotted ruler border, the radar circle blooming where a palm lands, the grey dashed ellipse turning bold red when the airbrush sprays, the blue shadow and cone between screens, the ghost copy sliding in from a sender's side, the vellum layer peeling over on handoff -- every one of these is drawn at the point of contact and vanishes when the hand lets go. There are no toolbars, no mode indicators, no chrome until a hand approaches. The palette discipline is the same as the paper's own: paper white, ink black, and one accent that always means the same thing (pink for selection, red for the spray and the hard tap, blue for the transferred shadow). The pieces this study prompts should keep that discipline -- the feedback drawn on the drawing, the mode in the hand, the interface a function of the hands rather than of the screen. [C36967]
