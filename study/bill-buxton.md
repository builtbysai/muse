## 1. The brief: the musician who measures the hand

Buxton enters computing through music, not through computing, and the whole career is a straight line from that door. He is a tenor saxophonist who works as a professional musician for more than a decade, takes a Bachelor of Music from Queen's University in June 1973 (composition and analysis), spends two years at the Institute of Sonology in Utrecht, and arrives at the University of Toronto in 1975 as an informal artist-in-residence, where he emerges in 1978 with a master's in computer science and a working digital musical instrument. The thesis title gives the game away: "Design Issues in the Foundation of a Computer-Based Tool for Music Composition". He never completes a PhD. His four doctorates are all honoris causa -- Ontario College of Art and Design 2007, Queen's 9 June 2009, Technical University Eindhoven 22 April 2010, University of Toronto 2013. The musician never leaves the work: the chunking paper of 1986 explains a dialogue the way a musician explains a phrase, tension and release; the three-state model is a notation for what a hand is doing; the collection is an archive of instruments. He is not a computer scientist who happens to like music. He is a musician who found that the instrument he needed did not exist, and spent fifty years building the theory of why.

**Takeaway:** The door matters. He came in through the saxophone, and every mechanism that follows is a musician's mechanism -- phrasing, tension, closure, two hands doing different jobs at once. [C35266]

## 2. What he is: a collector-theorist, not an inventor-hero

The first thing to get right about Buxton is what he is not. He is not the inventor of the mouse, or of multi-touch, or of the marking menu as a solo act, and his own writing is at pains to say so. His column on the AT-550 exists to puncture exactly that myth: the companies that start the buzz "neither invented the underlying technology, nor were they the first companies to exploit it commercially" -- and that, he insists, is "not a criticism, but rather a respectful commentary on the nature of design and innovation". What he is, is a collector-theorist. He collects input devices -- more than 800 of them, over thirty-five years -- and the collection is the research method: put the objects in a row and the history becomes visible, the long nose made of plastic and glass and steel. The papers are the theory of what the objects show. The books are the method for making the next object. To call him an inventor-hero would be to miss the argument, which is that invention is the wrong unit of analysis. Prospecting is the unit: finding something already there, below the surface, and refining it until the world is ready.

**Takeaway:** Do not build him a hero's monument. Build the row of objects, and let the history in them do the arguing. [C35267]

## 3. Queen's: the studio he built, and Le Caine

At Queen's in Kingston, Ontario, beginning in about 1971, Buxton builds an electronic music studio from scratch with David Keane -- there had not been one there before -- and works at three levels at once: user, designer and maintainer, teacher of others. In his own telling this is the origin of his design sensibility: "there is no faster way to learn about the inefficiencies of design ... than to try and explain to somebody else how to use it." In 1972-73 he travels two or three times to Ottawa to work on a film soundtrack using Ken Pulfer's computer music system at the National Research Council, encouraged by Istvan Anhalt, and spends hours in conversation with Hugh Le Caine, working just around the corner on analogue devices -- the Sackbut keyboard, the variable-speed tape recorder used for Dripsody, the photo-resistor Spectrogram, on which you draw music on acetate and pull it over light cells. Le Caine's axiom, which Buxton says he still follows: "Really, all you have to do is say what you want and learn how to describe it, and I will build it for you." Alongside, in 1971-73, he takes courses at St. Lawrence College: basic electronics, technical math, advanced photography. The pattern is set before he ever touches a computer as a profession: describe what you want in your own terms, and make the machine meet you there.

**Takeaway:** The whole career is in the Le Caine axiom. The user describes; the builder builds. Everything Buxton writes later is about how rarely machines keep that bargain. [C35268]

## 4. Utrecht: the slant

Two years at the Institute of Sonology, Utrecht State University, 1973-75 -- classes in studio technique, compositional theory and computer composition, linguistics, electro-acoustics, psycho-acoustics; lecturer in computer music techniques in 1974/75 -- at what his home-page bio calls "one of the top electro-acoustic music studios in the world" at the time. In his own words it "slanted my viewpoint in ... a very lucky direction" and "saved me from wandering around in ... not the most fruitful directions". The slant is the point. Utrecht gives him the psycho-acoustics and the linguistics, the sense that a system is something a body perceives and a language describes, and then Leslie Mezei writes from Toronto, suggesting he come to fill gaps in his computer-science background and build the team and funding for a new digital instrument. Mezei is the hinge of the whole biography: the man who recruits the musician into the computer science department, and whom Buxton still names among his mentors fifty years later. Without that letter there is no Toronto, no multi-touch tablet, no three-state model, no column about a watch.

**Takeaway:** A career is a slant, not a plan. Two years in Utrecht bent the trajectory, and a letter from Toronto finished the bend. [C35269]

## 5. Toronto: artist-in-residence and the Structured Sound Synthesis Project

In 1975 he arrives at the University of Toronto's Dynamic Graphics Project as an informal artist-in-residence in the Computer Science department, "experimenting with new ways of making music with computers". The project is the Structured Sound Synthesis Project, an interdisciplinary team -- Gustav Ciamaga from the Faculty of Music, Leslie Mezei and Ron Baecker from computer science, Casey Smith from Electrical Engineering, graduate and undergraduate students and composers including the Canadian Electronic Ensemble -- funded by the Canada Council, and he raises a 250,000-dollar grant to build a digital instrument. U of T Magazine's verdict on the period: "We were all kind of outlaws within that department ... deemed to not be doing serious things" -- and the HCI contributions were "unintended consequences" of play. That is the honest origin story of a field: not a research programme with milestones, but outlaws playing with a digital instrument, and the play turning out to be the serious thing. The score editors the team builds in these years -- hierarchy and instance in a data structure for computer music (1978), the evolution of the SSSP score editing tools (1979), a microcomputer-based conducting system (1980), scope in interactive score editors (1981) -- are where the gesture ideas are born: drawing on a screen, circling to group, conducting as input. The input theory of the 1980s is the music software of the 1970s, generalised.

**Takeaway:** The serious work started as unserious play. The outlaws in the department were building the future while the department looked the other way. [C35270]

## 6. The MSc and the pivot

M.Sc., Computer Science, University of Toronto, 1978, thesis "Design Issues in the Foundation of a Computer-Based Tool for Music Composition". His home-page bio puts it in one sentence: "He arrived in 1975 as an artist in residence, and emerged in 1978 with an MSc in Computer Science -- and a working digital musical instrument." This is the pivot, and it is worth naming as one: music to human-computer interaction, in three years, without ever leaving the instrument behind. From here the publications change key: the computer-music papers continue into the early 1980s, but "Continuous Hand-Gesture Driven Input" (Graphics Interface '83, Edmonton) is the bridge paper, and "Lexical and Pragmatic Considerations of Input Structures" (1983) is the first paper that is HCI rather than music. The pivot is not a rejection of music. It is the recognition that the problem was never music. The problem was input -- how a person tells a machine what they want, in their own terms, with their own hands -- and music was only the first place he had met it.

**Takeaway:** The pivot is a widening, not a turn. The question stays the same; the instrument gets bigger. [C35271]

## 7. Multi-touch in 1984: the drum, the tablet, and not being first

The fact that matters most about Buxton and multi-touch is the date, and the second fact that matters most is that he was not first. His Toronto group was working on multi-touch in 1984, the same year the first Macintosh was released: the electronic drum he designed that year was one of the first multi-touch devices, and the capacitive multi-touch tablet -- Lee, Buxton and Smith, CHI '85 -- sensed more than one point of contact at once, each with a continuous degree of contact, from light touch to hard press. But Buxton is explicit, in print and repeatedly, that his group was not first: touch screens were in use for air-traffic control in the late 1960s, Bob Boie's Bell Labs tactile sensors precede his tablet and were more advanced, and the pinch gesture dates to 1983 in Wayne Westerman's later account. The claim he does make is narrower and stronger: an early capacitive multi-touch tablet with degree of contact, and, more importantly, the argument for why degree of contact matters -- without pressure, a touch tablet is crippled exactly when you need it most, because to signal a state change you must lift off, that is, stop touching. With pressure, a harder press is the signal. Three fingers can fade three colour channels at once. The iPhone arrives in 2007, twenty-three years later, and the world calls multi-touch new. Buxton's whole later career is the answer to that sentence: it was not new, it had a long nose, and the nose is the story.

**Takeaway:** He was early, not first, and he says so. The honesty is the argument: if you want to know what is new, look at what is twenty years old. [C35272]

## 8. EuroPARC and the split years

Lecturer at Toronto from 1979 or 1980 -- his own CV gives both dates in different sections, September 1979 in the teaching section and September 1980 in the employment history, and no source read adjudicates it -- then Associate Professor 1987-94. In June 1987 he moves to Cambridge, England, to help establish Rank Xerox EuroPARC, a satellite of Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center, holding the Toronto associate professorship through the whole period. From June 1989 to June 1994 he splits: Scientific Director of the Ontario Telepresence Project at Toronto, and Senior Research Consultant at Xerox PARC in Palo Alto, half-time from September 1989. The signature output of these years is the marking menu, with his PhD student Gordon Kurtenbach -- "Issues in Combining Marking and Direct Manipulation Techniques" (UIST '91), the empirical evaluation with Abigail Sellen (1993), the limits of expert performance with hierarchic marking menus (InterCHI '93), user learning and performance (CHI '94) -- work that wins the UIST Lasting Impact Award in October 2005. The split years are also the Toolglass years at PARC, and the years he sits on the editorial board of Human-Computer Interaction and juries the Prix Ars Electronica. The pattern of the career is visible now: Toronto and California, university and industry, held together by the same questions about hands and modes.

**Takeaway:** He works in two places at once for a decade, and the work is better for it. The marking menu is a Toronto idea tested in California. [C35273]

## 10. Buxton Design, Bruce Mau, and the book

Principal of his own boutique design and consulting firm, Buxton Design, Toronto, December 2002 to December 2005; Microsoft's 2005 press release adds that he was also chief scientist at Bruce Mau Design in this period, a role the CV's employment history does not list, so it is carried as secondary-source only. Lecturer at the Ontario College of Art and Design in late 2004, Visiting Professor at the Knowledge Media Design Institute, and Visiting Researcher with the Computer-Mediated Living Group at Microsoft Research Cambridge in early 2005. In this period he is finishing the book that becomes his best-known work. **Takeaway:** The book is a job application written as a theory. He wrote down what design should be, then went to work where the people who believed it were. [C35275]

## 14. The three-state model

Every pointing transaction can be described as movement between three states of the input device: State 0, out of range, where the device is not sensed at all and movement has no effect; State 1, tracking, where movement moves only the cursor and nothing is engaged; State 2, dragging, where movement moves or engages objects on screen. Not every device supports all three: a mouse has only States 1 and 2, because it is always in range; a touch tablet has only States 0 and 1, because touch is tracking and there is no separate "press" state; a stylus tablet has all three, hover for State 1 and press for State 2. The model, published as "A Three-State Model of Graphical Input" (INTERACT '90, 1990, refining Buxton, Hill and Rowley 1985), is a state-transition diagram, not a device list: it lets you match the demands of a transaction against the capabilities of a transducer, and it exposes which transactions a device simply cannot express. That last part is the point. A device that cannot reach State 2 cannot drag, and no amount of clever software changes that; a device that cannot leave State 1 cannot hover. The model is a vocabulary for saying, precisely, what a hand can and cannot do with a given thing in it -- and Buxton himself notes in the paper's footnote that the published version missed a reference to Newman (1968), a small honesty that tells you how the man works.

**Takeaway:** Three states, and the state is the subject. Show the viewer the state change itself -- ghost, tracker, dragger -- and the model becomes visible. [C35279]

## 15. The taxonomy and the lexical/pragmatic split

Two papers from 1983 do the classifying work the whole field still uses. "Lexical and Pragmatic Considerations of Input Structures" (Computer Graphics 17(1), 1983, sole author) splits Foley and van Dam's "lexical" level in two. Lexical: the spelling of tokens -- the ordering of lexemes, the alphabet used, symbolic versus iconic. Pragmatic: gesture, space, and devices -- where devices sit, what physical gesture articulates a token. The same lexical command, different pragmatics, different interface: the keystroke model's key-push count is lexical, homing time and pointing time are pragmatic, and design fails when it optimises spelling and ignores the body that has to spell it. The same paper builds the taxonomy: devices classified by the physical property the transducer senses -- position, motion, or pressure -- against the number of dimensions sensed, one, two, or three, and by whether the hand reaches the sensor through a mechanical intermediary (a stylus, a puck, a joystick) or by direct touch. The grid is rooted in the human motor and sensory system, so an empty cell is a prediction -- a device that could exist and a gesture it could capture. Card, Mackinlay and Robertson build on it explicitly in 1990-91. The taxonomy is not filing. It is a map with blank spaces that say: dig here.

**Takeaway:** The grid predicts. An empty cell is not an absence, it is an instruction -- a device waiting to be invented, and a gesture waiting to be caught. [C35280]

## 16. Chunking and phrasing: tension and closure

Expert skill is the ability to perform ever-larger chunks of a problem automatically, and a dialogue should be engineered so its structure matches the chunking an expert would use. That is the argument of "Chunking and Phrasing and the Design of Human-Computer Dialogues" (IFIP World Computer Congress, Dublin, 1986, sole author -- Baudel is not a co-author, whatever the brief said). The glue is phrasing: a compound action is articulated by one continuous gesture, held together by muscular tension, and delimited by closure -- relaxation back to a neutral state -- exactly as a musical phrase is bounded by tension and release. The proof-reader's mark is the model case: one stroke says the verb (move), the direct object (what), and the indirect object (where), with no syntax errors possible because the ordering is implicit in the gesture. A menu selection is the same shape: invoke, navigate, select, return -- one phrase, held together by the finger staying pressed. The musician is audible in every line of this paper. A phrase is not a sequence of notes. It is a shape held together by tension until it closes. A dialogue should be the same.

**Takeaway:** A gesture is a phrase. Tension holds it together, closure ends it, and if the gesture breaks mid-phrase the meaning collapses with it. [C35281]

## 17. Contiguous gesture and marking menus

The applied form of chunking is the contiguous gesture, and its home is GEdit, Gordon Kurtenbach's testbed (Kurtenbach and Buxton, SIGCHI Bulletin 1991; UIST '91). In GEdit a single uninterrupted mark can express a complete command: the shape of the mark says what object to create -- direction of stroke for square, circle, triangle -- the starting point says where it goes, and an extended mark says move, copy, or delete. Groups are specified by circling. Different aspects of one mark control different parameters: location, shape, size. And markings can eliminate modes: there is no tool palette to select from, so there is no mode to be in. The marking menu is the same idea made into a menu (Kurtenbach, Sellen and Buxton, 1993; Kurtenbach and Buxton, InterCHI '93 and CHI '94; Kurtenbach's 1993 Toronto PhD thesis, supervised by Buxton). A marking menu is a pie menu in which the cursor's path leaves an ink trail: a novice presses, waits about a third of a second, the radial menu appears, and strokes through a sector to select; an expert makes the same stroke without waiting -- the menu never appears, the mark alone invokes the command. The physical movement is identical in both cases, so practice is rehearsal: every novice selection trains the expert gesture. Three design principles from Kurtenbach's thesis -- self-revelation, guidance, rehearsal -- and the measured payoff: marks about 3.5 times faster than menu selection in the test application, reliable for four-item menus up to four levels deep and eight-item menus two levels deep. Attribution matters here and is stated plainly: Kurtenbach is the lead, pie menus themselves are Callahan and colleagues (1988), and Buxton is co-author and the theoretical frame. To say "Buxton invented marking menus" would be the inventor-hero error his own column warns against.

**Takeaway:** The same movement for novice and expert, and the ink trail is the evidence. Without the visible mark it is just a radial menu, which is someone else's. [C35282]

## 18. Two hands: parallel, asymmetric, and the wrong hand

Give each hand a continuous subtask of one compound task, on a separate transducer, and people do them simultaneously without prompting and without significant extra cognitive load -- and speed tracks the degree of parallelism. That is "A Study in Two-Handed Input" (Buxton and Myers, CHI '86, Boston -- not CHI '87 as one bibliography says): in the first experiment subjects position a shape with one hand while scaling it with the other, trained and instructed to do it sequentially, and do it in parallel anyway; in the second, novices using two hands outperform experts using the conventional one-handed technique. The everyday model is writing with one hand and turning pages with the other. But not all two-handedness is equal. Kabbash, Buxton and Sellen (CHI '94) distinguish independent bimanual work, where each hand has its own subtask, from asymmetric-dependent work, where the non-preferred hand's action is the input to the preferred hand's action: the left hand sets the frame of reference, acts first and acts more coarsely, and the right hand then acts finely within that frame. This is Guiard's kinematic-chain model (1987), applied and tested by Buxton's group, not invented by them. Result: the asymmetric-dependent Toolglass technique gives the best overall performance, while the independent two-cursor technique gives no time benefit over one-handed use and worse cognitive-load measures. Two hands help only when their roles are asymmetric and dependent. And the non-preferred hand is not a bad preferred hand (Kabbash, MacKenzie and Buxton, InterCHI '93): for small targets at short distances the preferred hand wins, but for larger targets and larger distances both hands perform about the same, and the trackball degrades least across hands. The design implication is that the non-preferred hand is well suited to coarse tasks that do not need precision -- such as scrolling -- which is exactly the role it plays in the compound tasks. The Napoleon line from "Smoke and Mirrors" (1990) is the polemic form of the same argument: computers like the Macintosh were designed for Napoleon, because unless you are typing, you can work all day with one hand tucked into your jacket.

**Takeaway:** Two hands are not twice one hand. One sets the frame, coarse and first; the other works finely inside it -- and the wrong hand is not worse, only different. [C35283]

## 19. Modes: held, not latched

A mode error (Norman, 1981) is acting as if the system were in a different mode than it is -- issuing navigation commands while in insert mode, or the reverse. Sellen, Kurtenbach and Buxton (Human-Computer Interaction, 1992; conference version INTERACT '90) show that kinesthetic feedback beats visual feedback at preventing them, and that the reason is maintenance: a non-latching foot pedal that the user must actively hold down to stay in insert mode gives continuous proprioceptive knowledge of the mode -- the tension in the leg is the mode indicator. A latching switch, or a visual indicator alone, lets the mode slip from awareness. Their conclusion, in their words: user-maintained mode states prevent mode errors more effectively than system-maintained mode states. This is the chunking paper's tension principle applied to modes, and the attribution is Sellen's first -- the work grows from her doctoral research on human error, the mode-error concept itself is Norman's, and Buxton is co-author. The watchable translation is direct: a piece with two modes -- draw versus erase, attract versus repel -- where the mode only holds while something is visibly held, a form staying dark and tense while pressed and relaxing pale on release. If the mode were latched instead, the piece would show the failure: forms acting in the wrong mode, marks appearing where erasures were meant, until nobody can tell which mode is live. The held state is drawn on screen as strain; the latched state as ambiguity.

**Takeaway:** A mode you must hold cannot be forgotten, because your body is holding it. A mode the system holds for you can slip away unnoticed. [C35284]

## 20. Toolglass, transparency, and virtual devices

A Toolglass widget is a semi-transparent tool palette that floats between the application and the cursor, like a sheet of glass: you position it with one hand and click through it with the other, so tool selection and application happen in one compound gesture instead of a trip to a fixed palette and back. Magic lenses are see-through filters that change the view of what lies beneath them. The underlying claim: modes should be spatial -- where the glass is -- rather than temporal -- which mode you are in -- because spatial modes are visible and do not have to be remembered. The T3 paradigm (Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Baudel and Buxton, CHI '97) integrates tablets, two hands, and transparency into one GUI. Attribution again: Toolglass is Bier and colleagues (SIGGRAPH '93), Buxton fourth author; do not credit it to Buxton. His role is the two-handed and compound-task framing and the CHI '94 experimental validation. The sibling idea is virtual input devices (Brown, Buxton and Murtagh, INTERACT '90): as a window system partitions one physical display into many virtual displays, one physical input surface can be partitioned into many virtual input devices -- spatially distinct regions of a single tablet, each emulating a separate controller, a slider here, a dial there, a keyboard elsewhere, each reporting to the application as if it were an independent physical device. It only works on transducers that are position-sensitive, with a fixed planar coordinate system. Touch tablets qualify; mice do not. Both ideas say the same thing from opposite sides: the surface is not one thing, and where you are on it is what you are doing.

**Takeaway:** Put the mode in space, not in time. Where the glass is, is the mode -- and one surface can be many instruments, if you can say where you are on it. [C35285]

## 21. The three mirrors

An evaluation test in the form of a metaphor, from "Smoke and Mirrors" (Byte, 1990) and "The Three Mirrors of Interaction" (Friend21 '91, Tokyo): hold a system up to three mirrors and ask how faithfully it reflects the user. Mirror one, physical: the motor and sensory system -- two hands, eyes, ears, feet, pressure sense. Mirror two, cognitive: how people think, learn, decide, and solve problems. Mirror three, social: group activity, power structures, the social ecology of daily life and work. A design is adequate only if it gives a reasonably undistorted reflection in all three; most systems fail at least one. "Smoke and mirrors" is the pun and the warning: stagecraft is not reflection. The three mirrors are also the measure of his own career: the physical mirror is the three-state model and the two-handed work; the cognitive mirror is chunking and the marking menu's novice-to-expert arc; the social mirror is the telepresence project and the late work on hybrid meetings. A piece built on the mirrors would hold the same system up to each in turn and then take one away, and the viewer would see the system go lopsided -- the same forms, suddenly clumsy, forgetful, or lonely. The test is the spectacle.

**Takeaway:** A system must reflect the body, the mind, and the society, all three. Fail one mirror and the reflection is a trick. [C35286]

## 22. The long nose, and the axiom

The claim that major technologies take at least twenty years, typically twenty to thirty, from first inception to the "billion-dollar" tipping point, passing through phases of research and early application below the radar. The mouse took about thirty years; multi-touch took about twenty-five, first work in the early 1980s, iPhone 2007. Innovation is therefore less about inventing brilliantly out of the blue than about prospecting -- finding something already there, below the surface, and refining it -- and the companies that win are not the inventors of the technology nor the first to exploit it commercially, but the ones that recognise its latent value and take the risk of exploiting it well. The popular form is "The Long Nose of Innovation" (BusinessWeek, 2 January 2008); the idea is carried in the multi-touch overview (original 12 January 2007, revised to 10 December 2025), in "A Touching Story" (SID, 2010), and in the 2009 column that names the AT-550 "a textbook example of my notion of the 'Long Nose of Innovation'". No first use before 2007 was verified in this study; do not cite an origin year earlier than that. The corollary, stated in the same texts: learn from failures more than successes, and a mediocre implementation can kill a good technology without the technology being at fault. And the standing challenge to presenters: if you claim something will have huge impact in five years and you cannot show fifteen years of its history, you have not done your homework. Beside the long nose sits the axiom, his "primary axiom" in his own words: "Everything is best for something and worst for something else." No technology is good in general; every device, technique, and modality has a for-what. The designer's job is knowing what it is best for, what it is worst for, for whom, when, where, and most importantly why -- including knowing when, why, and how not to use an otherwise trendy technology. Applied to touch: thin lines are hard with a finger, "there's a reason we use a paint brush"; a finger is no more suited to signing a contract than a pen is to turning a page. Do not try to replace the mouse; find what is strong where the mouse is weak and let it complement the mouse, in the other hand if necessary. No first-use year for the axiom's phrasing was verified either; it is his, and it is undated.

**Takeaway:** Nothing important is new, and nothing is good for everything. The long nose says wait twenty years; the axiom says ask what it is worst for. [C35287]

## 23. Pen + touch, and the ear

Pen and touch are both direct input, but the pen works through a physical intermediary and touch does not, and that asymmetry is a strength for each. The division of labour advocated by Hinckley, Yatani, Pahud and colleagues (UIST 2010; SID 2010), with Buxton as co-author: the pen writes, touch manipulates, and the combination yields new tools. From their Surface prototype: hold a photo with touch and drag off with the pen to create and place a copy; hold it and cross it with the pen to slice it in two; hold photos and tap one with the pen to staple them together. Touch unifies selection with mode-switching for the pen, and the muscular tension of holding touch phrases the whole multimodal gesture together -- chunking again, tension as glue, now across two hands and two modalities. Earlier devices, the Palm Pilot of 1996, were pen or touch; the claim here is pen and touch. Hinckley is lead author on both papers; the "pen writes, touch manipulates" slogan is the group's, not Buxton's alone. The ear gets the same treatment in parallel. Buxton edited the 1989 special issue of Human-Computer Interaction on nonspeech audio, wrote the introduction "Using Our Ears" (1990), and co-ran the CHI '91 tutorial on non-speech audio with Gaver and Bly: nonspeech audio cues -- auditory icons and earcons -- can carry information in parallel with vision, including information the eye is not on, state changes, background events, mode. The attribution flag is printed on this one too: the SonicFinder, the canonical demonstration, a Macintosh Finder with auditory icons, is Gaver's (1989), not Buxton's; Buxton champions it, cites it, and builds the argument around it, but did not build it. Similarly, auditory icons as a concept are Gaver's, and earcons are Blattner and colleagues'. Buxton's mechanism is the advocacy and the framing: sound as a legitimate, parallel, information-bearing channel, and the three-mirrors point that a system using only the eye reflects only part of the body.

**Takeaway:** Two agents, visibly different in kind, each half a tool alone. The new tool appears only in the overlap -- and the ear is a channel, not a decoration. [C35288]

## 24. Sketching and the funnel

Two distinct jobs, in order. Getting the right design: explore many competing alternatives cheaply, early, while change is still affordable -- sketches are quick, timely, inexpensive, disposable, plentiful, and deliberately ambiguous ("leave big enough holes, like Swiss cheese") so imagination has room. Getting the design right: once the right design is chosen, refine it iteratively with usability engineering. The design funnel pictures this: a narrowing trapezium, the space of possibilities closing as decisions accumulate, against a rising arrow, the cost of change increasing. The 10+10 method: generate ten different concepts, reduce to the most promising, then generate ten variations of that one. Design is choice, and creativity lives in two places: enumerating meaningfully distinct options, and defining the criteria by which you choose. "Sketches are not prototypes": a prototype is expensive to abandon; a sketch is cheap to throw away, which is the point. The funnel diagram is adapted from Pugh (Total Design, 1990, page 75) -- Buxton says so; credit the funnel's shape to Pugh and its use in interaction design to Buxton. The follow-up is the Workbook (Greenberg, Carpendale, Marquardt and Buxton, 2011/2012), the how-to sequel, Buxton fourth author. The empirical backing is "Getting the Right Design and the Design Right: Testing Many Is Better Than One" (Tohidi, Buxton, Baecker and Sellen, CHI '06). The funnel is also the shape of this whole study's subject: Buxton spent his career arguing that the cheap sketch -- the 1983 watch, the 1984 tablet, the 1991 marking menu -- is where the future is decided, long before anyone calls it the future.

**Takeaway:** Breadth first, then depth, and the sketch is cheap to throw away on purpose. A piece that performs the funnel on itself -- ten rough variants, then one refined -- is the book, enacted. [C35289]

## 25. The four watches: Buxton on the AT-550

This is the doorway, and it deserves its own section, because the column is the best thing written about the watch and the watch is the best exhibit for the theory. "The Mad Dash Toward Touch Technology", BusinessWeek, Wednesday 21 October 2009, opens on a photograph of four watches from Buxton's collection. Watch (a) is the Casio AT-550. Watch (b) is the Casio TC-50 -- the black-plastic version of the TC-500 Touch Sensor Cal, not a misprint for TC-500, as #810 supposed; the TC-50 is a real model, and Buxton named the watch he owns. Watch (c) is a Tissot Touch. Watch (d) is a Casio Data Bank 150, with a physical mechanical keypad. Three of the four have touchscreen crystals, three have built-in calculators, and knowing how to use one buys you "pretty much nothing in terms of knowing how to use the other two". Buxton's sharpest observation about the AT-550 is not that it has a touchscreen. It is that the whole screen is used for entering each character, "thereby bringing the scale of the action well within the bounds of normal human finger motor control". Compare watch (b), where a keypad appears on the face and you must touch small virtual keys: "a victim of what happens when fat fingers meet small targets -- even when accompanied by high concentration. As for touch typing, forget it." The AT-550's trick is the opposite: because the gesture is the whole crystal, you do not have to look at what you are doing. Buxton calls this "heads up" data entry, "the equivalent of touch typing": "I can input numbers without diverting my gaze from you or from the document from which I am copying a number." And the arithmetic of the long nose: the character recognition was created using only one 131,072th of the computer power that would be found on an equivalently sized chip in 2009 -- not, as the brief had it, of the machine he was writing on. Two corrections to #810 ride inside this section, and both are printed here rather than hidden: the TC-50 is real, and the 1/131,072nd comparison is to a 2009 chip. Buxton also writes that Casio released the AT-550 "in 1984 for under $100"; #810 established 1983 as the birth year, with 1984 as the American introduction, so his 1984 is carried with its source, not repeated as settled fact. The column's verdict on the word "touch" is the sentence this study keeps: "Anyone who describes a product as having a 'touch interface' is likely unqualified to comment on the topic. The granularity of the description is just too coarse."

**Takeaway:** "Touchscreen" says almost nothing. The AT-550's touch is a whole-screen gesture you can do without looking -- and the watch is the long nose, worn on a wrist. [C35290]

## 27. The afterlife: students, Surface, and the influence line

The afterlife is already visible, and it runs through people more than products. ## Aesthetic logic

Buxton's world is a workbench, not a gallery: a row of devices, a hand in motion, a sketch that is cheap to throw away. The aesthetic of the study follows from that workbench. Everything is a state, a gesture, or a duration: the three states of a pointing device, the one unbroken stroke that is verb and object and destination, the twenty-year nose that makes slowness the content. The pieces this study feeds should be systems you can watch learn and watch wait: a form that is a ghost until it is tracked, tracked until it is dragged; a flick that leaves an ink trail and a menu that fades as the piece graduates from showing to knowing; a form that takes the full duration to emerge, its earlier versions visible inside its mature shape. The palette should be workbench-like -- paper, pencil, ink, the amber of old electronics, the black of a watch crystal -- and the interaction should be earned by the system, never pasted on: drag where the model earns dragging, hold where the mode earns holding, watch where the nose earns watching. Where the sources disagree, the disagreement is printed, not smoothed over: the year, the name, the title, the prefix, the size of the collection. Where a claim cannot be verified -- the manual that does not exist, the device never handled, the first use of a phrase -- the gap is named, not filled. Buxton collected devices to prove that the future is old; this study does not invent a fact to prove anything at all. [C35293]
