Schuette was born in Oldenburg on 16 November 1954, the second of five children of an engineer, the family moving every three years, his childhood spent in Bavaria and Lower Saxony; the family details rest on a single gallery CV mirrored by docslib, except for the Bavaria and Lower Saxony childhood, which the CaixaForum collection page corroborates. In 1970 he was twice German high-school champion in coxed fours rowing, as cox, and he hitchhiked alone through Europe every summer until 1975. In 1972 he visited documenta 5 in Kassel twice and began to draw; the visit is the hinge of his biography -- the CaixaForum and SMAK pages both say the documenta 5 visit at eighteen is what made him apply to art school the following year, and Studio International adds that there he encountered Sol LeWitt, Blinky Palermo and Daniel Buren. He entered the Kunstakademie Duesseldorf in 1973 and studied there until his graduation in 1981, alongside Katharina Fritsch, first in Fritz Schwegler's class and then with Gerhard Richter, with Daniel Buren and the art historian Benjamin H. D. Buchloh as further teachers; the teacher list is a four-way split by source (four teachers per English Wikipedia, two per German Wikipedia, three per Sotheby's), and when he joined Richter's class is itself split -- his own Tate account says 1976, the Museo Reina Sofia says 1975, and English Wikipedia dates self-portraits under Richter to 1975; the first-person 1976 is carried as the stronger source. Richter's teaching, as Schuette remembers it, was "friendly, but merciless": he never said a work was bad, but asked "Didn't you ever think about doing something else, other than art?" -- a question that kept a student awake for days. Schuette calls Richter "definitely the main influence on how I work": the lesson was that if you cannot continue in one direction you switch to another -- between problems, media or scale -- and that even with a limited field you can create a rich story if you work every day. In 1975-76 he did a year of community service in a retirement home in the countryside, and became friends with fellow students Thomas Ruff and Thomas Struth, who were studying photography with Bernd and Hilla Becher. He regards Grosse Mauer (Great Wall), 1977 -- overpainted panels resembling a brick wall -- as launching his independent artistic activity. He credits two people besides Richter for pulling him out of the art-school scene into the world: the gallerist Konrad Fischer and the curator Kasper Koenig, whom he met in 1979 during a six-month scholarship at the Cite des Arts in Paris; he had made his first three-month stay in New York in 1978. [C27880]

The first exhibitions come fast and are agreed across the CVs: a first show at Galerie Ruediger Schoettle, Munich, in 1980 (Arbeiten 1977-1980, 10 May-10 June), the Juergen Ponto Prize in Frankfurt the same year, and in 1981 graduation from the academy, first architectural models and stage scenery, invitation by Kasper Koenig to Westkunst in Cologne, and a first exhibition at Konrad Fischer in Duesseldorf (Plaene I-XXX, 17 October-14 November 1981). In 1982 he modelled his first Mann im Matsch (Man in Mud), followed by further versions until 1985, and from 1983 he shared a studio building at Hildebrandtstrasse 13/15 in Duesseldorf for five years with Klaus Jung, Ludger Gerdes and Wolfgang Luy. The gallery CV records a breakdown in 1983, "poisoned by lacquer paint and heavy drinking", and that he gave up painting for health reasons and stopped drinking in 1987 -- both details rest on that one CV and are carried as such. In 1984 he took part in Von hier aus - Zwei Monate neue deutsche Kunst in Duesseldorf (Messe Duesseldorf, 29 September-2 December), the show that also anchors his tie to Mucha, and in 1986 his first solo museum exhibition was at Museum Haus Lange, Krefeld (26 January-16 March), curated by Julian Heynen. In 1987 he built Kirschensaeule (Cherry Column) for Skulptur Projekte in Muenster (14 June-4 October), took part in documenta VIII in Kassel (12 June-20 September), and appeared in the four-person Foerg, Kiecol, Mucha, Schuette bill at Galleria Lia Rumma, Naples, and in Raumbilder at the Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofia, Madrid (8 April-22 June). The first US solo show followed at Marian Goodman Gallery, New York, in 1989 (Big Buildings, 10 March-8 April), the same year he began working with clay at the Niels Dietrich workshop in Cologne. In 1990 Ulrich Loock organised an overview of his work at Kunsthalle Bern (Sieben Felder, 27 January-11 March), which travelled to the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris and the Stedelijk Van Abbemuseum, Eindhoven. [C27881]

The 1990s run through the figure. His daughter Carla was born in 1994, the year he began drawing from real life and concentrating on figures and watercolours, with the solo show Figur travelling from Hamburg to Stuttgart and to Nimes; his son Henri was born in 1996. In 1992 he exhibited Die Fremden (The Strangers), his first group of life-sized ceramic figures, at documenta IX in Kassel (13 June-20 September); the group was later split, part remaining in Kassel and part standing on the roof of the Musik- und Kongresshalle in Luebeck. In 1995 he began the Kleine Geister (Little Spirits), made for a Lisson Gallery show in collaboration with Richard Deacon, and in 1996 the Grosse Geister (Big Spirits) in cast polished aluminium, later also in steel and bronze. He took part in Skulptur Projekte Muenster 1997 (22 June-28 September) and documenta X in Kassel (21 June-28 September) the same year, and in 1998 showed at the Whitechapel Gallery, London (16 January-15 March), travelling to De Pont, Tilburg, and the Serralves Foundation, Porto. The three-part Dia Center for the Arts survey followed in New York -- Scenewright (24 September 1998-13 June 1999), Gloria in Memoria (4 February-13 June 1999) and In Media Res (16 September 1999-18 June 2000), curated by Lynne Cooke -- for which most of the 120 Ceramic Sketches of 1997-99 and the first female figures in steel were made. The twenty works inspected run 1977-2012 and trace one practice at growing scale, then turning back on itself. Early, 1977-1987: systems and models -- a modular ring-set reset at every showing (Ringe, 1977), theatre sketches with toy figures and slogans (14 Skizzen zum Projekt Grosses Theater, 1980, with Princess Leia action figures before banners reading "Freedom", "In the Name of the People", "Status Quo"), a wax man who cannot stand (Mann im Matsch, 1982), a cherry column garnishing a car park (Kirschensaeule, 1987). The model is the work, and its status is deliberately uncertain: proposal for a real structure, thing in itself, or paradigm of human relations. Middle, 1988-1994: the figure takes centre stage -- small Fimo puppets in shirts and socks (Mohr's Life, 1988; Old Friends, 1992), bound pairs under bell jars (United Enemies, 1992-94), closed-eyed strangers with luggage (Die Fremden, 1992), big heads and masks (Drei grosse Koepfe, 1993; Masken, 1994), trios of hobos tied back to back in mud (the Respect trilogy, 1993-94), and a memorial that counts its dead on tabletops (Wo ist Hitlers Grab?, 1991) -- while the architectural models keep running alongside (Chinatown, 1989). Then, 1995-2006: the figure is monumentalised and the sketch is monumentalised with it -- wax spirals become eight-foot Geister (Grosse Geister, 1995-2004), one-hour clay sketches become eighteen Frauen on steel tables (Frauen, 1998-2006), each cast five times, twice in steel, twice in bronze, once in aluminium. Late, 2007-2012: the model returns at public scale -- a glass hotel on the Fourth Plinth (Model for a Hotel, 2007), an armless Father State (Vater Staat, 2010), bottle-capped warriors in charred wood (Krieger, 2012) -- and the newest work, Mutter Erde (2024), answers Vater Staat with the first standing female figure in the oeuvre, grown from a figurine found in a Dreikoenigskuchen. Grades: A 5, B 12, C 3 -- the five A grades are Mann im Matsch (1982/2009), Kirschensaeule (1987), Die Fremden (1992), United Enemies (1992-94) and Frauen (1998-2006); the three C grades are Notes (1989), Masken (1994) and Krieger (2012). Throughout, the same helical path the Art in America piece names: exploring images and configurations and letting them go, only to work them anew later. [C27885]

## M1 -- The failed hero / the anti-monument

The heroic figure is built and then denied the means of heroism: sunk to the knees in mud, bound in a robe with no arms, stuck back to back with his rivals, given a bottle cap for a helmet. The watchable behaviour is the monument failing in public: the viewer approaches expecting uplift and finds a little man in a short coat holding a dowsing rod, a tyrant who cannot lift his arms, warriors whose limbs are warped like burls. Seen in Mann im Matsch (1982/2009), Vater Staat (2010), Grosser Respekt (1993-94) and Krieger (2012).

**Takeaway:** build the hero, then take away his footing, his arms, or his cause; the failed monument is the monument. [C27886]

## M2 -- The scale jump: miniature model to monumental figure

A small, hand-made thing -- a wax man in a bucket, a pair of Fimo puppets under a bell jar, a one-hour clay sketch, wax cords twisted into spirals, wax warriors with bottle-cap helmets -- is enlarged to public-statue size without changing its nature. The watchable behaviour is the intimate made inescapable: the thumbprint, the squashed face, the crooked binding survive at four metres, so the monument keeps the hesitation of the sketch. Seen in United Enemies (1992 small pairs under 40 cm high, bound with cord, tape and medical sticking plaster under glass bell jars, enlarged in 2011 to monumental bronze pairs about four metres high), Mann im Matsch (1982 wax, 2009 nearly six-metre bronze in Oldenburg), the Frauen (120 Ceramic Sketches of 1997-99, an hour each, enlarged to steel-table bronzes), the Grosse Geister (Kleine Geister wax spirals to colossal aluminium) and Krieger (2012).

**Takeaway:** enlarge the sketch, not the idea; the monument must keep the fingerprint. [C27887]

## M3 -- The bound pair / the back-to-back trio

Two or three figures are tied, taped, roped or cast together so they cannot face each other, cannot move apart, and cannot stand alone. The watchable behaviour is forced company: each figure gazes elsewhere, struggles for a degree of autonomy, never looks at its mate -- the pair is a permanent situation, not a moment. Seen in United Enemies (eighteen pairs of small male figures, each under 40 cm high, formed from Fimo modelling clay, dressed in fragments of the artist's own clothes turned into dressing gowns, bound together with cord, tape and medical sticking plaster, displayed under glass bell jars on tall plinths like scientific specimens; the artist called the project "a definitive model for a permanent situation"), the Respect trilogy (trios back to back in mud, tar and red goo), the Janus head of Drei grosse Koepfe (one head, two faces, one looking slightly older than the other) and the two-faced 2011 bronzes.

**Takeaway:** tie them together and point them apart; the bond is the subject. [C27888]

## M4 -- The architectural model as proposal and shelter

Buildings are offered as small models -- villas, bird hotels, temples, museums, tribunes, holiday homes for terrorists, a hotel, a pavilion -- that are also sculptures in themselves, hovering between a proposal for a real structure and a paradigm of human relations. The watchable behaviour is the viewer leaning in to a small thing that claims a large space: the model invites entry through tiny doors, then withholds the interior; built at 1:1 it becomes a shelter you can actually enter, and at eight tonnes of glass it becomes a building that is only a picture of a building. Seen in the Westkunstmodelle and One Man Houses, Ferienhaeuser fuer Terroristen / Kreuzzug Modelle (2002-06, including Tanke Deutscheland, perspex, steel and wood, 172 x 170 x 220 cm), Model for a Hotel (2007, 21 storeys of red, yellow and blue glass, 5 x 4.5 x 5 m, over eight tonnes, Fourth Plinth, 7 November 2007-7 May 2008), the Krefeld Pavilion (2019-20) and the 1:1 models on the K21 dome.

**Takeaway:** offer the house as a model; the shelter that cannot be entered is the point. [C27889]

## M5 -- The head as character grammar

The face is modelled, squashed, gouged, glazed, photographed and enlarged as a unit of character, not likeness: heads are types, stretched forward, ploughed up with fingers, coated in parti-coloured glaze, dropped on a table and gilded, cast in Murano glass, printed huge from tiny clay originals. The watchable behaviour is the gaze that does not return: eyes closed (Die Fremden), eyes looking elsewhere (United Enemies), hollow sockets, Janus faces old and older -- the viewer reads character from distortion, not from resemblance. Schuette sought to convey "the grammar of the character" as opposed to mere representation of psychological states. Seen in Drei grosse Koepfe (1993, three 34-inch-high heads, types rather than likenesses of middle-aged males, stretched slightly forward, clay ploughed up with the fingers), Masken (1994), Innocenti (1994), the Wichte and Klotzkoepfe (2008), the Berengo glass heads (2011) and the Grosser Doppelkopf Nr. 8 (2017).

**Takeaway:** model the face until it stops being a portrait; character is in the distortion. [C27890]

## M6 -- The reclining body on the table

A female body, fragmented and lumpy, is laid on a steel table -- a workbench, a theatrical stage, an operating table -- headless, limbless, or with a handle in its thigh for easy lifting. The watchable behaviour is the body presented as material to be worked: the viewer circles a table the way a maker circles a bench, and the reclining nude, the oldest subject in sculpture, becomes a site for trying out form. Seen across the Frauen (1998-2006), in Aluminiumfrau Nr. 8 (2001, lacquered aluminium on a steel table, 127 x 267 x 124.8 cm, a shocking mutation of the reclining nude with an aquamarine and gold sheen), Bronzefrau Nr. 7 (2002, a headless, limbless torso unfurling on a vast steel plinth) and Torso (2005), a cross-section of a Grosse Geist.

**Takeaway:** lay the body on the workbench; the table turns the nude into a working problem. [C27891]

## M7 -- The stranger with luggage, eyes closed

A figure arrives with everything it owns -- sacks, urns, a suitcase, a shopping bag, a carpet-man's bundle -- and stands still, eyes closed, mouth sealed, head slightly bowed. The watchable behaviour is arrival without welcome: the figure is placed on high, on a portico or a department-store roof or a wooden pedestal, surveying a city it cannot enter, while the viewer below is surveyed in return. Seen in Die Fremden (1992, large figures, each accompanied by one or two sacks or urns, modelled in clay and colourfully glazed, arranged in three family groups; expressionless, eyes closed, caught in silent suspended animation; instead of legs, each figure incorporates a flowerpot-shaped base; first shown at documenta IX on the portico of the former Roten Palais, overlooking the city; the Luebeck group -- three figures and seven pieces of luggage on the roof of the Musik- und Kongresshalle, glazed terracotta up to 2 metres high and up to 300 kilograms -- was inspired by the Gulf War and the large influx of Kurdish refugees, and by the asylum debate of late 1991 and early 1992), Mann mit Koffer (1991) and Teppichman (1985).

**Takeaway:** give the stranger luggage and close its eyes; the arrival that never lands is the work. [C27892]

## M8 -- The memorial that counts

Grief and loss are tabulated rather than depicted: crosses stamped row after row on tabletops, urns and coffins as containers for the unnamed, graves named after singers. The watchable behaviour is counting as mourning: the viewer does the arithmetic -- 100,000 tables to finish the count -- and the scale of the loss arrives through the number, not through the image. Seen in Wo ist Hitlers Grab? (1991, six large tables and four drawings; rows and rows of crosses stamped on the light grey tabletops are but a start at tabulating the casualties of the Second World War), Gloria in Memoria (Dia, 1999), Untitled (Coffins) (1992-2026), Mein Grab and Urnen mit Seele (K21, 2026).

**Takeaway:** count the dead instead of picturing them; the unfinished tally is the memorial. [C27893]

## M9 -- Material promiscuity: the wrong material for the genre

Every classical genre is executed in a deliberately wrong material: monuments in glazed ceramic and Fimo; memorials in perspex and children's clay; portrait busts in Murano glass; heroic nudes in lacquered aluminium; warriors in charred wood with bottle-cap helmets; a hotel in coloured glass. The watchable behaviour is the genre wobbling: the viewer recognises the monument, the bust, the reclining nude -- and then the material refuses to behave, melting, glaring, squashing, shining, so the tradition is quoted and undermined in the same object. Seen in Die Fremden (fired clay for a public monument), United Enemies (Fimo and fabric under bell jars), the Grosse Geister (wax spirals cast in polished aluminium), the Frauen (one-hour clay sketches cast in steel, bronze and aluminium) and the glass heads.

**Takeaway:** cast the monument in the wrong stuff; the material's refusal is the critique. [C27894]

## M10 -- The sketch that talks back: watercolour as counter-voice

Drawings and watercolours run alongside the sculpture as a second, freer voice -- topical, funny, melancholic, sometimes flat -- and the sketch is treated as a counterpart and adversary to the model that emerges from it, not as a study for it. The watchable behaviour is the small sheet undercutting the big object: a sock problem, a slogan, a flower with a sentence written across it, a sunset closing an elegy of fifty sheets. Seen in Notes (1989, dual-language sketches and watercolours alluding to Germany's position in the out-of-synch world of post-Cold War politics: Altes Sock Problem, three mismatched socks hanging on a line; a map of the United States headed "Egologic" with "NO BOMBS - NO BULLETS - WE BUY" in block letters below), the 80 Skizzenbloecke (1990-95), Requiem (1992, fifty sheets for a dead friend and an expected child), the Deprinotes (2006-08) and the watercolour portraits hung beside the sculpture at the Serpentine in 2012.

**Takeaway:** let the watercolour contradict the bronze; the sketch is the adversary, not the draft. [C27895]

## Aesthetic logic

Schuette's logic is the monument taken apart and rebuilt by hand: classical genres -- the statue, the bust, the reclining nude, the memorial, the architectural model -- are modelled quickly in clay, wax, Fimo or watercolour, then cast, enlarged, glazed or built in the wrong material, and set where monuments belong -- on plinths, porticoes, squares and tables -- so that they fail, wait, or refuse to behave. The materials run from children's modelling clay and fabric to glazed ceramic, Murano glass, lacquered aluminium, rusted steel and patinated bronze; the scale runs from a five-centimetre wax man to a six-metre bronze and an eight-tonne glass hotel; and the titles do the naming work: a man in mud, strangers, united enemies, big respect, no respect, holiday homes for terrorists, father state, mother earth. The practice, as the galleries and museums describe it in agreement, subverts the language of sculpture with uncanny, subtly humorous work about power, memory, monumentality and the human condition; reconceives classical subjects and reflects on the legacies of modernism; and returns across decades to the same motifs, reworking them at new scales. Against Mucha's filed room -- found objects behind glass, counted and repeated -- Schuette's answer is the figure modelled by hand and then monumentalised: the cabinet is replaced by the bell jar, the archive by the pair, and the station by the square, the portico and the table. [C27896]
