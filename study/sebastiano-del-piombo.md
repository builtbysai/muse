# Sebastiano del Piombo: Study Notes

**Site:** Sebastiano Luciani, known as Sebastiano del Piombo (c.1485, probably Venice - June 1547, Rome; the day is disputed, Vasari says 14 June, the Wikipedia infobox says 21 June, and the current Britannica text says 21 July, which is almost certainly an error -- see C62), Italian painter of the High Renaissance and early Mannerism, famous as the only major artist of the period to combine the colouring of the Venetian school in which he was trained with the monumental forms of the Roman school. He began as a lutenist and singer in Venice, turned to painting late, around age 18-20 (c.1503-1505), trained with Giovanni Bellini and probably afterwards with Giorgione, and left no signed or firmly documented work from his Venetian period; in 1511 the Sienese banker Agostino Chigi brought him to Rome to decorate the Villa Farnesina, where he painted lunettes and a large Polyphemus (1512) beside Raphael's Galatea. By about 1515 he had allied himself with Michelangelo, who supplied drawings and designs for him to execute in oil and colour -- the Viterbo Pieta (c.1515-17), the Borgherini Chapel Flagellation (1516-24) and the Raising of Lazarus (1517-19), painted in direct competition with Raphael's Transfiguration for Narbonne Cathedral. After Raphael's death in 1520 he was Rome's leading painter, concentrated on portraits and religious easel paintings, survived the Sack of Rome in 1527 staying with Pope Clement VII, and in 1531 was appointed Keeper of the Papal Seal (piombatore), a lucrative office paying about 800 scudi a year, from which he paid 300 scudi a year as a pension to the rival claimant Giovanni da Udine; to hold it he took vows as a friar despite a wife and two children, and signed thereafter "F(rater) Sebastianus Ven(etus)". Output fell; he painted mostly portraits and slow devotional works, many for Spanish patrons, often on stone -- his posthumous inventory listed 46 paintings on stone against two on canvas and two on panel. His friendship with Michelangelo ended in 1534 over the Last Judgement: Sebastiano had the Sistine altar wall prepared for oil painting, Michelangelo furiously rejected it and had the wall re-plastered for fresco. He died in Rome in June 1547 after a short violent fever, was buried simply in Santa Maria del Popolo as his will directed, and his remains were moved in 1561 to the predecessor of the Accademia di San Luca. ## M1. Venetian Colorito Transplanted to Rome

What it IS: Sebastiano's core mechanism is oil painting in the Venetian manner -- rich, subtly varying colour built up in oil, with complicated pigment mixtures -- applied to Roman monumental subjects. He is described in every source as the only major artist of the period to combine the colouring of the Venetian school with the monumental forms of the Roman school. In the Raising of Lazarus he used a very wide range of pigments, often in complicated mixtures, aiming at "the greatest and most subtly varied range of colours ever seen in a single painting" (National Gallery Technical Bulletin, vol. 30, quoted in Wikipedia -- second-hand, the Bulletin was not read in full; see Q17). Technical examination found expensive, carefully manipulated materials: ultramarine in Christ's mantle, red lakes and other pigments in robes and flesh, and powdered glass in paint mixtures, probably to help the oil dry. Flesh was often built from darker foundations towards lighter passages. In several passages he shifts abruptly from one hue to another instead of modelling fabric only through lighter and darker tones of the same colour -- the acid yellow-green clothing near the centre of the Lazarus is the conspicuous example. How it works: oil allows slow blending, glazing, and reworking; mixtures are laid over a grey-brown priming (in the Lazarus), so colour sits on a dark, warm bed rather than on white gesso. Colour does the modelling work that line does in Florentine painting. The watchable behaviour is a crowd scene where every robe is a different hue and the eye is pulled across the surface by colour contrast, not by outline; flesh that glows out of a dark ground; a red robe (Christ's in the Lazarus) that has faded to pink with age, showing how much of the original effect depended on pigment that no longer survives. **Takeaway:** colour is the structure, not the decoration; build the image from hue changes, never from outlines.

## M2. Borrowed Monumental Design -- Michelangelo's Drawings as Armature

What it IS: a division of labour. Michelangelo supplied drawings -- sometimes a small design (piccolo disegno), sometimes studies for the principal figures, once (Viterbo Pieta) according to Vasari a full cartoon -- and Sebastiano translated them into painted, coloured, atmospheric pictures. Vasari's phrase for the Lazarus is "sotto ordine e disegno in alcune parti" (under the direction and in certain parts to the designs) of Michelangelo, a vague formula much discussed. Modern imaging shows the real sequence: Sebastiano began and developed the Lazarus composition himself, and Michelangelo's figure of Lazarus was painted over a different figure, presumably Sebastiano's own, at a relatively advanced stage (perhaps when Michelangelo visited Rome in January 1518). The help was limited to the main male figures; the crowd and landscape background were Sebastiano's composition. How it works: a sculptural, anatomically massive figure group -- Lazarus and his two attendants, Christ at the column -- is set inside a Venetian colour field. The drawn figure supplies the skeleton; the oil supplies the skin, air, and light. The seam between the two hands is visible in the underdrawing and in pentimenti. The watchable behaviour is figures that look carved rather than painted, with Michelangelesque bulk and twist, standing in a soft, cloudy, Venetian landscape with Roman ruins; a central body that seems to have been revised mid-painting, as if a second artist reached in and moved it. **Takeaway:** the mechanism is a two-author picture. The tension -- drawn monument inside painted atmosphere -- is the visible drama, not a hidden fact.

## M3. Oil on Plaster -- the Wall That Refuses Fresco

What it IS: Sebastiano's signature technical invention for walls: painting in oil on dry plaster instead of true fresco. The Borgherini Chapel Flagellation (San Pietro in Montorio, 1516-24) is painted in oil on plaster on a concave altar wall; the Transfiguration in the semi-dome above it is in fresco. Vasari records the recipe in detail: Sebastiano formed his intonaco with mastic and pitch from the pine, mixed carefully over fire, laid it on the wall, smoothed it with a trowel, and covered it with a plaster surface brought glowing from the fire. The same mixture was used on peperino, coloured stones, porphyry, and other hard surfaces. How it works: the hot resinous ground seals the wall against damp, so oil colours laid on it do not blacken the way earlier oil-on-wall attempts did. Vasari: Domenico Veneziano first attempted oil on wall, and Andrea del Castagno, Antonio and Piero del Pollaiuolo all failed to stop the blackening; only Sebastiano succeeded. The Flagellation "has never changed as yet, but retains its freshness and animation unimpaired as on the first day" (Vasari). The late Visitation fragments at Alnwick Castle were likewise oil on dry plaster, not true fresco. The watchable behaviour is a chapel wall with the depth, darkness, and slow modelling of an oil easel painting at fresco scale; colour that stays fresh for centuries where fresco would have dulled; a semi-dome in fresco above an oil wall below, two media in one chapel. **Takeaway:** the medium is the argument. Oil on a wall is a deliberate refusal of fresco's speed and matte surface -- a slow, dark, durable image where fresco wants a fast, light one.

## M4. Oil on Stone and Slate -- the Secret Ground

What it IS: from about 1530 Sebastiano became the great promoter of painting in oil on stone -- slate, marble, peperino, porphyry. He did NOT invent it (the Prado is explicit -- see C64), but he was the first to obtain successful results from 1530 onwards, and his method was considered secret. Laboratory analysis and reproduction showed the key: a mixture of hot oil, resins, and melted wax applied to the support, which made the oil paint adhere. His posthumous house inventory listed 46 paintings on stone, against just two on canvas and two on panel. Key works: the Ubeda Pieta (1533-39, oil on slate, 124 x 111 cm, 103 kg with frame, Prado), Christ Carrying the Cross (1535-40, oil on slate, Budapest), Portrait of Clement VII on slate (c.1531, Getty). How it works: the hot oil-resin-wax ground seals the impermeable stone; paint sits on a hard, dark, reflective bed. Slate's greyish chalky surface and tiny mica slivers produce a glowing effect that harmonises the image, making figures look as if emerging from the stone's interior; a wider chromatic range is possible with fewer pigments. In some works he left the background unpainted so the stone itself is the ground (the small head of Clement VII in Naples). In the Christ Bearing the Cross he used the striated mineral surface and dark background to create the illusion of bodies embedded in rock, and avoided bright reflections, "another of his obsessions" (Prado). The watchable behaviour is a heavy, cold, dark slab whose painted figures seem to rise out of the rock; flesh that shimmers because the ground beneath it glints; a picture that cannot be rolled or easily moved, whose weight is part of its presence; stone grain doing the work of a painted background. The Ubeda Pieta took seven years, partly because the support was a single fragile slab that later broke in the Spanish Civil War. **Takeaway:** the support is the subject. Stone gives the picture weight, darkness, and a claim to eternity -- "neither fire nor the worm could injure them" (Vasari) -- and the painting is an image growing out of rock.

## M5. Modelling from Darkness -- Dark Grounds, Sombre Late Colour

What it IS: a tonal mechanism that deepens across his career. Early Venetian works use Giorgionesque soft colour; Roman works are built on grey-brown priming; late works (after 1530) are "rather sombre, with touches of bright colour" (Wikipedia), and his late religious paintings for Spanish patrons are "more muted in colour and darker in tone" (Cavallini). The Viterbo Pieta is set in a twilight landscape; the Raising of Lazarus has darkened as its paint layers thinned over grey-brown priming and some pigments discoloured (greens turned dark brown, Christ's red robe faded to pink). How it works: forms are modelled by lifting light out of a dark ground rather than by shading a light ground. Flesh is built from darker foundations toward lighter passages. Bright colour is rationed -- a single acid yellow-green garment, a single red robe -- so it reads as an event inside the dark. The watchable behaviour is figures surfacing slowly out of shadow, as if the picture is still developing; a dark field in which one hot colour detonates; late works that feel like night scenes even when the subject is not nocturnal. **Takeaway:** darkness is the default state and light is the exception. A piece on this mechanism should start nearly black and earn every lit passage.

## M6. Two Kinds of Underdrawing -- Freehand Sketch vs Pricked Cartoon

What it IS: Sebastiano changed his drawing method when he moved from Venice to Rome. His early works use the Venetian technique of freehand underdrawing on the surface to be painted, following a relatively approximate sketch -- the method of the Kingston Lacy Judgement of Solomon, where after 1980s restoration three different compositions are visible, with more visible in infra-red reflectography. After some years in Rome he adopted the Florentine/Roman method of full-size cartoons, pricked along the lines and then pounced with soot through the holes to give dotted lines on the surface -- used in the fresco Transfiguration of the Borgherini Chapel, for which some pricked sheets survive. That was his last work in fresco. He preferred to draw on light blue paper in black chalk with white highlights, a Venetian habit. How it works: freehand underdrawing leaves the composition open to revision while painting; the pricked cartoon fixes the design before paint touches the wall. The Judgement of Solomon is the document of the first method: two versions of the elaborate architectural background, a painting abandoned about 1508 and reworked, 40 cm lost off the left edge, still over 2 x 3 metres. The watchable behaviour is a picture that contains its own earlier versions -- ghost architecture behind the final architecture, a figure moved, a composition re-thought in public; versus a wall painting whose figures sit exactly on their dotted transfer lines. **Takeaway:** the drawing method decides how much a picture can change while it is being made. Freehand underdrawing is a mechanism for revision; the pricked cartoon is a mechanism for fidelity.

## M7. Monumental Few-Figure Composition -- Emptiness as Structure

What it IS: Sebastiano's most original compositional device is the near-empty, geometrically severe arrangement of one or two huge figures. The Viterbo Pieta (c.1515-17) is the model: Christ lies across the bottom of the picture space at the feet of a Virgin looking up to Heaven, so the two figures do not actually touch -- highly unusual for the subject. Freedberg (quoted in Wikipedia): "an idea of high tragic power is expressed with extreme simplicity in a structure of severe geometric rigour." The late Alnwick Visitation fragments show the end point: over life-size main figures in "a style of impressive simplicity," the result of "a tendency to over-generalize appearances and pictorial structures so that they verged on an effect of geometrical abstraction," increasing since his early Roman years. His Christ Carrying the Cross (Budapest, 1535-40) is a single half-length figure on a solid dark background with no other figures at all. How it works: scale and spacing replace narrative detail. A body laid horizontally across the whole width of the panel; a Virgin who does not touch her son; a void between figures that carries the emotion. Colour and modelling are concentrated on very few forms, so each one is rendered with maximum weight. The watchable behaviour is a picture that feels monumental with almost nothing in it; the viewer's eye held by a single massive body and the empty space around it; grief expressed by distance between two figures rather than by gesture. **Takeaway:** subtraction is the composition. One body, one gap, one dark field -- and the geometry of the gap is the meaning.

## M8. Portraiture as the True Vocation -- Likeness and the Blacks

What it IS: Vasari insists portraiture was Sebastiano's proper vocation: "there was no difficulty in obtaining some portrait taken from the life from Sebastiano: this he did with tolerable ease and promptitude, but with anything appertaining to stories or other figures, it was altogether the reverse." Almost half his surviving paintings are portraits. He painted Clement VII (several versions, including on slate), Andrea Doria (1526), Vittoria Colonna, Pietro Aretino, Giulia Gonzaga, Cardinal Bandinello Sauli with secretaries and geographers (1516). His technical signature in portraiture is material truth: in the Aretino portrait Vasari counts "not less than five or six" different kinds of black -- velvet, satin, silk of Mantua, damask, and cloth, all black, with a very black beard finely distinguished on the sable clothing -- plus the head and hands "a sort of miracle," and the velvets, linings, and satins of the Albizzi portrait painted so that "it does not seem to be painted but living." How it works: a portrait is painted from life, in a single campaign, with the sitter present -- fast, direct, observational -- where a history painting is laboured over for years with drawings, cartoons, and revisions. The blacks are differentiated by paint handling and sheen, not by hue: matte cloth vs glossy satin vs deep velvet, each a different black. The watchable behaviour is a face that seems to breathe while the costume around it is a catalogue of textures; black on black that stays legible because each black reflects light differently; a sitter who looks out at the viewer (the Magdalene in San Giovanni Crisostomo, the Dorotea) with direct, unperformed attention. **Takeaway:** portraiture is the fast mechanism inside a slow practice. The picture is finished when the likeness lives -- and the proof is in the blacks, not the face alone.

## M9. Slow, Reworked, Unfinished Practice

What it IS: Sebastiano worked slowly, revised constantly, and left major works unfinished. Numbers from the sources: the Borgherini Chapel, 1516-24 with interruptions (see C72 on the six- versus eight-year figures); seven years on the Ubeda Pieta (1533-39); three years on the Piero Gonzaga portrait on stone; the Chigi Chapel Birth of the Virgin, contracted 1 August 1530 with a three-year deadline, still unfinished at his death in 1547 (see C73); the Santa Maria della Pace Visitation begun in the late 1530s and still half-finished at his death in 1547, then removed in the 17th century; the Birth of the Virgin finished after his death by Francesco Salviati. Vasari's verdict: "this man performed all that he did with infinite difficulty and most laborious pains-taking, and... no part of his work was effected with that facility with which Nature and study will sometimes reward those who delight in their vocation." How it works: slowness is visible as pentimenti, multiple compositions in one panel, and works that stop mid-thought. The Judgement of Solomon carries three compositions; the Lazarus carries a replaced Lazarus; the Alnwick Visitation survives only as fragments of a wall painting abandoned in place. Unfinishedness is not an accident of his career but its recurring condition. The watchable behaviour is a picture that visibly took years -- paint layered, revised, and left; a chapel that stays scaffolded for a decade; a masterpiece that exists only as three fragments in a country house. **Takeaway:** time is a material. The work records its own making -- revisions, delays, abandonments -- and the finished picture is only the last state of a long argument with itself.

## M10. The Piombo Office as a Throttle on Output

What it IS: in 1531 Clement VII gave Sebastiano the office of Keeper of the Papal Seal (piombatore): a sinecure paying about 800 scudi a year, requiring attendance on the pope most days, travel with him, and holy orders as a friar despite a wife and two children. The office gave him his name -- del Piombo, "of the lead," from the lead seals -- and changed his practice. Output fell; he painted mostly portraits and small devotional works, many on stone, many for Spanish patrons. Vasari presents this as moral decline into idleness and good living ("he gave himself up to his repose... if on any occasion he felt obliged to execute a painting, he went to his work with such manifest reluctance that he might have been supposed to be rather going to his death"), but modern scholarship notes the decline was "possibly not by as much as Vasari suggests" and that Vasari was heavily influenced by Michelangelo's hostility after 1534 (see C69). How it works: the courtier-painter works in short campaigns between court duties; supports get smaller, heavier, and more durable (stone slabs instead of chapel walls); subjects get more personal and emotional (late Spanish Pietas, Christ Carrying the Cross) and darker in tone; signatures change to "F(rater) Sebastianus Ven(etus)," marking the friar, not the Venetian. The office also explains the late portraits on slate: a prestige object a busy courtier can finish. The watchable behaviour is a career that visibly narrows -- from chapel walls and a 381 cm altarpiece to a 43 cm slate panel; from public competition with Raphael to private devotional objects for Spanish grandees; the same hand, slower, darker, and working in stone because stone waits. **Takeaway:** a sinecure is a technique. Economic security changed what Sebastiano painted, how big, on what, and for whom -- the office is visible in the work as shrinkage, darkening, and stone.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Venice, to 1511. Giorgionesque beginnings, no signed works. Sebastiano trained late: a lutenist first, he began painting only around 1503-05, aged 18-20, under Giovanni Bellini and then Giorgione ("si acconcio con Giorgione", Vasari). No signed or firmly documented Venetian work survives, and attributions are disputed across the whole early group -- works were long confused with Giorgione, and several remain divided between Giorgione, Titian, Sebastiano and lesser Venetians. What the early works show: freehand underdrawing; a smooth surface that develops into heavy brushstrokes (the organ shutters); elaborate architectural backgrounds in one-point perspective (Judgement of Solomon, where two versions of the background are visible); fullness of form and breadth of movement, possibly influenced by Fra Bartolommeo, who was in Venice in 1508; the St Sebastian figure showing awareness of classical sculpture (Apollo Belvedere). The San Giovanni Crisostomo altarpiece is compositionally innovative -- the saint reading at a desk in profile, Mary Magdalene looking out at the viewer -- and was later copied by Venetian painters including Titian. Small devotional panels and portraits dominate (Judith, Wise Virgin, Ceres); the only large early works are the Judgement of Solomon and the altarpiece. What changed within the phase: scale grows from small cassone panels and half-length devotional pictures (c.1505-08) to the large altarpiece and organ shutters (1508-11); brushwork becomes heavier; architectural settings become more elaborate and more Roman in feeling even before the move.

Phase 2 -- Early Rome: Chigi and the Farnesina, 1511-c.1515. Sebastiano left Venice in August 1511 in the entourage of Agostino Chigi, the richest man in Rome, who had fetched him after a six-month trip to Venice on business for Julius II. His first Roman works were the Farnesina lunettes and Polyphemus in the Sala di Galatea, finished by 27 January 1512, under Peruzzi's ceiling -- the walls were to hold a whole series of classical divinities, but only Sebastiano's Polyphemus and Raphael's Galatea (1514) were ever painted. What changed: immediate adaptation to the Roman style, above all to Michelangelo, whose Sistine ceiling had just been completed. The Death of Adonis is the key document: Freedberg calls it a "working dialectic of Roman and Venetian classical styles", its figures "enlarged... into an almost bulking massiveness, ponderous and sensuously splendid". Figures grow larger and more sculptural; the Venetian landscape softens into a hazy, distant backdrop (the view of Venice in the Death of Adonis). Portraits show Raphael's influence (the 'Fornarina' and the 'Dorotea' were both long attributed to Raphael), while Sebastiano's Venetian colour in turn influenced Raphael somewhat. He was painting portraits of cardinals and ambassadors (Carondelet, del Monte) within a year of arriving. What stayed constant: oil painting throughout; the half-length and three-quarter-length portrait format; the devotional single-figure subject; Venetian colour as the carrier of the picture, even as the drawing becomes more Roman.

Phase 3 -- The Michelangelo alliance and the Raphael rivalry, c.1515-1520. By about 1515 Sebastiano had befriended and allied himself with Michelangelo, who recruited him "as a kind of deputy for him in painting" to "contest Raphael's first place". Michelangelo was godfather to Sebastiano's son Luciano (born 1520). The alliance shaped the work directly: Michelangelo supplied drawings -- a cartoon for the Viterbo Pieta figures (Vasari; confirmed by technical examination showing Michelangelesque underdrawing transferred from a cartoon), a drawing for the Borgherini Flagellation (1516), and drawings for Lazarus and the two attendants in the Raising of Lazarus. The extent of dependence is unclear; direct supervision was likely slight, and Michelangelo left Rome in 1516, returning only occasionally. What changed: method. From freehand Venetian underdrawing to full-size pricked cartoons, pounced with soot (Borgherini Transfiguration, his last fresco). Technique: oil on plaster for the Flagellation -- Vasari says only Sebastiano prevented oil-on-wall colours from blackening. Scale: the Raising of Lazarus (381 x 289.6 cm) is his largest painting and the direct answer to Raphael's Transfiguration -- same size, same patron (Cardinal Giulio de' Medici), same destination (Narbonne Cathedral), both exhibited together in the Vatican in April 1520 after Raphael's death on 6 April 1520. Raphael was preferred; only Sebastiano's picture was sent to Narbonne. The Lazarus shows the adaptation to monumental Roman style "effectively complete", its colours retaining "Venice-like luminosity"; its composition shows much Raphael influence despite being conceived as a move against him. Michelangelo's help was limited to the main male figures; the crowd and landscape are Sebastiano's own -- and 2017 imaging showed the present Lazarus figure, based on Michelangelo's drawing, lies over a different figure designed by Sebastiano. The Vich triptych (1516) and the first Christ Carrying the Cross (c.1516) show the same monumental turn in devotional painting, and the Flagellation/Transfiguration pairing in the Borgherini Chapel is his most ambitious theological programme (Apocalipsis Nova reading: Flagellation = corrupted present, Transfiguration = glorious future). What stayed constant: Venetian colour -- the Lazarus used, per the National Gallery Technical Bulletin (2009), "the greatest and most subtly varied range of colours ever seen in a single painting"; the slow, deliberate working method (the Borgherini Chapel took years, interrupted by the Lazarus commission and by plague); the portrait practice running alongside the large religious works.

Phase 4 -- Middle Roman career, 1520-1531: leading painter in Rome. After Raphael's death in 1520 Sebastiano was "clearly the leading painter operating in Rome". He tried and failed to secure Raphael's Sala dei Pontefici (blocked by Raphael's workshop and by Michelangelo's refusal). He mostly avoided very large church commissions and concentrated on portraits -- in which he had a considerable reputation -- and on religious easel paintings (the Visitation for France, 1518-19; the Madonna of the Veil, c.1525, an adaptation of Raphael's Madonna di Loreto) in a "refined monumental classicism". What changed: a gradual loss of the Venetian style, more monumental forms, cooler colour (Cavallini). The plague of 1522 left little evidence of activity for over a year. Clement VII became pope in 1523; the Sack of Rome came in 1527 -- Sebastiano stayed with the pope through the retreat to Orvieto, spent time in Venice in 1528 and perhaps 1529 (his first return since 1511), and wrote in a 1531 letter: "I still don't feel I am the same Bastiano that I was before the Sack." Portraits become the dominant output (Aretino, Arsilli, Albizzi, the Vittoria Colonna group, Andrea Doria 1526 -- one of the earliest state portraits). Colour: he became less interested in colour as his career progressed; later works are sombre with touches of bright colour. What stayed constant: the portrait format and the single-figure devotional subject; the monumental, sculptural figure handling once Romanised; the slow pace -- the Viterbo Flagellation replica was still being finished in early 1525, and the Ubeda Pieta would take six years.

Phase 5 -- The piombo office and late works, 1531-1547. In 1531 Sebastiano was appointed piombatore (keeper of the papal seal), with a stipend of about 800 scudi plus a canonry at Torcello; he paid his rival Giovanni da Udine 300 scudi a year for the post. He had to take friar's vows despite a wife and two children, and his signatures become "F(rater) Sebastianus Ven(etus)". Output fell (Vasari overstates the decline): large single paintings took years -- the Ubeda Pieta, 1533-39, was the last piece with Michelangelo's help (a drawing) -- and after 1531 he painted mostly portraits. What changed: the support. Sebastiano was, per Vasari, the first to succeed in painting on stone -- peperino, marbles, mischi, porphyry, hard slabs -- and slate became his favoured support for important commissions because he believed it lasted longer. His posthumous inventory listed 46 paintings on stone, 2 on canvas and 2 on panel. The late religious paintings were mainly for Spanish patrons (the Ubeda Pieta for Cobos, the Hermitage Christ Carrying the Cross for Cifuentes, the Vich triptych already in Spanish hands); they are more personal and emotional, more muted in colour and darker in tone, and they influenced later Spanish artists, Luis de Morales and Francisco Ribalta. The friendship with Michelangelo ended in 1534 over the Last Judgement: Sebastiano urged oil on plaster, the wall was prepared, Michelangelo furiously rejected it and had the wall re-plastered in arriccio for fresco -- "oil painting was an art for women and for leisurely and idle people like Fra Sebastiano". The late style shows a "tendency to over-generalize appearances and pictorial structures so that they verged on an effect of geometrical abstraction", increasing since early Rome; the Santa Maria della Pace Visitation fragments have an "impressive simplicity". Two late church projects were unfinished at his death: the Birth of the Virgin (600 x 350 cm on peperino stone, finished by Salviati) and the Santa Maria della Pace Visitation. He died in June 1547 and was buried in Santa Maria del Popolo; his remains were moved in 1561 to the Accademia di San Luca. Almost half his surviving paintings are portraits (Vasari: portraiture was his greatest talent); he had no significant pupils, and his influence was limited by the small number of print copies after his work. What stayed constant to the end: oil painting; the monumental, sculptural figure; the portrait as the core of his practice; the slow, deliberate method -- the Ubeda Pieta took six years, the Birth of the Virgin was still unfinished after seventeen.

What changed, what stayed constant: what changed most is the support and the scale of ambition. Sebastiano begins in small Giorgionesque devotional panels with no signed work, grows in Rome into the largest altarpiece of his career (the Lazarus, 381 cm wide) painted against Raphael himself, and ends in small, heavy, dark pictures on slate for Spanish grandees -- the career narrows from chapel walls to a 43 cm slate panel. The drawing method changes with the city: freehand Venetian underdrawing gives way to pricked Roman cartoons, and the design itself is borrowed for twenty years from Michelangelo, whose drawings supply the monument while Sebastiano's oil supplies the skin. Colour cools steadily: the widest, most subtly varied range of colours ever seen in a single painting (the Lazarus, 1517-19) gives way to sombre late works with touches of bright colour rationed like events. The patron moves too: Venetian churches, then Chigi and the papal court, then Spanish secretaries of state -- and finally the papal seal office itself, which pays him to paint less. What never changes is the oil medium -- he never returns to true fresco after the Borgherini Transfiguration, and he avoids large fresco schemes once established; the monumental, sculptural figure once Romanised; the portrait as his home territory, done fast and from life while history painting costs him years; and the slow, deliberate method that leaves pentimenti, replaced figures, and unfinished chapels as the visible record of its own making. The late pictures look like a different painter only until you see that the Viterbo Pieta of c.1515 already holds its grief in the gap between two figures who do not touch; the late style is the early subtraction carried to its limit, with the colour finally surrendered to the dark and the stone.

## Aesthetic Logic

The logic is that one Venetian in Rome can do what no Roman can: put Venetian colour inside a Roman monument. The Raising of Lazarus is the proof and the programme -- a sculptural figure group drawn by Michelangelo set inside a soft, cloudy Venetian landscape with Roman ruins, every robe a different hue, the widest range of colours ever seen in a single painting, all of it built over a grey-brown priming so the colour glows out of darkness. The contest with Raphael is the same logic in public: same size, same patron, same destination, exhibited together -- and the Roman verdict preferred Raphael's grace, because Sebastiano's picture is a fusion and fusions always show their seam. The seam is the point: the present Lazarus lies over a different figure designed by Sebastiano, the borrowed design painted over the painter's own. Then the logic turns inward. The Borgherini Chapel puts oil on plaster below and fresco above in one room, because the medium is the argument -- oil is slow, dark, and durable where fresco is fast and matte, and Sebastiano would rather fight the wall than submit to it. The Viterbo Pieta subtracts everything but two figures and the gap between them, because the monument is the body and the void, not the narrative. And the piombo office finishes the logic by economics: a sinecure pays the painter to paint less, so the pictures shrink, darken, and move onto stone -- a support that cannot be rolled, cannot be hurried, and claims to outlast fire and worm. The late Spanish Pietas are the end of the same road: personal, emotional, muted, dark, made for private devotion rather than public contest. The pictures were made to be the one Venetian thing in a Roman room -- colour where Rome wanted line, oil where Rome wanted fresco, stone where Rome wanted canvas -- and they still want to be seen that way: as the foreign element that refuses to assimilate, and is monumental because it refuses.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] Sebastiano is the only major artist of the period to combine Venetian colouring with Roman monumental form. Sources: Wikipedia lead; Britannica lead ("tried to combine the rich colours of the Venetian school with the monumental form of the Roman school"). Evidence: leg 1.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] His birth year c.1485 is an extrapolation, not a document: it rests only on Vasari's statement that he was 62 at his death in 1547, and his birthplace (probably Venice) and family background are uncertain. Sources: Wikipedia (Venice section); Cavallini to Veronese. Evidence: leg 1.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] Michelangelo's contribution to the Raising of Lazarus was limited to the main male figures and came late: imaging shows Sebastiano's own Lazarus underdrawing beneath the figure based on Michelangelo's drawing, and the crowd and landscape are Sebastiano's. The National Gallery's credit line is precise: "Sebastiano del Piombo, incorporating designs by Michelangelo." Sources: Wikipedia (Lazarus article); National Gallery object page; thehistoryofart.org via search. Evidence: leg 1.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] Raphael won the Narbonne contest in Roman critical opinion. Vasari: both pictures were "vastly extolled," but "the works of Raffaello had no equals in their perfect grace and beauty." Only the Sebastiano was sent to Narbonne; the Raphael stayed in Rome. Sources: Vasari; Wikipedia (Lazarus article). Evidence: leg 1.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] Sebastiano did NOT invent painting on stone. Vasari associated the origins with him, but the Prado states he "did not in fact invent the technique" -- he promoted it and was "the first to obtain successful results from 1530 onwards." Source: Prado, In Lapide Depictum. Evidence: leg 1.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] The piombo office (1531) reduced his output, but Vasari overstates the idleness. Wikipedia: output reduced "though possibly not by as much as Vasari suggests"; Vasari's account was "probably much influenced by Michelangelo" and his hostility after 1534. Sources: Wikipedia (1532-1547); Vasari. Evidence: leg 1.
- P7. [FROM RECORD] The friendship with Michelangelo ended in 1534 over the Last Judgement oil-vs-fresco dispute, after nearly twenty years of closeness (c.1515-1534). Michelangelo was godfather to Sebastiano's son Luciano (1519 per leg 1; born 1520 per leg 2 -- see C78). Sources: Wikipedia (Personal life; 1532-1547); Vasari. Evidence: leg 1.
- P8. [FROM RECORD] His late works are darker, more muted, more personal and emotional, mainly for Spanish patrons, and influenced later Spanish painters Luis de Morales and Francisco Ribalta. Sources: Cavallini to Veronese (intro); Prado (Spanish commissions). Evidence: leg 1.
- P9. [FROM RECORD] Almost half his surviving paintings are portraits, and Vasari calls portraiture his true vocation; he painted them with "tolerable ease and promptitude" while history painting cost him "infinite difficulty." Sources: Wikipedia; Vasari; Cavallini to Veronese. Evidence: leg 1.
- P10. [FROM RECORD] He avoided large fresco schemes once established, working in oils; his last fresco was the Transfiguration in the Borgherini Chapel semi-dome, and the Flagellation below it is oil on plaster. The one fresco cycle he began in Venice (Fondaco dei Tedeschi area) is lost. Sources: Wikipedia (intro; Technique); Cavallini to Veronese (Borgherini Chapel). Evidence: leg 1.
- P11. [FROM RECORD] The Raising of Lazarus is his largest painting and his most complete fusion: 381 x 289.6 cm (National Gallery figure; see C68), same size, same patron and same destination as Raphael's Transfiguration, exhibited together in the Vatican in April 1520 after Raphael's death on 6 April 1520; Raphael was preferred. Sources: leg 2 (works table entry 35); National Gallery object page; Wikipedia (Lazarus article). Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P12. [FROM RECORD] The Borgherini Chapel pairs oil and fresco in one room: the Flagellation in oil on plaster below (1516-24), the Transfiguration in fresco above in the semi-dome -- his last fresco, with a full-size pricked cartoon pounced with soot. Sources: leg 2 (works table entries 33-34); Vasari; Cavallini to Veronese. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P13. [FROM RECORD] His posthumous inventory listed 46 paintings on stone against 2 on canvas and 2 on panel -- stone is not a late experiment but the dominant late support. Sources: leg 1 (M4); leg 2 (medium counts); Cavallini to Veronese. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P14. [FROM RECORD] The piombo office paid about 800 scudi a year, of which 300 went to Giovanni da Udine; the appointment required friar's vows despite a wife and two children, and his signatures changed to "F(rater) Sebastianus Ven(etus)". Sources: leg 1 (M10); leg 2 (Phase 5); Cavallini to Veronese; Wikipedia. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P15. [FROM RECORD] After Raphael's death in 1520 he was Rome's leading painter, yet he failed to secure Raphael's Sala dei Pontefici, blocked by Raphael's workshop and by Michelangelo's refusal. Sources: leg 2 (Phase 4); Cavallini to Veronese. Evidence: leg 2.
- P16. [FROM RECORD] The friendship with Michelangelo lasted about twenty years (c.1515-1534) and broke over technique, not taste: Sebastiano prepared the Sistine altar wall for oil; Michelangelo had it re-plastered for fresco. Sources: leg 1 (M10, P7); leg 2 (Phase 5); Vasari. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P17. [FROM RECORD] His late religious paintings were mainly for Spanish patrons (Cobos, Cifuentes, Vich) and influenced Luis de Morales and Francisco Ribalta. Sources: leg 1 (P8); leg 2 (Phase 5); leg 3 (N72-N77). Evidence: legs 1, 2 and 3.
- P18. [FROM RECORD] The Judgement of Solomon carries three compositions and two versions of its architectural background, abandoned about 1508 and reworked -- the document of his freehand Venetian underdrawing method. Sources: leg 1 (M6); leg 2 (works table entry 3); Wikipedia. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P19. [FROM RECORD] He had no significant pupils, and his influence was limited by the small number of print copies after his work. Sources: leg 2 (Phase 5); Wikipedia. Evidence: leg 2.
- P20. [INFERRED -- parent] The road not taken: had Sebastiano stayed in Venice he would have been Titian's lifelong rival in the same market; going to Rome made him the only Venetian colourist in the city that set the terms of monumental painting, and that scarcity -- one Venetian in Rome -- is the whole of his historical position. The inference follows from P1, P3 and P15, but no source states it in this form.

## Load-bearing corrections

- C62. DEATH DATE -- THREE VERSIONS. Vasari says Sebastiano died 14 June 1547. Wikipedia's infobox says 21 June 1547. Encyclopaedia Britannica says "died July 21, 1547" -- the July date is almost certainly an error in Britannica's current text (no other source gives July). The study gives June 1547 and does not state any single day as certain. (Leg 1, C1.)
- C63. BIRTH YEAR -- c.1485 is an inference, not a fact. It rests solely on Vasari's "62 at death." Some older sources state 1485 flatly; the "circa" must stay. (Leg 1, C2.)
- C64. INVENTOR OF PAINTING ON STONE. Vasari credits Sebastiano with discovering the method; the Prado's research says he did not invent it but promoted it and first got successful results from 1530. The study uses the Prado's correction. (Leg 1, C4.)
- C65. FLAGELLATION AUTHORSHIP. Vasari says Michelangelo made the small design and possibly even the outline of the Christ figure ("many believe Michelagnolo to have made, not the small design for this work only, but even the outline of that figure"). Modern scholarship: Michelangelo supplied drawings for the principal figures; the extent of his direct involvement is unclear and "any direct supervision... seems likely to have been slight" (Cavallini). Do not credit the Flagellation's Christ to Michelangelo flatly. (Leg 1, C5.)
- C66. SAN GIOVANNI CRISOSTOMO ATTRIBUTION. Vasari attributed the altarpiece to Giorgione in the first (1550) edition of the Lives and to Sebastiano in the second (1568) edition. Attribution of Venetian-period works between Giorgione and Sebastiano remains contested; the Judgement of Solomon was long attributed to Giorgione. (Leg 1, C6.)
- C67. PIETA DATING. Britannica dates the Viterbo Pieta "c.1517"; Wikipedia and Cavallini place it c.1515-1516; recent dating puts it as early as 1513. Do not fix a single year. (Leg 1, C3; leg 2, entry 26.)
- C68. DIMENSION CONFLICTS. Fifteen of the 74 works entries carry conflicting dimension figures, and the study does not reconcile them: Judgement of Solomon three ways (175.3 x 289.6 National Trust, preferred, vs 208 x 315 Cavallini vs 208 x 318 artsdot); Viterbo Pieta three ways (259 x 219 Cavallini vs 270 x 225 secondary sources vs 247 x 187 Italian national catalogue); Andrea Doria three ways (125 x 94 Cavallini vs 153 x 107 Wikipedia infobox vs 150.5 x 103.3 Musei di Genova) with the location also in conflict (Doria Pamphilj, Rome, preferred, vs Villa del Principe, Genoa); Raising of Lazarus 381 x 289.6 (National Gallery, preferred) vs 381 x 299 (Wikipedia infobox, likely conflated with Raphael's Transfiguration, also cited as 381 x 299); Martyrdom of St Agatha 132 x 178 (Uffizi, preferred) vs 127 x 178 vs 131 x 175; Cardinal Bandinello Sauli 121.8 x 150.4 (Kress/NGA, preferred) vs 154 x 182 (wahooart/wikioo); Ubeda Pieta 124 x 111.1 (Prado, preferred) vs 120 x 107; and eight further small conflicts listed in leg 2. Several dimensions rest on secondary or reproduction sites only (wahooart, artsdot, allpaintingsstore, wikioo); museum or primary sources are preferred and named wherever they supplied the figure. (Leg 2, Conflicts.)
- C69. VASARI AS A SOURCE. Vasari knew Sebastiano but probably not well; his Life is heavily influenced by Michelangelo's hostility after 1534 and by the moralising frame of the industrious artist corrupted by a sinecure. His anecdotes (the idleness, the good table, the replies in Q2-Q3) are reported speech and partisan judgement, not neutral record. Wikipedia and modern scholarship both flag this. The study presents Vasari's judgements as Vasari's, not as facts. (Leg 1, C10.)
- C70. WEEKDAYS BEFORE 1582. No weekday is asserted for any 16th-century date in this study. Pre-1582 dates are Julian, and `date -d` computes proleptic Gregorian weekdays, so a weekday produced that way is not the weekday of the historical date. The #514 study's weekday claims (e.g. "Friday 27 August 1576" for Titian's death) are proleptic-Gregorian computations of the same kind and carry the same caveat; the parent re-ran 21 June 1547 and 14 June 1547 with `date -d` in America/New_York and both return Saturday in the proleptic Gregorian calendar, which is why no weekday is asserted here. (Parent.)
- C71. THE #513/#514 MICHELANGELO AND RAPHAEL HOLD, LIFTED FOR THIS LEG. At #513 Michelangelo and Raphael were held as "formation masters, held for a later leg"; at #514 they were held again as "too broad and central-Renaissance". That hold was right for Veronese and Titian, whose Michelangelo link is background influence. For Sebastiano it no longer holds: the Michelangelo alliance is the engine of his Roman career, a documented twenty-year design-supply partnership with a named rupture, and the Raphael link is a named two-picture contest for one patron and one destination. Michelangelo is therefore the top pick for #516 and Raphael is main entry 1 in the New from #515 block; Parmigianino, held on the same grounds, stays a reserve for that reason. (Parent, from leg 3.)
- C72. BORGHERINI CHAPEL -- SIX YEARS VS EIGHT YEARS. Leg 1 (M9) says six years on the Borgherini Chapel (1516-1524, with interruptions); leg 2 (Phase 3) says the chapel took eight years, interrupted by the Lazarus commission and by plague. 1516 to the 24 March 1524 unveiling is eight calendar years; the six-year figure counts working years with the interruptions removed. The study states the span 1516-1524 with interruptions and does not assert a single figure. (Parent.)
- C73. CHIGI CHAPEL BIRTH OF THE VIRGIN -- TEN YEARS VS SEVENTEEN. Leg 1 (M9) says ten years with the Chigi Chapel in a state of preparation only; leg 2 (entry 73) records a contract dated 1 August 1530 with a three-year deadline, work begun in the late 1530s, and the picture still unfinished at Sebastiano's death in 1547. Leg 1's ten-year figure is not reconciled with either the contract date or the seventeen-year span from contract to death; the study states the contract date and that the work was unfinished at his death, without asserting a single span. (Parent.)
- C74. THE COLUMBUS PORTRAIT DATE. The Metropolitan portrait is inscribed 1519, but Hirst suggested the sitter might be a cleric present at Bologna in 1530 for the coronation of Charles V, which would make 1519 impossible; the identity is unresolved. (Leg 2, entry 39.)
- C75. SALOME. No separate securely attributed "Salome with the Head of John the Baptist" was found; the National Gallery "Judith (or Salome?)" (NG2493, 1510) is the closest match and its title is disputed. (Leg 2, entry 10.)
- C76. NOT VERIFIED DIMENSIONS. Four of the 74 entries have no verified dimensions: the Farnesina lunettes and the two Borgherini Chapel wall paintings (wall paintings, where a canvas-style dimension does not apply) and the 1540s Portrait of a Lady (National Gallery L1059). The San Giovanni Crisostomo altarpiece dimensions (200 x 156) rest on Cavallini and reproduction sites only; no museum record was retrieved. The Fitzwilliam Virgin and Child roundel dimension ("66 in dia") is recorded as stated in the source and is unusually large for a roundel; treat with caution. The January 1525 Ecce Homo rests on a single auction source (Dorotheum, sale 23 October 2025); its attribution and identification are recent. (Leg 2.)
- C77. LEG 3's WIKIPEDIA READ. Leg 3 read the English Wikipedia Sebastiano del Piombo article to line 452 of 487; legs 1 and 2 read it in full (487 lines). The article's references tail (lines 453-487) was read by no leg line by line. (Parent.)
- C78. LUCIANO'S BIRTH YEAR -- 1519 VS 1520. Leg 1 (P7) says Michelangelo was godfather to Sebastiano's son Luciano in 1519; leg 2 (Phase 3) says Luciano was born in 1520. The sources differ and the legs do not reconcile them; the study records both and does not assert a single year. (Parent.)
- C80. THE #514 BLOCK'S CARRIED ENTRIES.
