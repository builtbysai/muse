## 1. Childhood, illness, and the infirmary

Lambert was born in Fulham on 23 August 1905, the younger son of the Australian painter George Washington Thomas Lambert and Amy Lambert, and the younger brother of the sculptor Maurice Lambert, born in Paris. The family was bohemian and hand-to-mouth: the parents had arrived in England in 1900, lived in France until the money ran out, and were back in London by 1902. The father left for Australia when Constant was about fifteen, and the desertion stayed with him. He was a delicate child, and the delicacy turned into a catalogue: near-fatal septicaemia in 1916, appendicitis, double mastoid, roseola infantum, osteomyelitis, repeated operations. He was left permanently deaf in one ear, lame for life, and in semi-permanent pain. The medical trauma left him with a lifelong fear and detestation of doctors, which is the mechanism behind the manner of his death: the diabetes that killed him went undiagnosed and untreated for years because he would not see a doctor. He spent long spells isolated in the school infirmary at Christ's Hospital, where he attended from 1915 to 1922, and the isolation is where the musician was made: he read voraciously, began composing, drawing, writing poetry, and became obsessed with ballet while his schoolfellows read cricket scores. He was taken to see the Ballets Russes as a schoolboy. He wrote his first orchestral work at thirteen. The infirmary is the first of Lambert's mechanisms, though it is not a musical one: enforced stillness, turned into study. Everything later -- the magpie reading, the arranging, the criticism -- is the infirmary habit continued into adult life.

**Takeaway:** the body broke early and stayed broken, and the isolation it forced became the method -- read everything, write everything, and let the theatre be the place the reading comes out. [C34467]

## 2. The Royal College of Music, 1922-1926

He entered the Royal College of Music in September 1922, a few weeks after his seventeenth birthday, on a scholarship. His teachers were Ralph Vaughan Williams and R. O. Morris for composition, Sir George Dyson for composition, Malcolm Sargent for conducting, and Herbert Fryer for piano. He admired Vaughan Williams and did not emulate him; he loathed Dyson. His contemporaries included Angus Morrison, Guy Warrack, Thomas Armstrong, Gavin Gordon, Patrick Hadley and Gordon Jacob. Vaughan Williams introduced him to Edward J. Dent, who taught him classical reserve and proportion and later collaborated with him on a modern edition of Purcell's The Fairy Queen; Dent called him in 1946 "the best all-round musician we have in this country." His tastes were already fixed by the time he left, and they never moved: he respected Schoenberg and especially Berg, loathed Stravinsky, preferred Satie to Debussy, thought Sibelius the greatest symphonist since Beethoven, had no interest in folksong, and adored jazz for its virtuosity and its instrumental sound. The thesis that later scholarship gives his career -- student years 1922-26, jazz period 1927-31, mature period 1932-51 -- starts here, with a student who already knew what he liked and, more importantly, what he despised. The despising mattered as much as the liking: the hostility to Stravinskian neoclassicism runs through Music Ho! and through every arrangement he made afterwards.

**Takeaway:** the RCM gave him a teacher he admired without imitating, a mentor who taught him proportion, and a set of hatreds that stayed fixed for life -- the taste was formed before the career began. [C34469]

## 3. Prodigy, Diaghilev, and the first ballets

The prodigy years run 1923 to 1926, and they are dense. Prize-fight, a satirical one-act ballet about a boxing match, was written in 1923-24; Mr Bear Squash-You-All-Flat, a narrated piece for eight players after a Russian children's tale nobody has ever traced, was completed in June 1924, two months before his nineteenth birthday, and was not performed until 1979. The Piano Concerto for piano, two trumpets, timpani and strings was written in 1924 and left in short score, unperformed in his lifetime. The Bird Actors overture came in 1925. He met the Sitwells as a schoolboy, turning up at their Carlyle Square door with a letter of introduction, and met Walton there; Walton dedicated Facade to him, he contributed a few measures, and he took over reciting Edith Sitwell's verses after remarking "You ought to have had me reciting it." At twenty he went with Walton to see Diaghilev, played his ballet Adam and Eve, and Diaghilev liked it but insisted on retitling it Romeo and Juliet -- "one could hardly expect Madame Karsavina to appear as Eve. A married lady!" The ballet, choreographed by Bronislava Nijinska, with Karsavina as Juliet and Lifar as Romeo, premiered in Monte Carlo on 4 May 1926: the first work by a British composer staged by the Ballets Russes, and Lambert and Lord Berners were the only two British composers Diaghilev commissioned in his lifetime. The premiere was fraught -- Diaghilev altered Nijinska's choreography, rejected Christopher "Kit" Wood's sets for Max Ernst and Joan Miro, Lambert at twenty threatened to withdraw the music, and the Paris staging two weeks later met a demonstration against Ernst and Miro. Pomona followed in 1926, again choreographed by Nijinska, again reusing Adam and Eve material. He later had a famous falling-out with Diaghilev; no source read gives the details, so the study does not elaborate it. The point of these years is not the success -- Romeo and Juliet was "hardly a success" -- but the launch: the Diaghilev commission led directly to the Camargo Society and the Vic-Wells conducting posts, and the twenty-year-old who threatened to withdraw his score from Diaghilev is the same man who would later run a national ballet's music.

**Takeaway:** the prodigy was not a composer who happened to meet Diaghilev but a theatre animal who used the commission to get inside the company -- and the company, not the concert hall, is where he stayed. [C34471]

## 4. The jazz years and The Rio Grande, 1927-1931

The jazz period is four years, 1927 to 1931, and it produced the work he is remembered by. In June 1923 he heard Will Vodery's Plantation Orchestra at the London Pavilion, with Johnny Dunn on trumpet, and wrote later that it was "electrifying... It definitely opened up a new world of sound." The Rio Grande, setting Sacheverell Sitwell's poem, was written in 1927 for alto soloist, mixed chorus, solo piano, brass, strings and fifteen percussion instruments played by five players, with no woodwind at all. It was first broadcast by the BBC on 27 February 1928, first performed in concert in Manchester on 12 December 1929 with Hamilton Harty at the piano and Lambert conducting, and given its London premiere the next day at Queen's Hall. It was an instant and lasting success, his most performed work, and he recorded it as conductor twice, in 1930 and 1949. Around it cluster the works that show the idiom spreading: Elegiac Blues, written in 1927 in memory of Florence Mills, the Blackbirds singer-dancer who died that November aged thirty-one; Music for Orchestra, dedicated to Lord Berners, first performed 14 June 1929; Eight Poems of Li-Po, worked on from 1926 to 1929 and dedicated to Anna May Wong, the Chinese-American film star he was infatuated with from afar until she came to London in March 1929 and the hoped-for romance failed; the Piano Sonata, composed 1928-29 and first performed 30 October 1929; and the Concerto for Piano and Nine Instruments, composed 1930-31 and first performed 18 December 1931, dedicated to the memory of Philip Heseltine, Peter Warlock, who died on 17 December 1930 -- almost a year to the day before the premiere. 1930 was a year of deaths: his father George in May, Christopher "Kit" Wood in August, Heseltine in December. He married Florence Kaye on 5 August 1931; their son Kit Lambert, born 1935 and named after Christopher Wood, later managed the Who. The jazz period ends where the institution begins: the Concerto is the last work in which jazz is the whole language, and after it jazz is absorbed into structure, then miniaturised, then remembered.

**Takeaway:** four years, one hit, and a cluster of deaths that turned the idiom from a style into a structure -- the jazz never leaves, but after 1931 it stops being the surface and starts being the engine. [C34473]

## 5. The Vic-Wells founding and the composing stall, 1931-1939

In 1930 he conducted the Camargo Society. In 1931 he joined de Valois and Ashton to form the Vic-Wells Ballet, which gave its first full-evening performance at the Old Vic on 5 May 1931, and he was appointed its music director the same year -- the 1935 date that appears in the de Valois article is the consolidation at Sadler's Wells, not the appointment, and this study uses 1931 throughout. He held the post until his resignation in 1947. The appointment is the hinge of his life, and it is a hinge that closes as much as it opens: his composing output collapsed under the ballet duties. Music Ho! was published in 1934. Summer's Last Will and Testament, composed 1932-35 with help from Hyam Greenbaum on the choral parts, was premiered at Queen's Hall on 29 January 1936, six days after George V's death, sparsely attended, in a national mood hostile to an hour of plague and death; Lambert concluded he had failed as a composer, and he completed only two major works in the remaining sixteen years of his life. The rest of the decade is arrangement, and the arrangement is the work: Les Rendez-vous after Auber in 1933, Bar aux Folies-Bergere after Chabrier in 1934, Apparitions after Liszt in 1936, Les Patineurs after Meyerbeer in 1937 -- the last of these, proposed by Lambert after he found an old programme for Paul Taglioni's 1849 skating ballet, performed in London every season from 1937 to 1968 except 1960, and one of the first ballets ever televised, by the BBC from Alexandra Palace on 3 May 1937. Horoscope, composed 1936-37 and premiered at Sadler's Wells on 27 January 1938 with Ashton choreographing and Fonteyn and Michael Somes dancing the leads, was the one original score of the decade, and it made stars of both dancers. He also orchestrated Vaughan Williams's Job for the theatre orchestra and conducted its danced premiere on 5 July 1931, and he began recording in earnest for HMV and RCA Victor in 1939-40 with the Sadler's Wells Orchestra. The stall is the story: the man who was hired to make the music for a company found that making the music for a company left no time to write music.

**Takeaway:** the institution he was hired to serve ate the composer -- the arrangements are brilliant, the one original ballet is a triumph, and the composing career effectively ends at thirty-one. [C34475]

## 6. War, Covent Garden, decline, and death, 1939-1951

He was ruled unfit for active service. The company toured Britain through the war, and in May 1940 it narrowly escaped from Holland as German paratroopers invaded -- the escape during which the Horoscope scores and sets were lost, and which he recalled in Aubade heroique, written in 1942 and first performed 21 February 1943, dedicated to Vaughan Williams on his seventieth birthday. The wartime works are small and functional: the Merchant Seamen film score in 1940, The Prospect Before Us after Boyce in 1940, Dirge from Cymbeline in 1940 (first performed by BBC broadcast 23 March 1947), Comus after Purcell in 1942, Hamlet incidental music in 1944. He was associate conductor of the London Promenade Concerts in 1945-46. The company moved to the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden; he conducted The Sleeping Beauty there on 20 February 1946 and the inaugural Covent Garden Opera production, his own Purcell arrangement The Fairy Queen, on 12 December 1946, and conducted Puccini's Turandot at Covent Garden in 1947. He retired as music director in 1947, succeeded by his former pupil Robert Irving, and was made an artistic director of the company in 1948. He divorced Florence Kaye in 1947 and married the artist Isabel Nicholas (Delmer) on 7 October 1947; she designed the sets and costumes for Tiresias, and after his death married Alan Rawsthorne. Trois pieces negres pour les touches blanches, three jazz-accented piano-duet pieces playable on the white keys only, was written in 1949 for the identical twin piano duo Mary and Geraldine Peppin, who premiered it on 17 May 1949. His first American tour came in 1949, conducting Sleeping Beauty and Swan Lake at the Metropolitan Opera in New York; Lincoln Kirstein called him "a divine conductor, the greatest ballet man in the business... a genius for tempi." His last work was Tiresias, composed 1950-51 for the Festival of Britain, choreographed by Ashton, premiered at the Royal Opera House on 9 July 1951 in the presence of Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother, with Somes as the male Tiresias and Fonteyn as the female; ailing, he called on Robert Irving, Alan Rawsthorne, Elisabeth Lutyens, Gordon Jacob, Denis ApIvor, Humphrey Searle and Christian Darnton to help finish the orchestration. The critical reception was generally negative; he instructed solicitors over Richard Buckle's hostile review, and some contemporaries held that the reception hastened his death. He conducted Summer's Last Will and Testament in May 1951. He died on 21 August 1951, six weeks after the Tiresias premiere. He never wrote an opera or a symphony. The company he helped found became the Royal Ballet by Royal Charter in 1956, five years after his death, so the title "founding music director" is retrospective -- in his lifetime it was the Vic-Wells Ballet, then the Sadler's Wells Ballet.

**Takeaway:** sixteen years of keeping the orchestra playing, two major works, and a last ballet finished by seven colleagues -- the conductor outlived the composer by a decade and a half, and the institution outlived them both. [C34477]

## 7. Jazz rhythm as a word-setting engine

Lambert's first mechanism is the one he stated himself, and it is a claim about language, not about style. He argued that English needs "an infinitely more varied and syncopated rhythm than is to be found in the nineteenth-century romantics," and that jazz rhythm is the mechanism for setting English words. The method: ragtime syncopation, triplet-against-duplet cross-rhythm -- the piano playing triplets against duplets, "redolent of a rumba" -- and habanera and Brazilian dance rhythms laid under a choral line so the words ride the off-beat. His own words from 1928, quoted in both Wikipedia and Christopher Palmer's notes: "The chief interest of jazz rhythms lies in their application to the setting of words... the best jazz songs of today are, in fact, nearer in their methods to the late fifteenth-century composers than any music since." The mechanism shows up in The Rio Grande, completed 1927, setting Sacheverell Sitwell, above all in the outer allegro sections and the central tango-nocturne, where the chorus's words are carried by the syncopation rather than laid on top of it. The watchable behaviour is a text that moves the way speech moves: the beat is not a grid the words are fitted to but a current the words ride, and the off-beat is where the meaning lands.

**Takeaway:** syncopation is not decoration on the words but the vehicle for them -- the rhythm does the work the melody cannot, and the English line sounds spoken because it is set to a beat that keeps slipping out from under it. [C34479]

## 8. The transplanted fanfare

A single overheard musical event becomes the generative seed of a whole work. In 1923 Lambert heard Will Vodery's Plantation Orchestra play a Delius-like fanfare at the London Pavilion -- first trumpet Johnny Dunn, "creator of wa-wa" -- and later wrote that it was "electrifying... It definitely opened up a new world of sound." Palmer, reconstructing the fanfare with the pianist Angus Morrison, the dedicatee of The Rio Grande, found that it "pervades The Rio Grande itself -- the chorus's fortissimo opening statement is a direct transmutation, or transcription" of it. The same fanfare-shape also runs through Delius's Walk to the Paradise Garden. Lambert's own gloss on the lineage: "juicy" jazz harmony originated in the spiritual, and the spiritual in Hymns Ancient and Modern. The fanfare returns as a motto through his life: a rising triplet figure near the opening of Elegiac Blues imitates the Delius-like fanfare that opened Blackbirds, and that fanfare "remained with him as a motto theme throughout his life." The mechanism is transplantation: hear a sound once, in a theatre, in a band, and carry it into a concert work where it does structural work -- the opening gesture of the piece is a memory of a night out, rebuilt as architecture.

**Takeaway:** the seed of the masterpiece was not invented but overheard -- a fanfare from a pit band, carried home, and rebuilt as the opening of a cantata, and it kept returning for the rest of his life. [C34481]

## 9. No-woodwind orchestration, with emancipated percussion

Deliberate subtraction of an entire orchestral family, compensated by promoting percussion to soloist status. The Rio Grande is scored for alto soloist, mixed chorus, piano, brass, strings and fifteen percussion instruments requiring five players -- no woodwind at all. Palmer's judgement: "you would expect the absence of woodwinds adversely to affect the brightness of the overall sonority, but it does not... the emancipated treatment of the percussion -- almost as much a virtuoso entity as the piano -- is to be found earlier in Stravinsky's Les Noces." The piano part is "of the utmost brilliance... the greatest advance in piano technique since Albeniz," Lambert's own praise of jazz piano writing, applied by Palmer to the part. The mechanism is subtraction as addition: remove the family that would soften the sound, and the families that remain have to work harder -- the brass gets brighter, the percussion gets promoted from accompaniment to a virtuoso entity, and the piano becomes the narrator. The same habit of choosing forces for exact timbral purpose recurs through the catalogue: the 1924 Piano Concerto for piano, two trumpets, timpani and strings, with no full wind complement; the Concerto for Piano and Nine Instruments, nine players and nothing else; Tiresias with no upper strings. Forces are chosen for the piece, never for the market.

**Takeaway:** take a whole family out and the rest of the orchestra has to become more itself -- the subtraction is the orchestration, and the percussion stops being accompaniment and starts being a voice. [C34483]

## 10. Free-fantasy ternary form with a cadenza hinge

A loose, legible large form: fast outer panels hinged on a virtuoso cadenza, with the coda recycled from the slow middle. The Rio Grande runs first section (allegro), then a cadenza for piano and percussion, then a slow central section "in the style of a nostalgic tango," then a recapitulation, then a tranquil coda based on material from the central section -- "the party's over, and the music drifts into the perfumed night." The cadenza is the pivot: the piece's energy is discharged through the soloist before the mood inverts. Palmer calls the form "simplicity itself," and the simplicity is the point: the form does not need to be clever because the material is doing the work -- the tango in the middle is the memory the coda returns to, and the cadenza is the moment the soloist burns off the energy that would otherwise have to be resolved by argument. Lambert conceived the piano as "the 'I' of a novel, a central narrator," so the form is also a narrative form: the narrator speaks, the narrator shows off, the narrator remembers, the narrator falls quiet.

**Takeaway:** a form with one hinge and one return -- the cadenza spends the energy, the tango supplies the memory, and the coda is the memory coming back as a farewell. [C34485]

## 11. Remote-meter wrong-footing

Destroying the listener's sense of the bar line by cycling through irregular meters and reversing beat and syncopation polarity. Passages in the Concerto for Piano and Nine Instruments run through remote time signatures -- 7/4, 11/8, 13/8 -- so "one's sense of the bar line is utterly undermined, where he reverses the polarity of main beat and syncopation to introduce a calculated ambiguity of pattern that is forever wrong-footing the listener, shifting the stress in the shape or shapes round the stress." The ANU dissertation on Lambert devotes its eighth chapter to "Rhythmic Disruption" in the mature works. The Piano Sonata does the same at smaller scale: its "experiments in rhythmical patterning go further with greater subtlety and purpose." The mechanism is the jazz idiom absorbed into structure: in 1927 the syncopation is a style worn on the surface, and by 1931 it is a structural device that blurs where the sonata's exposition ends and its development begins, because the bar line itself keeps moving. The watchable behaviour is a listener who cannot find the downbeat -- and a performer who has to, because the ensemble only holds together if somebody is counting.

**Takeaway:** the bar line is not a fact but a habit, and the habit can be broken -- change the meter, flip the stress, and the listener is wrong-footed while the structure holds. [C34487]

## 12. The jazz-band / Pierrot lunaire hybrid

A nine-player ensemble that is simultaneously a jazz band and a Schoenbergian chamber group. The Concerto for Piano and Nine Instruments is scored for flute doubling piccolo, three clarinets doubling bass and E-flat members of the family, trumpet, trombone, cello, double bass and percussion -- "the effect is starkly incisive, verging on abrasive." The first movement is a "highly personalized sonata in its own right"; the second movement, the Intermede, fuses slow movement and scherzo in one span. Near figure 42, flute, piano and maraca deliver "a grotesque version of the accompaniment to 'Corpus Christi'," followed by "a manically high-spirited paraphrase of 'The Frost-bound Wood'" -- folk material quoted and distorted. The concerto was dedicated to Philip Heseltine, Peter Warlock, who died by suicide in December 1930, and Easterbrook's analysis reads the whole work as exorcism and therapy: the jazz-blues idiom of The Rio Grande, absorbed in the Sonata, is here used "unselfconsciously to perform certain structural tasks." The Intermede is "the abode of ghosts": a slow dark opening returns at the end, carrying the spirit of Warlock's The Curlew, which Lambert had conducted and recorded, and between the two returns a frantic section quotes Warlock grotesquely. The Finale maintains a claustrophobic tread throughout, marked Lugubre, and ends with a macabre self-parody of The Rio Grande -- "The noisy streets are empty and hushed is the town" -- dribbling away "teasingly, enigmatically and in darkness."

**Takeaway:** nine players, two traditions, one dedication -- the jazz band and the Pierrot ensemble are the same nine people, and the folk quotations are Warlock's ghost being quoted back at himself. [C34489]

## 13. Blues-in-rondo and the flash mood-switch

A formal container, the rondo, filled with blues material, plus the jazz ability to flip mood instantaneously without stylistic break. The Piano Sonata's second movement is a blues in rondo form: a slow impressionistic opening with a prominent triplet figure, a blues melody against a guitar-like accompaniment -- "ironic and grotesque" -- and episodes like "a distant dance band through a haze of Gauloises bleues." More generally, Palmer's judgement on what Lambert borrows most creatively from jazz is "the ability to shift in a flash from one mood to its opposite without any stylistic inconsistency," and "to harness the expressive force of tonal centres while not subjecting him to the tyrannies of the conventional key system." The Sonata's arc is the pivot of his career in miniature: "the almost irresponsible joyousness of the earlier work has yielded to something more stern and tense... the pastoral has become urban, built over with bars and bordellos." Cecil Gray heard in it "the dark, black, Celinesque quality inspired by long, cat-like prowlings through the suburbs of Paris." Its strength, per Easterbrook, is "continuous development and growth" -- the music never stops becoming something else, and the mood-switch is the engine of that becoming. The Sonata's blues-movement phrase is later quoted in the baritone's opening line of the Summer's Last Will Saraband, so the mood-switch is also a memory device: the blues comes back, years later, as a dirge.

**Takeaway:** a blues in a rondo is a mood that keeps coming back wearing different clothes -- and the switch between moods is instant because the tonal centre is a place to visit, not a system to obey. [C34491]

## 14. The Elizabethan dance-form masque as narrative engine

A fifty-five-minute choral work built entirely from named Elizabethan dance and vocal forms, sequenced as a day, a season, and a life. Summer's Last Will and Testament runs seven movements, each a period form: Intrata for orchestra alone, a Pastorale and a Siciliana; Madrigal con ritornelli, "Fair Summer droops"; Corante, "Spring, the sweet Spring"; Brawles, "Trip and go, heave and ho!"; Madrigal con ritornelli, "Autumn hath all the Summer's fruitful treasure"; Rondo burlesca, "King Pest," for orchestra alone; Saraband, "Adieu, farewell earth's bliss!," for baritone solo. The shape is two-part: Part I builds to a wordless choral climax at "high summer," the voices dispensing with words "as they do in Delius's Song of the High Hills"; Part II turns, the chorus "dissonantly deplores the approach of autumn," and the machinery "runs down, stutters and fails" into the Saraband. The text is Thomas Nashe's 1592 "pleasant comedy," refashioned in contemporary style, learned from Warlock. Lambert called it a "masque," and he called it his best work: "I like Summer's Last Will and Testament the best of all my work," he told Ashton in 1949. Malcolm Arnold called it "one of the undiscovered treasures of the English choral repertoire." The premiere, Queen's Hall, 29 January 1936, six days after George V's death, was sparsely attended in a national mood hostile to an hour of plague and death; it received only one or two performances in his lifetime, and one complete commercial recording, Hyperion in 1992. The failure convinced him he had failed as a composer. The mechanism is the period form as a clock: each dance is a time of day and a season and a stage of life, and the sequence does the narrating, so the music never has to explain itself.

**Takeaway:** seven old dances in a row are a life in a row -- the forms are the plot, the plague is the turn, and the machine running down is the ending. [C34493]

## 15. Quotation as memorial and self-quotation web

Real incidents and his own earlier music are quoted verbatim at structural climaxes, turning quotation into biography. In "King Pest," the phrase "carry him off to the burying ground" -- from the sea shanty "Walk him along, Johnny," which Heseltine once performed in an African witch-doctor's robe as a danse macabre at his own imagined funeral -- is "quoted at the climax... hammered out by the full orchestra in brutal fortissimo unison," over fleeting allusions to the Dies irae outline, not the full chant as in Berlioz. The Saraband's baritone opening derives from the Piano Sonata's blues movement; the strings quote "They dance no sarabande" from The Rio Grande just after the basses mutter "Lord, have mercy on us!" The whole work opens and closes on the same sustained high A -- "As in Carmina Burana, Fortune's wheel has turned full circle." The web runs both ways: the Concerto for Piano and Nine Instruments quotes Warlock's "Corpus Christi" and "The Frost-bound Wood" grotesquely in its Intermede, and its Finale parodies The Rio Grande itself. The mechanism is quotation as a dated object: the quoted phrase is not decoration but evidence -- it happened, somebody did it, somebody died, and the music remembers by repeating it exactly where it hurts most.

**Takeaway:** a quotation is a date and a death smuggled into the score -- the shanty is Heseltine's funeral, the blues is the Sonata coming back as a dirge, and the wheel turns full circle on one held note. [C34495]

## 16. The astrological sign system as ballet dramaturgy

A ballet whose plot, casting of dances, and resolution are all generated by a natal chart. Lambert's own programme statement for Horoscope: "When people are born they have the sun in one sign of the Zodiac, the moon in another. This ballet takes for its theme a man who has the sun in Leo and the moon in Gemini, and a woman who also has the moon in Gemini but whose sun is in Virgo. The two opposed signs of Leo and Virgo, the one energetic and full-blooded, the other timid and sensitive, struggle to keep the man and the woman apart. It is by their mutual sign, Gemini, that they are brought together, and by the moon that they are finally united." Each sign gets its own dance-form: "Dance for the Followers of Leo," frenzied; "Saraband for the Followers of Virgo," Ravel-like restraint; "Valse for the Gemini"; "Bacchanale"; "Invocation to the Moon and Finale." The opening "Palindromic Prelude" -- its theme Lambert claimed was dictated to him by the ghost of Bernard van Dieren -- is a musical palindrome, though no source read describes how the palindrome is constructed. Ashton suggested the tune for the Invocation. The ballet was composed in 1937 and premiered at Sadler's Wells on 27 January 1938, choreographed by Ashton, scenery by Sophie Fedorovitch, Lambert conducting, Fonteyn the Woman and Michael Somes the Man; it made stars of both, Somes especially. It is widely read as a symbolic representation of Lambert's affair with Fonteyn. Francis Toye called it "perhaps the most successful modern ballet that has been produced at this theatre for a long time." The full score was lost when the company fled the Netherlands in 1940, leaving scenery, costumes and scores behind; only nine numbers survive, and the ballet was never revived after twenty-nine performances. The mechanism is the chart as a machine: feed in two suns and two moons, and the plot, the dances and the ending all fall out of the arithmetic.

**Takeaway:** a horoscope is a plot generator -- two suns that cannot meet, one moon they share, and the dances are the signs arguing until the moon settles it. [C34497]

## 17. The classical-dance-number suite -- homage, not parody

Early ballets built as strings of named Baroque and classical dances, where the scenario is only a peg for the music. Romeo and Juliet, composed 1924-25, is two tableaux of twelve titled numbers: Rondino, "The dancers arrive late for class"; Gavotte and Trio; Scherzetto; Siciliana; Sonatina; Sinfonia; Alla Marcia; Toccata, the duel; Musette, the balcony scene; Burlesca; Adagietto, the death of Juliet; Finale: Presto -- despite Diaghilev's surrealist scenario, in which the lovers elope by aeroplane in leather coats and goggles. Pomona, composed 1926, is a seven-number suite: Intrata, Coranto, Pastorale, Menuetto, Passacaglia, Rigadoon, Siciliana. Lambert's stated distinction is the key to the mechanism: his scores were homages to the Classical era, whereas Stravinsky's Pulcinella was experienced by him as parody -- which explains his later hostility to Stravinskian neoclassicism. The scenario is "a mere peg on which to hang the music," per MusicWeb, and the peg is allowed to be absurd -- a ballet class, a duel, an aeroplane -- because the numbers are the work. The watchable behaviour is a suite that keeps announcing its next dance by name, so the audience always knows what form is coming, and the surprise is what Lambert does inside the form he just named.

**Takeaway:** name the dance and the dance does the work -- the scenario can be anything, even an aeroplane, because the gavotte and the siciliana are the argument. [C34499]

## 18. Found-music arrangement -- the magpie re-composition

Building new ballet scores entirely from other composers' music, re-orchestrated and re-linked, often triggered by an archival find. Les Patineurs, premiered 16 February 1937: Lambert chanced on an old programme for Paul Taglioni's 1849 ballet Les Plaisirs de l'Hiver, ou, Les Patineurs, then chose vocal and dance numbers from two Meyerbeer operas, Le prophete and L'etoile du nord, and "linked them into an irresistibly cheerful score" beginning with the waltz opening Act 2 of L'etoile du nord -- a divertissement with no story, a Victorian skating party, performed every London season from 1937 to 1968. The same method elsewhere: Dante Sonata, premiered 23 January 1940, Liszt's Apres une lecture du Dante orchestrated for piano and orchestra, two opposed groups, the Children of Light and the Children of Darkness, tossed "as if by a great wind"; Apparitions, 1936, Liszt arranged by Lambert and orchestrated by Gordon Jacob; Les Rendez-vous, Auber from L'Enfant prodigue; The Prospect Before Us, 1940, William Boyce, whose eight symphonies Lambert edited; The Wise Virgins, Bach via Walton, the music chosen by Lambert; piano-duet transcriptions of Walton's Facade suites. MusicWeb's summary: "Lambert, rather like Beecham, was a happy rummager among dusty disowned scores." The mechanism is the archive as a quarry: find a dead programme, a forgotten opera, a set of symphonies nobody plays, and rebuild them into a ballet that runs for thirty years. The arranging is not secondary work -- after 1931 it is most of the work, and Les Patineurs outlasted every original ballet he wrote except The Rio Grande.

**Takeaway:** the dust is the material -- find a programme from 1849, a Meyerbeer waltz, a Boyce symphony, and link them until the dead music skates again. [C34501]

## 19. The white-keys-only constraint

A late miniature built under a hard pitch constraint: both pianists play only the white keys. Trois pieces negres pour les touches blanches -- three jazz-accented piano-duet pieces, Aubade, Siesta, Nocturne -- use only the white keys of the piano, so all harmony is diatonic by construction and colour must come from rhythm, spacing and voicing instead of chromaticism. It is described as "a kaleidoscopic, jazz-accented tour de force exploration of the piano's white keys." Commissioned by the London Contemporary Music Centre, dedicated to its president Edward Clark, premiered by the identical twins Mary and Geraldine Peppin on 17 May 1949. The title's conceit, noted by MusicWeb: "Black" music played on the "White" keys only. The mechanism is the constraint as the composition: take the jazz idiom that made his name, shrink it to a parlour game, remove every black key, and see what rhythm and spacing can still say. It is the last return of the jazz idiom in his catalogue, and it returns miniaturised -- the expansive symphonic jazz of 1927 reduced to four hands on seven notes, and still recognisably his.

**Takeaway:** seven notes are enough if the rhythm is right -- the constraint does the composing, and the jazz survives by getting smaller. [C34503]

## 20. Conducting as dance-service

Conducting in which the beat serves the dancers' bodies as much as the score -- the mechanism that made him the Royal Ballet's founding music director. He was appointed conductor and music director of the Vic-Wells Ballet in 1931, aged twenty-six, and stayed until his resignation in 1947. The Dance Research review of Lloyd's biography puts it plainly: he "understood both the rhythmic life of a score but also the rhythmic identity and demands of the choreography and of its executants: he encouraged, inspired the dance life of a ballet because he recognised the needs of the dancers." De Valois called him "the greatest ballet conductor and advisor his country had ever had." Ashton called him the finest ballet conductor he ever worked with. As a programmer he championed neglected repertoire in Britain -- Liszt, Chabrier, Waldteufel, romantic Russians, Boyce -- and his 1939-40 Sadler's Wells recordings, Tchaikovsky's Sleeping Beauty excerpts, Les Patineurs, Rossini's William Tell ballet music, were praised for swagger and suavity and an orchestra audibly "having a really good time." He also prepared Walton's orchestra for the early Facade performances and recordings, and was the work's definitive reciter of Edith Sitwell's verses. Lincoln Kirstein, after the 1949 New York tour: "Marvellous music, exquisitely played by Lambert, a divine conductor, the greatest ballet man in the business... a genius for tempi." The mechanism is tempo as a service: the beat is not the conductor's property but the dancers' floor, and the right tempo is the one the bodies can dance on. No source read describes his baton technique or rehearsal method -- the sources praise the effect, the dance-serving rhythm and the swagger, not the gestural mechanics -- so this study does not invent them.

**Takeaway:** the beat belongs to the bodies, not the baton -- conduct for the dancers' feet and the orchestra follows, and the tempi are the conducting. [C34505]

## 21. Music Ho! -- the polemic as a mechanism of taste

The book is a mechanism too, because it states the theory the arrangements practise. Music Ho!: A Study of Music in Decline, published by Faber in 1934, subtitled as a study of modern music in relation to the other arts and to the social and mechanical background of modern life -- "a study of movements rather than musicians," per its preface. The thesis: the revolutionary pre-war innovations of Debussy, Schoenberg and Stravinsky have given way to an age of pastiche, "an age of pastiche, an obsession with the music of the past." The sentence that carries the whole book: "When speed and familiarity have reduced traveling in space to the level of the humdrum those in search of the exotic will have to travel in time." Modern taste is the post-war room where "an Adam mantelpiece is covered with negro masks while Victorian wool-pictures jostle the minor Cubists" -- and Poulenc's music is "the chic chaos" of that room: Poulenc "does not write in any particular style... but in every style of the past and present that is not actually frowned on as pompous and outmoded." Stravinsky's neoclassicism "mistakes the ornamental -- the sequences, cadences, appoggiaturas -- for the heart of the music." Satie alone succeeds at abstraction: his trios of pieces are "sculpturesque... as though we were to move slowly round a piece of sculpture." On atonalism: "the more we free ourselves from tonal prejudice... the less appeal atonalism will have, because it is based on a direct reversal of academic method. Like blasphemy, it requires a background of belief for its full effect." He dismissed Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue as "neither good jazz nor good Liszt, and in no sense of the word a good concerto," while praising Duke Ellington and Milhaud's La Creation du Monde. Isaiah Berlin, reviewing it in the Spectator in 1934, praised its "vigorous, intelligent and gay" non-technical prose, and noted that Lambert's fiercest attacks fall on the Parisian pasticheurs around Stravinsky and his "delightful infatuation" with Russian music -- Glinka, Mussorgsky, Borodin, Balakirev. The book predicts the audience splitting into a mass popular public and a tiny contemporary niche. The polemic is the theory of the arrangements: if the present is pastiche, travel in time -- Meyerbeer, Auber, Boyce, Liszt -- and make the past new by re-orchestrating it. This leg read the book only through reviews and essays quoting it, not in full, and the quotations here come via Electric Literature and Berlin's review.

**Takeaway:** when the present is exhausted, travel in time -- the polemic is the programme, and the arrangements are the polemic put to work. [C34507]

## 22. The network

Lambert's network is a theatre network, not a concert-hall one, and it is held together by three rooms: the Sitwells' house, Diaghilev's company, and de Valois's company. The family first: his father George Washington Lambert, the Australian painter, born in St Petersburg and raised in Australia, who left for Australia when Constant was fifteen and died in May 1930; his elder brother Maurice Lambert, the sculptor, born in Paris; his first wife Florence Kaye, married 5 August 1931, divorced 1947, mother of his son; his son Kit Lambert, born 11 May 1935, named after the painter Christopher "Kit" Wood, later the manager and producer of the Who from 1964 to 1976, whose godfather was William Walton and whose godmother was Margot Fonteyn, and who is buried in his father's grave in Brompton Cemetery -- though the sources are clear that Constant largely ignored him, so the Who link is real and the father-son bond was thin; his second wife Isabel Nicholas (Delmer), married 7 October 1947, the artist who designed Tiresias, who after his death married Alan Rawsthorne. The triumvirate: Ninette de Valois, who engaged him in 1931 and called him the greatest ballet conductor and advisor the country had ever had; Frederick Ashton, for whom he wrote Horoscope and Tiresias and arranged a dozen scores, and who called him the finest ballet conductor he ever worked with; Lilian Baylis, whose theatres at the Old Vic and Sadler's Wells housed the whole enterprise, who engaged de Valois in 1926 and 1928 and reopened Sadler's Wells at the gala on 6 January 1931. The dancers: Margot Fonteyn, the on-and-off affair from the mid-1930s, the great love of her life by her friends' account, the Woman of Horoscope, the female Tiresias; Robert Helpmann, the fellow Australian who joined the Vic-Wells in 1933-34 and danced Satan in Job in 1933, his first major role, Lambert conducting; Michael Somes, who joined in 1936 and co-starred with Fonteyn in Horoscope, the performance that made him a star. The impresario: Sergei Diaghilev, who commissioned Romeo and Juliet in 1925 and staged it in Monte Carlo on 4 May 1926, the first English composer for the Ballets Russes, and with whom Lambert later had the famous falling-out whose details no source read gives. The composers: William Walton, the close friend from the Sitwells' house, dedicatee and reciter of Facade, whose Wise Virgins Lambert initiated and whose godson Kit was; Ralph Vaughan Williams, the teacher at the Royal College of Music from 1922 to 1926, whose Job Lambert rescored for the pit; Philip Heseltine, Peter Warlock, the friend whose death in December 1930 the Concerto for Piano and Nine Instruments is dedicated to and whose ghost haunts its Intermede; Benjamin Britten, verified but hostile, not collaborative -- Lambert "detested" him, called him "Darling Bengie" in a 1945 letter amid a rant about a supposed homosexual conspiracy in music led by Britten and Peter Pears, yet reviewed Britten's Piano Concerto warmly in The Listener in 1938 and conducted the premiere of Britten's Ballad of Heroes in April 1939. The adjacent artists, evidenced by shared works: Christopher "Kit" Wood, the painter whose sets for Romeo and Juliet Diaghilev rejected for Ernst and Miro, and after whom Kit Lambert was named; Bronislava Nijinska, who choreographed Romeo and Juliet in 1926 and Pomona in 1927; Joan Miro, who with Max Ernst designed Romeo and Juliet, the Paris opening degenerating into a surrealist riot. The patrons and friends: John Maynard Keynes, who called Lambert "perhaps the most brilliant man he had ever met"; Anthony Powell, for whom Lambert was the model of Hugh Moreland in A Dance to the Music of Time; the Sitwells, Edith, Osbert and Sacheverell, who absorbed the schoolboy into their house and whose poem Sacheverell supplied for The Rio Grande; Anna May Wong, the distant infatuation to whom the Eight Poems of Li-Po are dedicated; Edward J. Dent, the mentor who taught him proportion; Robert Irving, the former pupil who succeeded him as musical director; Duke Ellington, the deepest musical influence alongside Liszt.

**Takeaway:** three rooms and one pit -- the Sitwells' house gave him the friends, Diaghilev gave him the launch, de Valois gave him the company, and the pit is where he spent the rest of his life. [C34509]

## 23. What he did not do

The honest limits matter as much as the mechanisms, because they are what keep the prompts honest. There is no named or codified technique with Lambert's name on it, no "Lambert system," no signature device published as a theory -- his mechanisms are working habits visible across scores, not a system, and the ANU thesis's "Dionysian modernism" is the scholar's label, not his. There is no serialism and no twelve-tone writing: harmony "sometimes approaching atonalism" is the strongest claim any source makes, and no source describes a dodecaphonic method. There is no folksong system: he studied with Vaughan Williams but, per the London Review of Books, "had no interest in folksong," and Music Ho! mocks the folk-tune dead end. There is no opera and no symphony: he "worked in almost every genre but opera and symphony." There is no detailed account of his conducting technique -- no source read describes his baton, his beat pattern or his rehearsal method; the sources praise the effect, the dance-serving rhythm and the swagger, not the gestural mechanics. There is no score-level detail on the "Palindromic Prelude" beyond its title and the van Dieren ghost anecdote -- no description of how the palindrome is constructed was found. There is no scenario detail for Pomona beyond the movement list, and no movement list, scenario detail or reception account for Les Rendez-vous or Bar aux Folies-Bergere beyond their existence and dates. The Prize-fight first-performance date is unresolved: the works list gives 6 March 1924, Lloyd's booklet notes say the piece remained unperformed until a BBC broadcast on 12 May 1969, and this study does not state a 1924 stage premiere without that caveat. The Bird Actors first performance is 5 July 1931 in the works list and 6 July 1931 in Lloyd's notes, a one-day split recorded, not settled.

**Takeaway:** no system, no serialism, no opera, no symphony, no conducting method on record -- the limits are the frame, and what is inside the frame is a theatre musician who chose his forces and his dead composers with exact care. [C34511]

## 24. Aesthetic logic: the magpie and the pit

The aesthetic logic of Lambert is that the theatre is the natural element and the past is the material, and the two facts explain each other. Nearly everything he wrote is a ballet, a masque, incidental music, a film score, or a song cycle with a dramatic conceit; he never wrote a symphony or a string quartet, because the concert hall was never the room. The early works lean on the present -- jazz, Satie, Stravinsky -- and the late works lean on older music -- Elizabethan Nashe, Victorian Meyerbeer and Auber, Russian Glinka -- and Music Ho! theorizes exactly that move: moderns "travel in time" in search of the exotic. The wit, the pastiche and the lampoon are the method from first to last: Prize-fight's Satie satire and Mr Bear's Stravinsky lampoon at nineteen, the self-parody of The Rio Grande inside the 1931 Concerto's Finale at twenty-six. The economy of means is the signature: two trumpets, timpani and strings in 1924; no woodwind in 1927; nine players in 1931; no upper strings in 1951; white keys only in 1949. Forces are chosen for the piece, never for the market, and the subtraction is the orchestration. The Blackbirds fanfare motto recurs as a lifelong signature, the one sound that never left him. And the arc is a tragedy of scale: the early works are short, witty and theatrical; the middle period attempts the large statement, Summer's Last Will, fifty-five minutes of plague and death; after its failure the scale collapses, and no original work over twenty-seven minutes follows except Tiresias, which needed five colleagues to orchestrate. The conductor outlived the composer by a decade and a half because the pit paid the rent and the desk did not. His own words give the logic away. On jazz: "The chief interest of jazz rhythms lies in their application to the setting of words." On the present: "When speed and familiarity have reduced traveling in space to the level of the humdrum those in search of the exotic will have to travel in time." On his best work, to Ashton in 1949: "I like Summer's Last Will and Testament the best of all my work." The study's one hard correction to the received picture is the appointment: he was not hired in 1935, when the company consolidated, but in 1931, when it was founded -- and the sixteen years that followed are the story, the composer hired to make the sound for a house, and the house eating the composer.

**Takeaway:** the theatre is the element, the past is the material, the subtraction is the orchestration -- and the pit is where the composer went to live, and where he stayed until there was no composer left. [C34513]
