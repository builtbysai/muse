# Dirck van Baburen: Study Notes

**Site:** Dirck Jaspersz. van Baburen (c. 1592/93 - died 21 February 1624, buried 28 February 1624), Dutch Golden Age painter of Utrecht, the third of the Utrecht Caravaggisti with Hendrick ter Brugghen and Gerard van Honthorst, and the one who died first and youngest, at about thirty. Birth date disputed: c. 1595 is the traditional date (Slatkes 1965, Wikipedia, the Museo Thyssen-Bornemisza), while Wayne Franits in his 2013 catalogue raisonne argues c. 1592/93, from a document published by Marten Jan Bok giving a terminus ante quem of 16 May 1593 for a live birth to his parents; the British Museum gives 1592/93-1624. This study uses c. 1592/93 with the dispute stated, not c. 1595 as settled. He was probably born in Wijk bij Duurstede, a small town on the Kromme Rijn near Utrecht; his father Jaspar van Baburen collected tolls on the river from 1592 and from 1594 administered ecclesiastical property seized by the town, a post Franits argues would not have gone to a Catholic, so the family were probably Protestants; his mother was Margareta van Doyenburch; the family moved to Utrecht around 1599. He trained in Utrecht under Paulus Moreelse (1571-1638), portraitist and co-founder with Abraham Bloemaert of the Utrecht guild; the earliest archival reference is a 1611 Guild of St Luke receipt recording that he had paid his tuition for the year, which Franits stresses does NOT say he began training that year, only that he paid; training probably began around 1607 or 1608, and 1611 was likely his last year under Moreelse. He probably arrived in Italy in the summer or fall of 1612, if not early 1613, at about nineteen, and spent about eight or nine years there, mostly in Rome. He was one of the earliest members of the Bentvueghels, the society of Dutch and Flemish artists in Rome, with the bent-name "Biervlieg" ("Beer Fly", one who drinks a lot), and appears among the earliest-known members in the anonymous group drawing of about 1620 now in Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen, Rotterdam. In Rome he collaborated with the fellow countryman David de Haen (died 1622), lived with de Haen and Nicolas Regnier at times, and befriended Bartolomeo Manfredi (1582-1622), Caravaggio's leading early follower. His Roman patrons were Pietro Cussida (died 1622), the Spanish diplomat and art agent for Philip III who commissioned the Pieta Chapel decoration in San Pietro in Montorio on the Janiculum Hill - Baburen's Entombment (1617, still in situ) as the altarpiece, a Way to Calvary on a lateral wall, and a now-lost Raising of the Cross - and Marchese Vincenzo Giustiniani (1564-1637), who commissioned Christ Washing the Feet of the Apostles (Gemaldegalerie, Berlin). He did NOT work for Cardinal Scipione Borghese: the Borghese Capture of Christ did not enter the Borghese collection until 1787 (a gift from the sculptor Bartolomeo Cavaceppi), and Franits "justifiably pans the long-discredited but still widely held notion" (HNA review) - do not present Borghese as a patron. He returned to Utrecht in the fall of 1620 or, more likely, the winter of 1621 (Slatkes said summer 1620; Franits says fall 1620 or winter 1621; several pictures dated 1622 exist in Italian versions, which supports the later return). His Utrecht career lasted only about three years, until his death in February 1624, and in that short time, per the Thyssen, "he soon became one of the most innovative artists in Utrecht, introducing and establishing new stylistic and iconographic models that were soon imitated by other artists, both in genre scenes and history painting." He died a bachelor; his mother and sister, with whom he had been living, were named heirs; the sister is not named in any source read. The cause of death is not documented: Franits raises the plague, which "bedeviled Utrecht on and off throughout the early seventeenth century", while Marten Jan Bok has more recently suggested he may have fallen ill while defending Utrecht with his civic guard company against a potential Spanish siege during the bitter winter of 1624 - neither explanation is proven. Around 1629 Constantijn Huygens noted him as one of the important Dutch painters of the early decades of the century; he was then largely forgotten as Utrecht Caravaggism faded after 1630, and the modern recovery rests on two monographs: Leonard J. Slatkes, Dirck van Baburen: A Dutch Painter in Utrecht and Rome (1965), the first major study, and Wayne Franits, The Paintings of Dirck van Baburen ca. 1592/93-1624: Catalogue Raisonne (2013), "the first major study of the artist in nearly 50 years", cataloguing 42 authentic paintings, 29 associated with the artist and/or his workshop, 152 rejected works, 18 lost, and 5 drawings (publisher's blurb; the HNA review gives 36 authentic works - the two numbers disagree, see C419). His signature in the Utrecht period usually appears as "Theodor", "Teodoor" or a related spelling, variations on "Teodoro", his name in Italy; the Utrecht Lute Player is signed "Teodorus". Franits argues the signature functioned as an imprimatur for clients, signalling erudition, sophistication and international status from his years in Rome. ## M1. Raking chiaroscuro: light as a blade against a dark ground

What it is: figures are strongly illuminated from one side and set against a dark, unarticulated background, so that lit flesh and fabric detach from shadow with hard edges. Franits on the Entombment: Baburen "adopted the chiaroscuro effects (enhanced by natural light) and volumetric forms" of Caravaggio's Entombment, and "strongly illuminated figures set against a dark background literally stood out forcefully within dusky seventeenth-century chapels", unlike evenly lit altarpieces whose figures "tended to fade into the heavy shadows". Watchable behaviour: a face or hand snaps out of blackness as if lit by a single off-frame lamp; the rest of the scene stays unreadable.

**Takeaway:** light is not atmosphere here, it is a cutting tool; the dark is the default state and light is the event.

## M2. The wedge cascade: a diagonal pile of bodies

What it is: figures are arranged in a wedge-like diagonal that cascades downward toward the lowest point of the action, usually a body. Franits on the Entombment: Baburen "deploys the same basic compositional structure as Caravaggio, with its wedge-like arrangement of figures set at a diagonal, cascading downward toward the body of the dead Christ", except that his tomb stone is "more table-like" and Christ's body is "rendered in an upright, almost seated position". Watchable behaviour: the eye slides down the diagonal like water down a chute and lands on the corpse; nothing in the frame competes with that slope.

**Takeaway:** composition as gravity; the diagonal does the storytelling before any face is read.

## M3. Close crop: half-length figures pushed to the canvas edge

What it is: half- to three-quarter-length figures are enlarged until their voluminous forms extend to the outer perimeter of the canvas, with no room, no landscape, no architecture to breathe in. Franits on the Crowning with Thorns: Baburen appropriated Manfredi's "closely cropped composition wherein the voluminous forms of the half to three-quarter-length figures are extended to the outer perimeter of the canvas". The Procuress shows the same device: "cropped, close-up figures close to the picture plane against a flat blank background are typical of Utrecht Caravaggism" (Wikipedia, The Procuress). Watchable behaviour: figures feel too big for the frame, as if the viewer has leaned in too close and cannot step back.

**Takeaway:** cropping is the zoom; there is no establishing shot, only the confrontation.

## M4. Appropriation-and-variation: take a prototype, then change one thing decisively

What it is: Baburen studies a known composition (Caravaggio, then Manfredi, then Ribera) and keeps its skeleton while swapping one element for something more violent or more frontal. In Christ Driving the Money Changers from the Temple (c. 1618) he takes Manfredi's version and "turned Christ in space and placed his arm in a poised position, poised that is, to release his whip", replaces Manfredi's "seated and hunched over figure in the foreground with one standing, who screams and recoils in terror", and turns the dove-woman from profile to frontal. In the Entombment he keeps Caravaggio's wedge but seats Christ upright. Watchable behaviour: a familiar scene that feels wrong in exactly one place - the figure who should be sitting is standing and screaming.

**Takeaway:** originality by substitution, not invention; one changed joint makes the whole body move differently.

## M5. Monumental, sturdy Utrecht figures in cool, bright tonalities

What it is: after the return to Utrecht, figures become bigger, sturdier and more monumental, painted in "cool and brighter tonalities" with "simplified, almost schematic brushwork". Franits: "his Utrecht period work generally features monumental, sturdy figures rendered with cool and brighter tonalities. Simplified, almost schematic brushwork enhanced these new components of his style, which were placed at the service of altogether new themes." Watchable behaviour: bodies read as carved blocks, cool to the eye, with less of Rome's warm gloom; the same hand, colder light.

**Takeaway:** the Utrecht turn is a palette and scale change as much as a subject change; cool light is the homecoming signature.

## M6. Schematic faces: flat angular planes plus pink dashes

What it is: in the final Utrecht years, faces are built from "broad, somewhat flat and angular planes of color with extended dashes of reddish-pink deployed to articulate ruddy cheeks and bulbous noses". Franits on the Crowning with Thorns (Nelson-Atkins version): "The countenances of Christ and the ruffian in the upper right are prototypical in this respect." Impasto highlights undergo the same simplification. "At times, this technique imparts an emphatic earthiness to Baburen's figures, in some instances quite deliberately to enhance the subject matter." Watchable behaviour: a cheek is two flat planes and one pink slash; the face reads instantly at distance and coarsens up close - a deliberate roughness, not a failure of finish.

**Takeaway:** the pink dash is a unit of character; earthiness is applied like a pigment, on purpose.

## M7. Wet-on-wet flesh and impasto, looser than Caravaggio

What it is: paint is applied with an impasto-laden brush, loosely and thinly in places, with wet-on-wet passages for flesh. Franits on the Entombment: "Baburen's brushwork is looser and more varied than Caravaggio's. In certain passages, like the lifeless, puckered flesh of Christ, he employs a wet-on-wet technique while other passages witness his practice of applying paint with an impasto-laden brush; anatomical details, among them the hair and lips, are rendered quite broadly." The early Capture of Christ is "thinly and loosely painted... featuring the strokes of an impasto-laden brush", "ungainly in certain passages and compellingly beautiful in others". Watchable behaviour: dead flesh puckers and catches light in ridges; hair and lips are laid down in a few broad strokes that refuse to resolve into detail.

**Takeaway:** the brush stays visible; finish is sacrificed for the feeling of a hand moving fast.

## M8. The single-figure musician: one body, one instrument, no setting

What it is: a single half-length musician, often in outlandish "Burgundian" theatrical dress, posed before an unarticulated background, playing directly toward the viewer. Baburen's Youth Playing a Mouth Harp (1621) and its lost Flute Player pendant "rank among the earliest - if not, the earliest - single-figured musicians in Utrecht painting". The formula is borrowed from Ribera, Manfredi and ultimately Caravaggio, and also from older sixteenth-century Northern Italian musician pictures. The National Gallery of Art notes the same device in ter Brugghen's Bagpipe Player (1624): "a single, larger-than-life-sized musician shown against a plain grayish ocher background". Watchable behaviour: one figure fills the frame, instrument raised, with nowhere else to look; the music is implied by the pose, not by a scene.

**Takeaway:** the musician is a portrait of an action, not a scene; remove the room and the player becomes monumental.

## M9. The ledge and the torque: a body behind a parapet, twisted slightly

What it is: the figure is placed behind a wooden ledge or parapet in unarticulated space, torqued slightly toward one side, lit by cool light from the left. Franits on St. Francis (c. 1618-19): "The positioning in an unarticulated space of the physically assertive, highly plastic saint behind a wooden ledge, torqued ever so slightly toward his right and enlivened by cool light entering in from the left, is strongly reminiscent of single-figure paintings by... Jusepe de Ribera." Honthorst's musicians posed before ledges (Singing Elder with a Flute, Merry Violinist with a Wineglass, both 1623) may in turn have been inspired by Baburen. Watchable behaviour: the ledge puts a barrier between viewer and figure, so the slight twist of the torso reads as a turn toward us across that barrier.

**Takeaway:** the ledge is a stage edge; the torque is the actor leaning out over it.

## M10. The outward gaze: a figure who looks back at the viewer

What it is: one figure in a crowded scene breaks the fourth wall and gazes directly out. In Christ Driving the Money Changers, "only one figure in Baburen's scene stands passively aloof: a woman at the right edge of the painting with a basket of doves on her head, who gazes directly at the viewer." In The Procuress the three figures are arranged as a transaction performed for the viewer, the procuress pointing to her palm. Watchable behaviour: while everything else in the scene panics or bargains, one calm face locks eyes with the viewer and holds the frame still.

**Takeaway:** the outward gaze is the anchor; it stops the chaos from spilling out of the picture.

## M11. The transaction gesture: coin, palm, and the price of the scene

What it is: the moral meaning of a genre scene is carried by a single hand gesture of exchange - a coin offered, an open palm inspected. The Procuress: "a man offering a coin for the services of a lute-playing prostitute while an old woman, the lady's procuress, inspects his money" (Wikipedia). The Thyssen notes "the moralising content of some of his compositions, such as The Procuress and The Backgammon Players... paved the way for the subsequent development of genre painting with a moralising undertone." In the Money Changers, a merchant "recoils in terror, grasping his money bag" while another "adjust[s] his pince-nez" - the pince-nez read by Franits (citing Alison Stewart) as "a cogent symbol of his (and his mercantile comrades') moral blindness". Watchable behaviour: the eye goes to the hands before the faces; the deal is the picture.

**Takeaway:** the hand is the plot; the coin is the smallest possible stage.

## M12. One cool light in the dark: the single candle, painted cold

What it is: artificial light appears exactly once in Baburen's entire oeuvre, and he paints it cold. In Youth Playing a Mouth Harp (1621), "a flickering flame of a smoking candle can be seen near the lower right edge of the canvas, rising from behind the sheet of music. The young man thus performs at night by candlelight in this the only surviving example of artificial lighting in our artist's entire oeuvre." Franits reads it as Baburen emulating Honthorst, "Gherardo delle notti", but "he substituted cooler and lighter tones for Honthorst's typical manner of capturing a candle's glow by deploying golden yellow gradations of decreasing intensity". Watchable behaviour: a small flame at the frame's edge, smoking, cool rather than golden; the musician is lit by something that barely counts as light.

**Takeaway:** one candle, used once, painted against type; restraint is the device, and the cool tone is the signature.

## M13. The borrowed upside-down body

What it is: a figure's pose is lifted wholesale from another painting and re-used in a new subject, even upside down. Prometheus Being Chained by Vulcan (1623) "adapts Caravaggio's upside-down figure of St. Paul from the Conversion of St. Paul (Santa Maria del Popolo, Rome) for the position of the fallen Prometheus" (Wikipedia, citing Brown). Similarly the Capture of Christ borrows the executioner-and-Matthew grip from Caravaggio's Martyrdom of St. Matthew in the Contarelli Chapel (Slatkes 1965, p. 48, via Franits n. 13). Watchable behaviour: a body in a pose that feels too contorted for its story, because the pose was designed for a different fall - a man struck from a horse, re-used as a Titan chained to a rock.

**Takeaway:** poses are reusable parts; the drama is in the transplant, not the invention.

## M14. Erudite subjects as status: painting the classics for princes

What it is: late Utrecht works take subjects of weighty literary content - Cimon and Pero (Roman Charity), Achilles before the Dead Body of Patroclus, Prometheus - that advertise the painter's education and social standing. Franits on Cimon and Pero: it "proffers erudite and sophisticated meditations on the function and meaning of painting as an art since it literally and deliberately recreates a lost work from antiquity". The Emperor Titus was one of twelve imaginary Roman emperors commissioned by Stadholder Maurits, alongside Rubens, Goltzius, Bloemaert, Moreelse and ter Brugghen - "Baburen was thus in very good company, a reflection, no doubt, of the esteem in which he was held by contemporary connoisseurs." Watchable behaviour: a brothel painter suddenly painting Roman Charity and emperors for a prince; the same broad brush, now applied to homework from Suetonius and Valerius Maximus.

**Takeaway:** subject matter is a social signal; the painter climbs by choosing harder stories.

## M15. The signature as imprimatur: "Theodor" on the canvas

What it is: in Utrecht, unlike in Rome, Baburen signs and dates an overwhelming percentage of his pictures, usually as "Theodor", "Teodoor" or a related spelling (variations on "Teodoro", his Italian name), and once as "Teodorus" in Latin. Franits: "For our painter's clients, his signature must have served as an imprimatur, signaling any number of qualities beyond the authenticity of the work in question (as a product of the master and/or his workshop), among them, his erudition, sophistication, and his international status gained from years of experience working in Rome". Watchable behaviour: the signature sits on the picture as a brand mark, not a scribble - the Italian name on a Dutch canvas, advertising eight years in Rome.

**Takeaway:** the signature is part of the painting's meaning; it sells the Roman years, not just the picture.

## Works

### Rome (c. 1612-1620/21)

W1. The Entombment (The Entombment of Christ). Date: 1617-1618; given as 1617 in most sources, c. 1617-19 in one recent essay. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: reported 220 x 140 cm; a second source gives 219 x 140 cm. NOT VERIFIED from a museum page in this leg; the 1 cm difference is unresolved here. Location: Pieta Chapel (Cappella della Pieta), Church of San Pietro in Montorio, Rome, in situ as the altarpiece. Commissioned by Pietro Cussida for his family chapel. What is observable: a large vertical altarpiece, indebted to Caravaggio's Entombment in the Vatican Museums but not a copy of it. The dead body of Christ is held diagonally, half-seated on the edge of the stone tomb, by St John and Nicodemus; the weight is the subject - John struggles to hold the body up so it does not slip, Nicodemus's knee supports the legs. Strong light falls on Christ's bare shoulder and on the faces of John and Nicodemus; parts of Christ's body are in shade, unlike Caravaggio's fully lit body. In the foreground, directly before the viewer, an overturned basket holds the tools of the Passion: hammer, tongs, nails, crown of thorns. The figures are placed around the tomb rather than on it. NOTE: described in the brief as the first northern Caravaggesque painting in a Roman church. That claim was NOT independently verified in this leg; do not repeat it as established (see C421). A nearly identical Entombment is in the Centraal Museum, Utrecht; its status (autograph replica, workshop, or copy) was NOT settled in this leg - see Separate list.

W2. The Capture of Christ (The Arrest of Christ; The Betrayal of Christ) - TWO VERSIONS, ONE ENTRY. Version A, with the Malchus episode: c. 1615-1616. Oil on canvas. Dimensions reported 125 x 95 cm. Location: Fondazione di Studi di Storia dell'Arte Roberto Longhi, Florence. Painted for Pietro Cussida; the earliest surviving work, per the Franits essay. Observable: the arrest in the Garden, with St Peter on the right severing the ear of Malchus - the Peter figure is a nod to the patron, Pietro Cussida. Version B, Borghese: c. 1616-1619 (Galleria Borghese official page gives c. 1616-1619; secondary sources give 1619; Slatkes/Franits propose c. 1619). Oil on canvas. Dimensions 139 x 202 cm, verified from the Galleria Borghese collection page. Inventory 028. Location: Galleria Borghese, Rome. Observable: a wide horizontal night arrest, closely following Caravaggio's Taking of Christ for Ciriaco Mattei (Dublin), with the kiss of Judas at the centre and soldiers closing in; figures in three-quarter profile, greater grace and harmony than Version A, per the Borghese catalogue entry. Attribution note: Version B was first attributed to Baburen by Roberto Longhi in 1926, rejecting an earlier Manfredi attribution by Venturi (1893). It did NOT enter the Borghese collection until 1787 (from the sculptor Bartolomeo Cavaceppi, in exchange for a monthly pension of 50 scudi), so it is not evidence Baburen worked for the Borghese in Rome - Franits explicitly pans that long-held notion (see C425).

W3. Christ Washing the Feet of the Apostles (Christ Washing the Feet of the Disciples). Date: before 1621; secondary sources give 1616, which is NOT confirmed by a museum page in this leg. Treat as Rome period, c. 1616-1620, date disputed. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: reported 199 x 297 cm. NOT VERIFIED from a museum page in this leg. Location: Gemaldegalerie, Berlin (Staatliche Museen zu Berlin). Commissioned by Marchese Vincenzo Giustiniani. What is observable: a very large horizontal history picture. Christ kneels to wash St Peter's foot; Peter sits at left, St John holds a towel, the other apostles look on. The large scale, the low viewpoint and the gesturing hands are Caravaggio-derived, filtered through Manfredi - Franits argues this picture and the Turin Money Changers draw directly from Manfredi. The St Peter face recurs in the Penitent St Peter (see Separate list).

W4. Christ on the Road to Calvary (The Way to Calvary). Date: 1617. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED in this leg. Location: Pieta Chapel, San Pietro in Montorio, Rome, in situ on a lateral wall of the chapel (the Entombment, W1, is the altarpiece; this is a side-wall canvas). Part of the same Cussida commission as W1. What is observable: Christ carrying the cross on the way to Calvary, with St Veronica offering her veil, in a large multi-figure procession. Sotheby's calls this picture, not the Entombment, "perhaps Baburen's masterpiece" and "the centerpiece of the chapel" - that conflicts with the Franits/Grilli account (Entombment as altarpiece, Way to Calvary as lateral wall). Do NOT repeat the Sotheby's "centerpiece" wording without resolving the conflict (see C422). The Raising of the Cross that hung on the opposite wall is lost - see Separate list.

W5. Christ Driving the Money Changers from the Temple - TWO VERSIONS, ONE ENTRY. Version A, Rome: c. 1618. Oil on canvas. Dimensions 170 x 217 cm, from the Robilant and Voena catalogue. Location in that catalogue: London-Milan, Robilant and Voena (dealer); earlier given as Turin, private collection, in the Franits review. Current location NOT VERIFIED in this leg. Observable: Christ wielding the lash at centre, a fleeing merchant immediately in front of him with left arm outstretched in terror, money changers seated at a table, a young woman with a basket on her head, monumental fluted columns of the Temple, an armed bravo at the far right. Manfredi's depiction of the subject is the direct model. Version B, Utrecht: signed and dated 1621. Oil on canvas. Dimensions 158 x 206 cm. Location: Schorr Collection, United Kingdom. Observable: same design reworked after the return to Utrecht - the Christ pose and the fleeing merchant are carried over, the woman with the basket is omitted, the bravo at the far right is larger in proportion to the canvas and more monumental, the seated money changers are retained. Date/version note: a third figure, 174 x 220 cm, circulates in reproduction databases for this title; it matches neither version above and was NOT used here. Do not quote it. This pair is the clearest proof that Manfredi remained a source after the return to Utrecht.

### Utrecht, first genre years (1621-1622)

W6. Youth Playing a Small Whistle (Young Man with a Jew's Harp; The Boy with a Mouth Harp). Date: 1621. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 65 x 53 cm. Location: Centraal Museum, Utrecht. What is observable: a single half-length young man, close to the picture plane, playing a small mouth instrument (called a Jew's harp / mouth harp / small whistle in different sources - the instrument name is a title problem, not two pictures). He is caught in a private rhythmic trance, attention entirely on the instrument. Google Arts and Culture calls this "the first in a series of isolated musicians painted in half-length, which the Utrecht Caravaggisti introduced to the north of Europe" and "generally accepted" as the first work Baburen made on returning home. The off-the-shoulder garment with a blue striped sleeve recurs in Laughing Democritus (W20) and in the Cleveland Violin Player (W18). Title warning: Wikipedia lists it as "Youth Playing a Small Whistle 1621"; museum / Google Arts sources call the same Centraal picture "Young man with jew's harp". Treat as one picture with competing titles, not two pictures.

W7. The Procuress (The Matchmaker). Date: 1622 (signed and dated 1622; Boston version is the original, rediscovered 1949, published by Gowing 1951). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 101.6 x 107.6 cm (40.0 x 42.4 in). Location: Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. Accession number 50.2721. What is observable: three half-length figures pressed close to the picture plane against a flat dark background, no deep space. Left: a young woman playing the lute (the prostitute). Centre: the male client, arm around her, holding a coin between his fingers. Right: the old procuress, pointing to her palm to indicate she expects payment, inspecting the coin. The lute is central; the hands and the coin are the transaction. The composition is claustrophobic by design; the viewer is made an accomplice. This is the bordeeltje (brothel scene) type, and the best-known Baburen. Afterlife: this picture, or a copy of it, was owned by Maria Thins, Johannes Vermeer's mother-in-law, and appears in the background of two Vermeer paintings, The Concert (stolen from the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum, Boston) and Woman Seated at a Virginal (National Gallery, London). Han van Meegeren forged a copy of it. At least three versions exist: Boston (original), Rijksmuseum (copy after 1623, 100 x 95 cm, harsh quality, NOT by Baburen - see Separate list), and Courtauld (17th-century, long thought a forgery, now confirmed as a genuine 17th-century version, possibly the one that hung in Vermeer's house).

W8. Christ Among the Doctors (Jesus Among the Doctors). Date: 1622 (Oslo prime version signed and dated 1622). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: Oslo prime version now 190 x 210 cm, after a 24 cm strip was added along the top; originally c. 166 cm high, so originally about 166 x 210 cm. The added strip is stated in the Sotheby's catalogue note for the second version. Location: Nasjonalmuseet (National Gallery of Norway), Oslo. Object NG.M.00109. Bought by the National Gallery from Johan Christian Dahl in 1843. What is observable: a densely packed group of temple elders, standing and sitting around the twelve-year-old Jesus, portrayed through arm gestures, draped garb and puzzled gazes. The elders are slightly caricatured and placed at the very front of the picture plane, so the division between their world and ours is almost suspended. Jesus stands further into the room; Mary and Joseph are discernible behind him. Dramatically focused lighting, gesticulating hands, and bare unclean feet are Caravaggio-derived. One of the few religious subjects Baburen painted after the return to Utrecht, and close in style to the Roman pictures. Second version: a second autograph version, formerly Mansi Collection, Lucca, sold Sotheby's New York, 29 January 2010, lot 277. Franits and Slatkes examined it firsthand and call it the second autograph version. Its signature/date are NOT visible now; sources record the Mansi picture as signed "T.D. Babueren anno 1622", but no trace of signature or date remains on the present canvas, so the Mansi identification is not definite. Dimensions of the second version were NOT stated on the Sotheby's page read in this leg (only "almost identical" to Oslo). A canvas of 165.7 x 207.6 cm titled Jesus Among the Doctors is listed at the Agnes Etherington Art Centre, Kingston, accession 14-033, as "Maker Once Known; Baburen", dated "1620 v. 1650" - that record was NOT verified as the Sotheby's second version in this leg; do not merge the two records.

W9. The Lute Player (Singing Man with Lute). Date: 1622. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 71.2 x 58.5 cm (Google Arts and Culture: w 58.5 x h 71.2 cm). Location: Centraal Museum, Utrecht, since 1955. What is observable: a single half-length man, singing with mouth open, lute pointed towards the viewer, right at the edge of the frame against a dark ground. The figure breaks the fourth wall: he is not a distant figure but a performer addressing the viewer. Baburen drew on Caravaggio's Lute Player and was, with this picture, one of the first to bring the Italian half-length musician into the northern Netherlands; Frans Hals's Lute Player of 1623 follows from it, as do ter Brugghen's lute players. Title note - this covers the brief's "Singing Lute Player" and "A Man Playing the Lute": those phrases are alternate titles / descriptions of this picture (and of the related pictures below), not separate verified Baburen pictures in this leg. No separate picture titled "A Man Playing the Lute" with its own museum location and dimensions was verified. See also W14 (a woman playing the lute) and the Separate list (A Lute Player and a Singer, Christie's 2012).

W10. Young Man Singing. Date: 1622 (signed 1622). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 71 x 58 cm. Location: Stadel Museum, Frankfurt (Stadelsches Kunstinstitut). Acquired late 2007 with support from the Kulturstiftung der Lander, Hessische Kulturstiftung and Stadelscher Museums-Verein. What is observable: a young singer in a feathered beret, head thrown back, holding a music book in his left hand, right hand underscoring the performance with a theatrical gesture, fixing the viewer - his audience - out of the corner of his eye. Exposed shoulder, neck muscles tensing from singing, tanned face lit by a strong glare. The Stadel description calls it a virtuoso sample of Baroque histrionics in a single half-figure. Do not confuse with W9: near-identical dimensions (71 x 58 vs 71.2 x 58.5), different picture, different city, singer without lute, with a music book.

W11. The Backgammon Players. Date: c. 1622. Medium: oil on canvas (medium NOT VERIFIED from a museum page in this leg; oil on canvas is the standard assumption in secondary lists). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED in this leg. No dimension was found in any search in this leg. Location: Bamberg. Sources disagree on the institution name: older lists give Residenzgalerie, Bamberg; Wikipedia gives Historisches Museum, Bamberg. Both may refer to the same Bamberg collection under different names, but that was NOT verified in this leg. Flag the discrepancy; do not pick one as certain. What is observable: NOT verified first-hand in this leg. No image description from a museum page was retrieved. Secondary lists place it among the moralising genre pictures: players at a board game, in the same moralising-company vein as The Procuress (W7) and Loose Company (W14). The Thyssen-Bornemisza notes, cited in search, call The Backgammon Players (New York, private collection) a moralising composition that paved the way for later genre painting - that "New York, private collection" location conflicts with Bamberg and may be a different version or a different picture; flag it, do not resolve it here. Inspect the Bamberg picture before describing it in detail.

### Mature and late Utrecht (1623 - February 1624)

W12. Granida and Daifilo. Date: 1623 (signed and dated lower right "T.D. Baburen fecit Ano 163" [1623]). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 165.7 x 211.5 cm (Sotheby's, 2009 and 2020 sales, same picture). Location: private collection. Commissioned by Peter van Hardenbroeck (1593-1656/58), Utrecht, in 1623; bequeathed by him to his lawyer Jan de Wijs; later private collection, Toulouse, 1941, and later private collection, New York. What is observable: a monumental canvas. The shepherd Daifilo kneels before the Persian princess Granida, in the third scene of Act One of Pieter Cornelisz. Hooft's pastoral play Granida (manuscript 1605, first published 1615). Daifilo kneels in supplication; Granida stands above him, in rich dress. The figures are depicted far larger, relatively speaking, than in later Granida pictures. Sotheby's and Franits call this the earliest treatment of the subject in western art, and the first painting of a theme that then became a staple of Dutch pastoral painting (at least 32 Dutch pictures of the meeting followed). It is also, on written evidence, a portrait historie: van Hardenbroeck had himself painted as Daifilo - one of the earliest examples of pastoral portraiture, and the only Granida picture that can unequivocally be called a portrait historie. Other versions: Baburen repeated the composition, or parts of it, on at least two further occasions. The only intact other version is unsigned and predominantly studio work, Musee Royal des Beaux-Arts / Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium, Brussels, 167 x 209.5 cm, catalogued as "Dirck van Baburen his workshop". A fragment, Granida alone, oil on canvas laid down on board, 122.8 x 78.1 cm, was sold Bonhams, London, 8 July 2020, lot 19.

W13. The Concert - TWO VERSIONS, ONE ENTRY. Date: c. 1623. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: Detroit prime version 105.4 x 133.3 cm (41 1/2 x 52 1/2 in), unframed, verified from the Detroit Institute of Arts page. Hermitage version dimensions NOT VERIFIED in this leg. Location: Detroit Institute of Arts, accession 2010.17, Museum Purchase, Robert H. Tannahill Foundation Fund. The second autograph version is in the State Hermitage Museum, St Petersburg, in the collection since the 18th century. What is observable: four figures engaged in musical performance around a table - lute, violin, guitar, books, and a vase of flowers on the table. The Detroit version (formerly called a studio replica) was re-examined by Franits after cleaning in 2008: he now calls it the prime version, with pentimenti (including a small area upper right used to wipe brushes) and a later-added strip of canvas at the bottom that was removed on cleaning, bringing its size close to the Hermitage version. The Hermitage version has no pentimenti; Franits calls it a second autograph version, effectively a replica by Baburen himself. The HNA review supports Detroit primacy and calls the Hermitage version a workshop replica - the two accounts differ on the Hermitage picture's status (autograph replica vs workshop replica); flag that. Note: the two versions date from the period when Baburen was likely operating a joint studio with ter Brugghen, per Franits via Adam Williams. That joint-studio hypothesis is now doubted by Franits himself for other pictures - see W20.

W14. Loose Company (The Prodigal Son). Date: 1623. Medium: oil on canvas (medium NOT VERIFIED from a museum page in this leg). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED in this leg. Location: Gemaldegalerie, Mainz (Mittelrheinisches Landesmuseum, Mainz). What is observable: NOT verified first-hand from a museum page in this leg. The picture is also titled The Prodigal Son: a merry company / loose company scene, with musicians, in the moralising genre vein. A figure caption calls a detail "Dirck van Baburen: The Prodigal Son (detail), 1623, (Mainz, Mittelrheinisches Landesmuseum)" and shows violin-family instruments. Do not describe a prodigal-son narrative (father, return, feast) as if verified, because the Mainz title in Wikipedia is Loose Company and the Prodigal Son identification comes only from a figure caption in this leg. Inspect the Mainz picture before describing its figures.

W15. Prometheus Being Chained by Vulcan (The Chaining of Prometheus). Date: 1623. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 202 x 184 cm. Location: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam. What is observable: a monumental mythological scene in a dark interior with reddish walls and dense shadow. Prometheus lies fallen, upside-down, his body adapted from Caravaggio's upside-down St Paul in the Conversion of St Paul (Santa Maria del Popolo, Rome). Vulcan, the club-footed blacksmith god, chains him, watched by Mercury, messenger of the gods. The figures are life-size or larger, sunburned hands and faces, dramatic illumination borrowed from Caravaggio. The picture bears two signatures: a clear one below Prometheus's right shoulder, and a second discovered at lower left during restoration. A painting Adam and Eve, also by Baburen, was sold at auction in 1707 together with the Prometheus; the two may have formed a pair, both illustrations of creation - conjecture only, flag it. This is the textbook Utrecht Caravaggesque history picture: classical subject, contemporary bodies, raking light, no idealisation.

W16. Cimon and Pero (Roman Charity). Date: c. 1623 (Wikipedia); the Art Fund record gives 1618-1628, which is a cataloguing range, not a date - use c. 1623 and flag the range. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 127.6 x 151.1 cm, from the Art Fund record. Location: York Art Gallery (York Museums Trust), inventory number 788 in the Christie's note (the Art Fund record does not repeat the number; the 788 comes from Christie's, lot note for A Woman Playing the Lute). Bequeathed to York by F. D. Lycett Green in 1955, through the Art Fund, part of a collection of 117 works. What is observable: Pero, a young woman, kneels and offers her breast to her aged father Cimon, imprisoned and starving, in the Roman Charity story from Valerius Maximus. The facial type of Pero is compared by Slatkes to the central female in Lot and His Daughters (1622, see Separate list) and to the woman lute player in W14-related pictures. Half-length or three-quarter figures, close to the picture plane, lit against darkness; the tenderness of the act against the grim prison setting is the point.

W17. The Crowning with Thorns (Christ Crowned with Thorns; The Mocking of Christ) - TWO 1623 VERSIONS, ONE ENTRY. Date: 1623 for the two versions listed below. A Munich version is dated 1621 (see below). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED for either 1623 version in this leg. Location: version 1, Museum Catharijneconvent, Utrecht; version 2, Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art, Kansas City, Missouri. Both listed in Wikipedia's Works list as Crowning with Thorns, 1623. What is observable: Christ mocked and crowned with thorns, surrounded by soldiers - the Passion mocking scene. First-hand description of either 1623 version was NOT retrieved in this leg; do not invent the figure count or the staging. The Munich picture, The Crowning with Thorns, 1621, oil on canvas, 106 x 136 cm, Alte Pinakothek, Munich (dimensions from a secondary catalogue record, NOT VERIFIED from the museum), is a related earlier version or variant of the same composition; whether it is autograph, a workshop version, or a distinct design was NOT settled in this leg. The Pieta Chapel lunette titled Mocking of Christ is a different picture and is attributed to David de Haen - see Separate list. Do not confuse the two.

W18. The Violin Player (Violin Player with a Wine Glass; Merry Violinist). Date: 1623. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 80.4 x 67.1 cm. Location: Cleveland Museum of Art. Acquired 2018; presented in gallery 213 from December 2018. What is observable: a half-length violin player at life size, close to the picture plane, in colourful dress marking him as a marginal figure - bold gaze, unshaven face, cheeky grin with a broken tooth, holding a violin in one hand and a wine glass (roemer) in the other. Cleveland's curator notes "confident brushwork and characteristic cool, bright tonalities" - a late-Utrecht palette note, cooler and brighter than the Roman pictures. The off-the-shoulder fanciful garment (all'antica) links this picture to W6 and W20.

W19. The Flute Player (Musician Playing the Flute). Date: NOT VERIFIED. Secondary reproduction databases give a range "between 1615 and 1624" only. Do not date it 1622 or 1623. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED in this leg. Location: Gemaldegalerie, Berlin (Staatliche Museen zu Berlin). Inventory KFMV.291. What is observable: a man in a black hat with a single white feather, holding a flute, with a book, in half-length against darkness. First-hand description was NOT retrieved from the museum in this leg. Attribution warning - this is the key point: Franits, via the HNA review, calls the Berlin Fluteplayer a workshop replica of a lost original, and downgrades it from the authentic list. It is therefore included here as a catalogue entry ONLY because the brief required the Flute Player to be verified first-hand, and the verification result is that the Berlin picture is NOT accepted as autograph. Do not use it as evidence for Baburen's own handling. The Flute and Fife Players connection: NO picture by Baburen titled "The Flute and Fife Players" was found in this leg. The closest material is: (a) this Berlin Flute Player; (b) a lost original of a flute / recorder player, known only through engravings - Cornelis Bloemaert II engraved a Flute Player after Baburen, image 16 x 11 cm, print dated 1625 (after Baburen's death), and a Recorder Player in the same tradition, with a Dutch motto about the flute sounding sweet but an old woman's throat sounding awful; (c) a Duet recorded by Slatkes as a copy after a lost original, in which a boy playing a flute was added in the background by the copyist - the Berlin copy lacks compositional unity, per Christie's, suggesting the extra flute boy was the copyist's invention, not Baburen's. Do NOT present a "Flute and Fife Players" as a Baburen picture (see C424). If the phrase comes from ter Brugghen (whose Flute Player / fife pictures are a separate matter), say so.

W20. Laughing Democritus and Weeping Heraclitus - PENDANT PAIR, ONE ENTRY. Date: 1622, both pictures inscribed "T. B. fecit Ano 1622". Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: Laughing Democritus 72 x 58.5 cm (Bijl-Van-Urk: 72 x 58.5 cm; Moiso-Diekamp gives 72 x 59 cm - the 0.5 cm difference is a rounding / measuring difference, flag it). Weeping Heraclitus nearly identical in size, per Franits; its exact dimensions were NOT VERIFIED in this leg. Location: Laughing Democritus, private collection (surfaced on the Swiss art market, spring 2020; Koller, Zurich, 19 June 2020, lot 3057, as by the workshop of Baburen; earlier Minister Fredrik Due Collection and Axel Wenner-Gren Collection, Stockholm; Schaffer Galleries, New York, 1961; Sotheby's London, 24 March 1965, lot 31, as by ter Brugghen). Weeping Heraclitus location NOT VERIFIED in this leg. What is observable: Laughing Democritus is a half-length old philosopher, abruptly truncated at the edges of the canvas, laughing manically at the follies of the world, in a fanciful off-the-shoulder garment with a blue striped sleeve, lit by raking light. Delicate peach-coloured tones articulate streaming light on the white cloth under his hat and on his shoulders - a parallel Franits draws with the undergarments in Prometheus (W15). Pentimenti are visible along the thumb and index finger of the philosopher's right hand and along the left edge of his hat, revealed on cleaning. Weeping Heraclitus is the complementary pendant: the same format, the philosopher weeping at the same follies. The pair's subject comes from Cicero and other Roman sources: Democritus laughed at the world, Heraclitus wept. Attribution history - the 1622 inscribed pair question: Slatkes (1965) listed both as by the workshop of Baburen (cat. D3); Nicolson called them by an assistant of Baburen; Slatkes and Franits (2007, ter Brugghen monograph) and Franits (2013, Baburen monograph, cat. WBTB3) placed them in a supposed joint workshop of ter Brugghen and Baburen. Franits has now changed his mind: in the Bijl-Van-Urk essay (after seeing the cleaned Democritus in colour in 2019-2020) he confirms Laughing Democritus as authentic Baburen, monogrammed and dated 1622, with "all the hallmarks of Baburen's influential style as it evolved after his return to Utrecht". He also states he no longer believes the two masters shared a studio, citing Julia van den Burg's 2009 Amsterdam thesis challenging the joint-workshop hypothesis, and noting that ter Brugghen and Honthorst seem to have shared more motifs than ter Brugghen and Baburen did. The pair are therefore now best described as Baburen, 1622, earliest known pendant depictions of Laughing Democritus and Weeping Heraclitus in Utrecht (ter Brugghen painted the pair later, 1628, Rijksmuseum). A copy of Laughing Democritus, 72.4 x 58.5 cm, is in the George W. Carruthers Collection, Gisborne, New Zealand; Franits calls it a later copy because it lacks monogram and date. Note: the inscription "T. B. fecit" is Theodor Baburen (Baburen signed as T. Baburen / Theod. Bab.), not ter Brugghen.

W21. Achilles Before the Dead Body of Patroclus (Achilles Preparing to Avenge the Death of Patroclus; Achilles Spurred into Battle by the Death of Patroclus). Date: 1624, signed and dated "Baburen F 1624" on the belt, lower left (indistinctly). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 204 x 287 cm (80 3/8 x 113 in). Location: DISPUTED in this leg. The Franits review (HNA) says the painting was "recently acquired by the Kassel Gemaldegalerie as Achilles before the Dead Body of Patroclus". An auction record (Invaluable, from a sale catalogue) lists the same picture, same dimensions and signature, as a private picture exhibited at the Centraal Museum, Utrecht, on loan 1998-2000, and calls it "Dirck van Baburen's largest painting and most likely the last work he ever produced". No current-location page for Kassel was read in this leg. Use Kassel only with the Franits-review citation and a flag; do not state it as settled without a Kassel collection check. What is observable: a huge horizontal history picture, the largest Baburen known. Achilles, in armour, prepares to avenge Patroclus, whose dead body lies before him. The scale (over 2 x 2.8 metres) and the 1624 date, in the month of the artist's death in February 1624, make it the natural closing picture of the oeuvre. Franits and Haverkamp-Begemann identify the subject as Achilles before the dead body of Patroclus, resolving an earlier controversial subject identification. This picture is essential to the late phase: it proves Baburen returned to monumental multi-figure history painting at the very end, at a scale larger than anything in Rome.

## Evolution

### Phase 1 - Rome, early: arrival to the Pieta Chapel, c. 1612-1617

Scale: small to medium religious pictures for private patrons (Cussida, Giustiniani), then the first large church commission. Palette: dark grounds, warm browns, deep shadow, flesh lit hard from one side. Italian black-grounded canvas is noted for the woman lute player; the Roman pictures are built on dark preparation. Light: Caravaggio's tenebrism learned second-hand, above all through Jusepe de Ribera and Bartolomeo Manfredi, not by copying Caravaggio directly. Franits stresses Ribera for Archimedes, St Francis and Pan, and Manfredi for the Money Changers and the Washing of the Feet. The Borghese Capture responds to Caravaggio "through the lens of Manfredi". Subject: almost entirely religious - arrest, washing of feet, saints, Francis, Sebastian. No genre, no musicians yet. What changes in this phase: Baburen moves from a competent follower of Moreelse's Utrecht portrait manner (his training) to a Roman Caravaggesque history painter who can carry a chapel. The Entombment (W1) is the hinge: a northern painter answering Caravaggio's Vatican Entombment on a church wall in Rome, with the body weight and the Passion tools in the foreground as his own additions.

### Phase 2 - Rome, mature: the Pieta Chapel and the great patrons, 1617-1620/21

Scale: large and monumental. Entombment 220 x 140 cm; Washing of the Feet reported 199 x 297 cm; Borghese Capture 139 x 202 cm; Money Changers Rome 170 x 217 cm. These are altarpiece and palace scale, not cabinet pictures. Palette: still dark, but with more saturated drapery and more varied flesh; the figures are larger in proportion to the canvas than in Phase 1. Light: raking, single-source, theatrical. Hands become a language: gesticulating elders, Christ's pointing hand, Peter's severing hand, the money changers' outstretched arms. The St Peter face is reused across pictures (Washing of the Feet, Penitent St Peter). Subject: Passion cycles for a chapel (Entombment, Way to Calvary, Raising of the Cross, lunettes with De Haen), plus palace pictures for Giustiniani and (via later collection history, not direct patronage) the Borghese circle. Classicising painters - Raphael, Reni, Domenichino - are also in play, per Franits, so this is not pure Caravaggism. What changes: Baburen is no longer borrowing a style; he is running a chapel decoration with a collaborator (De Haen), repeating compositions in second versions, and reworking a design (Money Changers) for a new patron. The workshop habit of prime version + replica starts here and continues in Utrecht.

### Phase 3 - Return to Utrecht, first genre years, 1621-1622

Scale: sudden contraction. The single musicians are 65 x 53 cm (W6), 71 x 58 cm (W10), 71.2 x 58.5 cm (W9) - cabinet scale, a tenth of the area of the Washing of the Feet. The Procuress (101.6 x 107.6 cm) and Christ Among the Doctors (c. 166 x 210 cm originally) are the exceptions that prove the rule: religious history stays large, genre goes small and close. Palette: cooler and brighter in the late pictures (Cleveland's phrase for the Violin Player), with peach-coloured light on white cloth, blue striped sleeves, and broad flat planes of colour in stylised garments. The brushwork becomes vigorous and schematic, the figures more expansive and earthy than their Italian antecedents (Franits on Democritus). Light: the same raking light, now used on a single half-length figure truncated at the canvas edge, so the figure's plasticity is enhanced and the viewer becomes the audience. The dark background is now flat and blank, with no architecture at all. Subject: the invention. Baburen is one of the first to bring the Italian half-length musician to the north, and one of the first to paint the Procuress, the Backgammon Players, and the loose company as moralising genre. He also invents literary history subjects in this phase: Democritus / Heraclitus (W20, earliest Utrecht pendants of the pair) and, in 1623, Granida (W12). What changes: the audience changes. In Rome the viewer is a worshipper before an altarpiece; in Utrecht the viewer is an accomplice before a brothel transaction, or the audience for a singer who looks out of the picture. The half-length, close-to-the-plane format is the mechanism, and Hals and ter Brugghen take it up immediately.

### Phase 4 - Mature and late Utrecht, 1623 - February 1624

Scale: expansion again, in both directions at once. Small musicians continue (Violin Player, 80.4 x 67.1 cm), but history returns at monumental scale: Prometheus 202 x 184 cm, Granida 165.7 x 211.5 cm, Achilles 204 x 287 cm - the largest picture of the career, signed and dated in the year of death. Palette: warm golds, browns and ochres in the Concert, cool bright tonalities in the Violin Player, sunburned flesh in Prometheus. The Utrecht pictures are more colourful and more deliberately costumed (all'antica dress, feathered berets) than the Roman pictures. Light: still single-source and raking, but now serving large multi-figure compositions with complex overlapping - Prometheus's upside-down body, Achilles's armour, Granida's kneeling shepherd - rather than single figures. Subject: literary and classical history takes over from the Bible: Prometheus, Granida and Daifilo from Hooft, Achilles and Patroclus from Homer, Democritus and Heraclitus from Cicero, Cimon and Pero from Valerius Maximus. Baburen is, in Franits's phrase, an iconographic innovator in these years: first Granida, first Utrecht Democritus / Heraclitus pendants, a St Francis without stigmata, an Achilles whose subject had to be re-identified by scholars. What changes, and what does not: the Caravaggesque devices (half-length truncation, raking light, close plane, earthy bodies) are not abandoned; they are transferred from genre into history painting. The late pictures are history paintings painted with genre-painting means. Death in February 1624 cuts this phase off after about a year: the Achilles is most likely the last work, and the career is about twelve years long, with only 42 authentic works in Franits's catalogue (36 per the HNA review - see C419).

## Aesthetic logic

Baburen's career is a twelve-year argument about scale, and the argument reverses itself twice. He starts small and borrowed, in a Roman chapel, answering Caravaggio with a wedge and a dead weight: the Entombment is a northern painter proving he can carry a church wall, and the proof is physical - the body is heavy, the stone is a table, the tools of the Passion lie in the foreground like evidence in a trial. The light is the prosecution's lamp. It says: look how much this weighs.

Then he comes home, and the scale collapses. The Utrecht pictures of 1621-22 are a tenth of the area of the Roman altarpieces, and the collapse is the invention. A single musician at 65 x 53 cm, a brothel transaction at 101 x 107 cm: the viewer stops being a worshipper at a distance and becomes the person the singer is singing to, the accomplice the procuress is pointing at. The crop is the whole argument. Baburen understood, before Hals and before ter Brugghen's 1624 turn to musicians, that the half-length figure against a blank ground is not a smaller history painting but a different kind of picture altogether - a portrait of an action, with the viewer cast in the second role. The Procuress works because the coin is between two hands and the third hand is pointing at the viewer's pocket; the Lute Player works because the mouth is open on a note aimed out of the frame. The room is gone because the room would give the viewer somewhere to stand that is not inside the deal.

Then, in the last year, the scale expands again, and this is the part of the career that makes the first two parts legible. Prometheus at 202 x 184 cm, Granida at 165 x 211 cm, Achilles at 204 x 287 cm: the monumental history painting returns, but it returns painted with genre means. The bodies are still earthy, the faces still schematic with their pink dashes, the light still raking from one side, the crop still tight - only now the subject is Homer and Hooft instead of a mouth harp. The late pictures are the proof that the genre years were not a detour: Baburen learned in the small pictures how to make a single body fill a frame, and then he applied that lesson to a Titan and an army. The Achilles, signed and dated in the month he died, is a history painting that behaves like a musician picture - one armoured figure, one dead body, no room, no landscape, the whole story in the torque of a torso.

That is why the joint-workshop question matters and why its collapse matters more. Slatkes built a whole category - 19 pictures, a shared studio - out of the fact that Baburen's and ter Brugghen's hands cannot always be told apart in 1621-24. Franits, who inherited Slatkes's catalogue, has now abandoned the category: the hands merge because the two men were painting the same new thing at the same time in the same small city, not because they shared a lease. The laboratory Franits describes - "creative rivalries and competition", motifs passing back and forth - is the truer account, and it is also the more interesting one for the pictures: the Flute and Fife Players are "so indebted to Van Baburen" not because ter Brugghen sat at his bench but because Baburen had just invented the format ter Brugghen needed, and ter Brugghen, who had been painting large dark histories for six years, recognised it at once. Influence here is not theft and not collaboration; it is two men solving the same problem in the same winter, one of them three years from death.

And then there is the silence at the end, which is part of the logic too. No pupils are documented. No named sitters. No cause of death. The musicians are probably self-portraits - the same man, again and again, playing for the viewer - which means the only face Baburen certainly painted is his own, and he painted it as a marginal figure in theatrical dress, singing with his mouth open. The signature says "Theodor", the Italian name, on a Dutch canvas: the brand of eight Roman years. He sold the Roman years the way he sold the pictures, and the pictures sold because the Roman years were in them - the raking light, the borrowed body, the coin in the hand. He died at about thirty with the largest picture of his career still wet, or nearly so, and the career reads, in the end, as a single continuous gesture: make the picture smaller until the viewer is inside it, then make it enormous without letting the viewer out.

## Positions

**P1.** Born c. 1592/93, probably in Wijk bij Duurstede (disputed against c. 1595; see Site).

**P2.** Family moved to Utrecht around 1599; pupil of Paulus Moreelse, with a 1611 Guild of St Luke tuition receipt as the earliest archival reference (training probably began c. 1607/08).

**P3.** Summer or fall 1612, if not early 1613, arrives in Italy at about nineteen; spends about eight or nine years there, mostly in Rome.

**P4.** 1615, earliest surviving work, the Capture of Christ for Pietro Cussida; 1615, documented altarpiece of the Martyrdom of St Sebastian for Santa Maria dei Servi, Parma (untraced).

**P5.** 1617-1618, the Pieta Chapel in San Pietro in Montorio, Rome: the Entombment as altarpiece, the Way to Calvary on a lateral wall, the Raising of the Cross (lost) opposite; the lunettes are David de Haen's, not Baburen's.

**P6.** Fall 1620 or, more likely, winter 1621, returns to Utrecht; his Utrecht career lasts about three years.

**P7.** 1621, Youth Playing a Mouth Harp and the first single-figure musicians; the 1621 Utrecht Money Changers reworks the Roman design.

**P8.** 1622, The Procuress (Boston, signed and dated), The Lute Player, Young Man Singing, Christ Among the Doctors, Laughing Democritus and Weeping Heraclitus (inscribed 1622) - the most productive single year of the career.

**P9.** 1623, Granida and Daifilo (commissioned by Pieter van Hardenbroeck), Prometheus Being Chained by Vulcan, Cimon and Pero, The Concert, The Crowning with Thorns (two versions), The Violin Player, Loose Company.

**P10.** 1624, Achilles Before the Dead Body of Patroclus, signed and dated 1624, the largest picture of the career (204 x 287 cm) and most likely the last work.

**P11.** 21 February 1624, dies in Utrecht, aged about 30 or 31; buried 28 February 1624 in the Buurkerk; a bachelor, his mother and sister heirs; cause of death not documented.

**P12.** c. 1629, Constantijn Huygens notes him as one of the important Dutch painters of the early decades of the century; then largely forgotten until Slatkes 1965 and Franits 2013.

### Patrons (Rome, Utrecht, Delft)

Pietro Cussida (also Pietro Cuside, Pedro Cossida; died October 1622), Spanish diplomat, art agent and collector for Philip III (and briefly Philip IV). Philip III of Spain (ruled 1598-1621) and Philip IV (ruled 1621-1665), Cussida's masters; the Spanish crown was the ultimate patron of the Pieta Chapel. Pieter van Hardenbroeck (1593-1656/58), Utrecht nobleman who commissioned Granida and Daifilo in 1623 and had himself painted as Daifilo; bequeathed it to his lawyer Jan de Wijs. ### Adjacent artists (3, evidenced)

**Abraham Bloemaert (1566-1651): the teacher who made the Utrecht school that Baburen joined.** Bloemaert taught Hendrick ter Brugghen, Gerrit van Honthorst and Jan van Bijlert - the three men Baburen worked beside after his return - and in 1612 founded the Utrecht drawing academy together with Baburen's own teacher Paulus Moreelse. He was, with Wtewael and Moreelse, one of the three leading Utrecht painters of his generation, and he never went to Italy: he absorbed Caravaggism second-hand, through the pupils who came home from Rome. The National Gallery notes he "rapidly assimilated the new ideas his pupils brought back from Italy", and Rubens and Elizabeth Stuart visited his studio. His own career runs the other way from Baburen's: late Mannerism (Death of the Children of Niobe, 1591) to a classicising Baroque, plus a famous teaching manual (Tekenboek, published by his son Frederick in 1650) that was still used for Utrecht training into the 19th century. Top pick for #531.

**Jusepe de Ribera (1591-1652): the Spaniard who shared Baburen's patron and taught him his saints.** Franits names Ribera as a particularly impactful visual guide for Baburen - a chief source for the Archimedes, the St. Francis (Vienna) and Pan Holding a Syrinx - and both men were patronized by the same man, Pietro Cussida, who commissioned Ribera's Five Senses (1615-16) in the same years he commissioned Baburen's Pieta Chapel. Ribera was in Rome c. 1612-1616, overlapping Baburen's arrival, living in the Via Margutta and associating with the northern Caravaggisti including Honthorst and ter Brugghen; he moved to Naples in 1616, married Caterina Azzolino, daughter of the painter Giovanni Bernardino Azzolino, and became the city's leading painter under the Spanish viceroys. He signed himself "Jusepe de Ribera, espanol" throughout his career.

**Gerard Douffet (1594-1660/61): the most direct documented Roman contact of Baburen's after de Haen and Manfredi.** The Web Gallery of Art biography states Douffet "must have moved in the circles of Bartolomeo Manfredi and Nicolas Tournier and known other northern artists in the city such as Dirck van Baburen and David de Haen", was documented in Valentin de Boulogne's studio in 1620-22, and lived in the same Sant'Andrea delle Fratte parish where Baburen and de Haen lodged. Christie's notes the influence of Valentin, Baburen and Manfredi predominates in his Ecce Homo, and that the picture was attributed to Caravaggio in the 1812 and 1954 sales. He took the restrained Caravaggism home to Liege, became official painter to the Prince-Bishop in 1634, and was documented in Utrecht in 1650 after exile in 1648. WARNING: the old claim that he worked in Rubens's Antwerp studio in 1612-14 is "very doubtful" per the Web Gallery of Art (a painting dated 1615 is quite unlike Rubens).

New from #530: Adam Elsheimer (Frankfurt, baptised 18 March 1578 - Rome 11 December 1610; as commonly cited, not verified from a primary source) - German painter in Rome, master of multi-source night light on copper. Elsheimer arrived in Rome in early 1600, the year Caravaggio's revolution broke, and answered it not with tenebrism but with up to five gentle light sources on a copper plate the size of a hand - moonlight, torchlight, firelight and starlight in one picture. His Flight into Egypt (1609) is one of the first realistic night skies in European painting. He influenced Rubens, Rembrandt, Claude Lorrain, Paul Bril, Jan Pynas, Leonaert Bramer and Pieter Lastman, and through Lastman the whole Amsterdam history school - the light-line that runs parallel to Baburen's dark-line and meets it again in Rembrandt. He died at 32, the same age as Baburen, and his friend Rubens mourned him in print. The doorway is the other northerner in Rome while Baburen was still a boy in Utrecht, and the one whose light Baburen never learned. - C423. The Baburen Calling of St Matthew does not exist. The brief asked to compare Baburen's two Callings with ter Brugghen's two Callings. Leg 2 found no painting titled The Calling of St Matthew by Baburen in any source consulted - only Caravaggio's, ter Brugghen's two versions, and Marinus van Reymerswaele's. The study does NOT list a Baburen Calling; the ter Brugghen comparison is used as a contrast (ter Brugghen's 1621 second Calling is the exact move Baburen makes in the single-musician pictures), not a parallel. Possibilities not asserted: the brief conflates Baburen with ter Brugghen; a Baburen Calling exists under another title in Franits; or the Money Changers (W5), informed by Caravaggio's Calling, was misremembered as a Calling.
- C424. "The Flute and Fife Players" as a Baburen title does not exist. Leg 2 found no Baburen picture with that title. The closest material is the Berlin Flute Player (W19), which Franits calls a workshop replica of a lost original; a lost original known only through Cornelis Bloemaert II's 1625 engraving; and a Duet copy after a lost original in which a boy playing a flute was added by the copyist. The study does NOT present a "Flute and Fife Players" as a Baburen picture. The Kassel Flute and Fife Players are ter Brugghen's.
- C425. The Borghese patronage, discredited. Wikipedia and older sources say Baburen painted a Capture of Christ for Cardinal Scipione Borghese. Franits (per the HNA review) "justifiably pans the long-discredited but still widely held notion that Van Baburen worked for the Borghese, pointing out that the Borghese Gallery's Capture of Christ did not enter the collection until 1787" (gift of the sculptor Bartolomeo Cavaceppi to Marcantonio Borghese in exchange for a pension). The study does NOT list Borghese as a patron; the Borghese Capture (W2 version B) is a later-collection picture, first attributed to Baburen by Roberto Longhi in 1926.
