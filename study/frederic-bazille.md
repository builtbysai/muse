# Frederic Bazille: Study Notes

**Site:** Frederic Bazille (1841-1870), French painter of the Gleyre circle, dead at 28 in the Franco-Prussian War -- born Monday 6 December 1841 in Montpellier, the eldest son of Gaston Bazille, a wealthy Protestant wine merchant and agriculturalist, and raised at the Domaine de Meric, the family estate at Castelnau-le-Lez outside the city; studied medicine at Montpellier from 1859, drawing at the Musee Fabre and copying old masters there, and learning from the Delacroix and Courbet pictures of the collector Alfred Bruyas; moved to Paris late in 1862 to continue medicine and entered the atelier of Charles Gleyre, where he met Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir and Alfred Sisley; left Gleyre with them in 1863 over the academic method and painted outdoors at Chailly and in the Forest of Fontainebleau with Monet; failed his medical exam in 1864 and painted full-time from then on, supported by his parents' money, which he also spent generously on his friends -- housing Monet and Renoir in his studios, sharing models and paying for them jointly, and agreeing in January 1867 to buy Monet's Women in the Garden for 2,500 francs; studios at 6 place Furstenberg (from January 1865), 20 rue Visconti (from 15 July 1866), 9 rue de la Condamine in the Batignolles (January 1868 to 15 May 1870) and rue des Beaux-Arts (spring 1870); Salon debut 1866 with Still Life with Fish accepted and Young Girl at the Piano rejected; Salon rejects in April 1867, answered by a letter to his mother proposing an independent exhibition of about a dozen young artists, inviting Courbet, Corot, Diaz and Daubigny as elders -- the plan the first Impressionist exhibition realised in 1874, four years after his death; Family Reunion and Potted Flowers accepted at the Salon of 1868; View of the Village accepted and Fisherman with a Net rejected in 1869; Summer Scene accepted and La Toilette rejected in 1870; volunteered on Tuesday 16 August 1870 for the 3rd Zouave Regiment, against the wishes of family and friends, served in Algeria and returned to France with his regiment, and was killed on Monday 28 November 1870 at the Battle of Beaune-la-Rolande, taking command after his officer was injured and leading a failed assault in which he was hit twice; his father travelled to the battlefield days later and brought the body back for burial in the Protestant cemetery at Montpellier. The oeuvre is small and the count is disputed: the Wikipedia list page counts 59 rows and calls itself incomplete against its own lead figure of "some 70 canvasses"; Michel Schulman's digital catalogue raisonne counts 85 painting records, of which two are flagged uncertain, leaving 83 uncontested. The major works are The Pink Dress (1864, reworked after February 1865), Family Reunion (1867-68), View of the Village (1868), Summer Scene (1869-70), La Toilette (1870), the two Young Woman with Peonies (1870), Bazille's Studio, Rue La Condamine (1870) and Ruth and Boaz (1870). He never exhibited with the Impressionists; he never married; he left letters, not a theory. The work stayed in the family for decades: his mother gave View of the Village and Still Life with Heron to the Musee Fabre in 1898, Family Reunion entered the national collection in 1905 with his brother Marc's help, and the Condamine studio picture and The Pink Dress came through Marc's 1924 bequest; the retrospective the sources record is the Salon d'Automne in 1910, and the full rediscovery came with the 2016-17 retrospective at the Musee Fabre (Saturday 25 June to Sunday 16 October 2016), the Musee d'Orsay (15 November 2016 to 5 March 2017) and the National Gallery of Art, Washington (Sunday 9 April to Sunday 9 July 2017), about 74 paintings and two sketchbooks. ## M1. The Figure Held Solid in Outdoor Light
Bazille's programme, stated in the 6 January 1865 letter to his father -- "I must paint a great many figures this winter" so that spring and summer outdoors can be used well -- is a body painted at full weight and full likeness inside a landscape painted en plein air, neither dissolved into light nor flattened into a studio cut-out. In The Pink Dress the striped dress keeps its pattern and mass against a blazing village; in Family Reunion faces stay distinct under the canopy while the countryside beyond burns bright; in View of the Village the shaded sitter's face holds against sunlit Castelnau. Berthe Morisot saw the problem exactly, writing to her sister Edma in 1869 (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Rewald 1974, p. 643): "He is trying to do what we have so often tried to bring off: to paint a figure in the open air [in the outdoor light]. This time I think he has succeed." Wikipedia's summary is the same claim in other words: "Many of Bazille's major works are examples of figure painting in which he placed the subject figure within a landscape painted en plein air." The watchable behaviour is the eye shuttling between near solid and far glare, and the figure never blinking out. **Takeaway:** the figure is the test of the light, not its victim; hold the body solid and let the landscape do the dissolving.

## M2. The Dark Frame That Opens the Distance
Dark vegetation at the edges framing a bright, small, sunlit settlement or stretch of water in the middle distance -- a Barbizon framing device Bazille uses twice as his signature composition, and a third time in another medium. In The Pink Dress dark trees frame Castelnau-le-Lez, the village shining between them while his cousin Therese des Hours sits with her back to us in the foreground; in View of the Village the same device returns with the sitter now facing out, the pine tree and the shaded ledge doing the framing; the three Aigues-Mortes views of 1867 use water and wall instead of trees -- the western ramparts stretching across blue-green water, the Queen's Gate brought close, the southern marsh view setting the architecture farther away. Google Arts and Culture's note on The Pink Dress names the effect: dark trees frame the middle ground to emphasise the contrast between far distance and foreground. The picture breathes in and out -- dark edge, bright core, dark edge -- and the distance is felt as a place you could walk to, not as a backdrop. **Takeaway:** frame the light with dark, and the distance opens by itself; the frame is the depth.

## M3. Two Climates in One Canvas (Shade vs Sun)
One picture containing two different lights -- cool, enclosed shade in the foreground and hot, open sunlight beyond -- with the boundary between them as the real subject. Family Reunion sets eleven relatives under a large chestnut tree against a sunlit terrace edge and countryside; View of the Village sets a shaded ledge against a shining village; Summer Scene sets poplar shade against bright ground beyond. Zacharie Astruc, reviewing Summer Scene at the Salon of 1870 (VERIFIED VERBATIM, L'Echo des Beaux Arts, 12 June 1870), saw the sun as the agent: "Bazille sets up his easel in direct sunlight to paint under the magical effects of daylight... Sun floods his canvases... the meadow is as though on fire." The light itself moves across the picture as dappled bands on the ground while the people stay still -- the irregular band of sunshine crossing the Family Reunion terrace at lower right is the clearest instance, a stripe of heat laid across a shaded gathering. **Takeaway:** do not paint one light; paint two lights and the edge between them, and the edge will do the drama.

## M4. The Group That Looks Out
A gathering posed in tableau stillness, most faces turned to the viewer, no interaction between sitters -- a wedding-photograph stillness that makes the viewer the person the picture is for. In Family Reunion eight of the eleven look at the observer directly or indirectly, with no interaction between them: parents seated left on a bench, uncle Eugene des Hours and his wife, cousin Pauline with her husband Emile Teulon, brother Marc with his wife Suzanne, cousin Camille back from a walk, and Bazille himself at far left behind his parents, added later. In View of the Village the sitter faces us, unlike The Pink Dress's turned back; in the Washington Young Woman with Peonies the Black woman faces the viewer directly, bundle of peonies held forward. The contrast is Monet's Women in the Garden, which Bazille bought: there the figures are absorbed in their own activity. Here the picture holds your gaze and will not release it; nothing happens, and that is the event. **Takeaway:** a group that looks out turns the viewer into the occasion; the stillness is the action.

## M5. The Studio as a Portrait of Artistic Life
The working room itself -- its pictures, stove, chairs, floorboards -- composed as carefully as a figure group, with the apparatus of painting as the subject. The Furstenberg studio of 1865-66 has no sitter at all: pictures crowding the upper walls, heater, chairs, small table below, an angled floor drawing the viewer in, furnishings establishing human use with no one there. The Visconti studio of 1867 adds the easel, the palette, the crowded wall, a long stretch of floorboards. The Condamine studio of 1870 is the statement: the painter at his easel beside Manet, Edmond Maitre at the piano, Renoir seated, the room first painted without occupants and the figures added later, and Manet painting Bazille's own figure himself -- "Manet painted me in", Bazille wrote to his father on 1 January 1870 (VERIFIED VERBATIM). The rejected pictures hang prominently on the left wall -- Fisherman with a Net, La Toilette, Renoir's rejected 1866 landscape -- so the room is also a manifesto: the Salon-refused work exhibited inside the painter's own picture of his life. The eye inventories the room like a guest; the paintings on the wall are a second exhibition inside the painting. **Takeaway:** paint the room as if it were a sitter; the tools and the pictures on the wall are the portrait.

## M6. The Studio Figure Transplanted Outdoors
Bodies drawn from the living model in the Paris studio in winter, then carried -- as drawings -- into a landscape painted on site, so a leisure scene is assembled from two different places and seasons. Summer Scene is the proof: conceived in April 1869 and intended as the crowning achievement of his plein-air figure exploration, its eight figures were first drawn in the Paris studio and then transported outdoors to the banks of the Lez near Meric. The only known full-composition drawing has seven figures, not eight; the wrestlers are locked more rigorously in the painting than in the drawing; the boy in the water floats with his head turned left, where in the drawing he swims naturally. The January 1865 letter states the programme outright -- winter figures first, summer outdoors second -- and the seams are the point: each body is individually posed and complete, yet they share one light. Look for the body that belongs to the studio and the ground that belongs to the river. **Takeaway:** a scene can be built from two seasons; the join is visible and it is the work.

## M7. Revision as a Visible Process (Scrape, Rework, Reuse)
A painting treated as a surface that can be scraped back, re-prepared and re-painted, or as a support to be reused whole -- so the finished picture is a stack of decisions, not a single sitting. The Pink Dress was substantially reworked after February 1865: scraping around the figure, the vegetation and the feet, possible solvent, a renewed white preparation over sky and ground, the dress, feet, torso, arms and neckline altered (Mottin, Techne 46, 2018, 14 paintings examined). Family Reunion was revised after the summer, its own figure added at the far left, the little dogs in the foreground replaced by a still life of a furled umbrella, a straw hat and a bunch of flowers. Ruth and Boaz was painted over the inverted Young Girl at the Piano, the rejected 1866 Salon picture, recovered by scientific imaging; the Young Male Nude Lying on the Grass has an earlier composition below it; the Condamine studio was first painted without occupants. Bazille said it himself in a letter to his mother, c. 18/25 August 1865 (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Tinterow/Loyrette 1994, p. 329), about the uncle portraits: "I have completely reworked them since my last letter; originally I had included a mass of details, which, from the distance they were intended to be viewed, had a very bad effect." The picture changes if you stare -- pentimenti, doubled edges, a figure that arrived late. **Takeaway:** rework is not failure, it is the method; leave the layers where they can be seen.

## M8. The Modern Male Nude
The academic nude brought into the present tense -- contemporary bathing dress, wrestling, swimming, a fisherman with a net -- so the body is a modern leisure body, not a mythological one. Fisherman with a Net (1868) shows a nude male body seen from behind in a wooded waterside setting; the Salon rejected it in 1869, and Bazille hung it prominently inside the Condamine studio painting, a refused picture given a place inside his own representation of artistic life. Summer Scene (1869-70) dresses its eight men in contemporary bathing dress -- striped trunks, red and blue trunks on the two wrestlers mid-distance, a swimmer at the lower edge, a man helped from the water at right -- clothed, not nude as first planned, which is the modernity: the body in the leisure of its own decade. The Young Male Nude Lying on the Grass (1870) is a single reclining body, the last of the line. The watchable behaviour is bodies in mid-action -- a grip, a stretch, a dive -- held still long enough to be looked at. **Takeaway:** take the nude out of mythology and into the river; the trunks and the wrestling are the subject, not the apology for it.

## M9. The Repeated Place (Meric / Castelnau Revisited)
One family property and one village returned to across six years, so the same terrace, the same view, the same sitters reappear in different arrangements and different light. The Pink Dress (1864) and Family Reunion (1867-68) and View of the Village (1868) are all the Meric terrace and the Castelnau view; the two Terrace at Meric pictures (1867) are the same subject twice in one year; Summer Scene (1869-70) is the Lez below the estate, and Landscape on the Banks of the Lez (1870) is the same river with no figures at all. Edmond Duranty caught the rhythm (SECONDHAND): "Every spring Monsieur Bazille returns from the South with summer paintings... full of greenery, sunshine and simple assurance." The recognition across pictures is the content -- the same hill, the same roof tiles, a dress seen again, a terrace that holds eleven people one year and one woman the next. **Takeaway:** return to one place until it becomes a language; the repetition is the portrait of the place.

## M10. Still Life as the Anchor Genre
The small, concentrated still life as the reliable ground of the practice -- the genre that got him into the Salon when the ambitious modern scenes were refused. Still Life with Fish was his Salon debut in 1866, accepted while Young Girl at the Piano was rejected; Still Life with Heron followed in 1867; Flowers and Potted Flowers in 1868, the latter shown at the Salon beside Family Reunion; the two Young Woman with Peonies of 1870 are portrait-like flower paintings, the model's attention on the task of arranging the flowers. The gleam against dark -- fish scales, heron feathers, peony petals -- is rendered with the same care as a face, and the small canvas holds the same light as the large one. When the big figure-in-landscape pictures were refused, the still life kept the practice visible and the hand in training. **Takeaway:** keep one small genre that always works; it is the anchor the ambitious pictures swing from.

## Evolution
Early, 1863-65: learning. Fontainebleau and Chailly landscapes with Monet, who already knew how to paint outdoors and helped him; figure studies in the studio; the first large figure-in-landscape attempts, Reclining Nude and The Pink Dress, begun in 1864 at 22-23. The programme is explicit in the January 1865 letter: winter figures first, summer outdoors second, and the winter of 1864-65 is spent on the living model at 6 place Furstenberg, Monet coming to wake him every morning. Middle, 1866-68: ambition. Still lifes as Salon entries; portraits of the circle -- Renoir twice in 1867, Verlaine, Maitre, Blau; the two big Meric set-pieces, Family Reunion and View of the Village; the three Aigues-Mortes landscapes of May 1867, planned from a letter at the Hotel Saint-Louis as walls reflected in water at sunset, needing at least eight fine days and getting four working outings; the independent-exhibition proposal of April 1867, never realised, the plan the 1874 exhibitions later carried out. Scale grows: Family Reunion is 152 by 230 cm, the largest canvas of his life. Late, 1869-70: several directions at once, and no last painting. The eight-figure Summer Scene; the paired Peonies, the same model twice with the variables changed -- facing out in Washington, arranging flowers in Montpellier -- the paired and tripled canvas, the mechanism folded here from the main leg's candidates, which also covers the three Aigues-Mortes views (panorama, close gate, distant marsh), the two Terrace at Meric pictures of 1867 and the two Renoir portraits: the same motif re-seen, and the difference between versions is the content. La Toilette as the indoor counterpart to Summer Scene; the Condamine studio as a statement about artistic life; the Lez landscape with no figures at all; Ruth and Boaz turning to night and the Bible, painted over the rejected Young Girl at the Piano; Melopee, never publicly exhibited before its 2026 sale. The open ending, the second mechanism folded here: the final year pursues incompatible aims simultaneously -- outdoor bathers, indoor toilette, flower portrait, studio group, figureless landscape, nocturnal Bible scene -- and death interrupts all of them, so there is no summation canvas. His last letter, to Edmond Maitre on 2 August 1870 (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Tinterow/Loyrette 1994, p. 338), says: "I have almost finished a large landscape [Landscape by the Lez River]... I am completely alone on the country; my cousins and my brother are at the resort. My father and mother are living in town; this solitude pleases me enormously; it makes me work a lot and read a lot." Two weeks later he left to join the 3rd Zouaves. Afterlife: the work stayed in the family and its circle for decades; when the first Impressionist exhibition opened in 1874 he had been dead more than three years, and his place in its prehistory rests on the work and associations of the 1860s, not on participation. His mother gave View of the Village and Still Life with Heron to the Musee Fabre in 1898; Family Reunion entered the French national collection in 1905 with his brother Marc's help; works including the Condamine studio picture and The Pink Dress followed through Marc's 1924 bequest. Historians and curators central to the recovery, per Hilaire: Roger Marx and Andre Joubin, alongside the family's gifts. There was no 1900 retrospective: the retrospective the sources record is the Salon d'Automne in 1910. The 2016 monograph's wording is that the paintings "were rediscovered after the turn of the century by art critics and curators" -- a gradual rediscovery, not a 1900 show. The full return came with the 2016-17 retrospective, about 74 paintings and two sketchbooks, shown beside predecessors (Rousseau, Courbet) and contemporaries (Manet, Monet). Monet's role in preserving the oeuvre is not established by any source read: the money and support flowed Bazille to Monet, not the reverse -- Bazille bought Monet's Women in the Garden for 2,500 francs and housed and supplied Monet, Renoir and Sisley. Do not assert a Monet preservation role. The largest single holder is the Musee Fabre, Montpellier, with 20 or more paintings and a whole room; the Musee d'Orsay holds The Pink Dress, Family Reunion, the Renoir portrait, the Improvised Field Hospital and the Condamine studio; the National Gallery of Art, Washington, holds the Blau and Maitre portraits, the Western Ramparts at Aigues-Mortes and the Washington Peonies.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is a refusal to choose between the studio and the field. Bazille's position, stated at 22-23 in a letter to his father in 1864 (VERIFIED VERBATIM, via Wikiquote citing Chong): "I do hope, that if I ever do anything, at least to have the merit of not copying anyone." The originality he wanted was not a style but a practice: figures learned in winter, landscapes painted in summer, the two joined in one canvas where the body holds solid and the light does the moving. He chose the modern era because it was the one he understood -- "The subject matter is unimportant, provided what I have done is interesting as a painting. I chose the modern era because it is the one I understand best; I find it more alive for people who are alive" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, via Wikiquote citing Chong) -- and the modern era for him was a family terrace, a riverbank of bathers, a studio full of friends, a girl under a tree with a village shining behind her. The big classical compositions were finished, in the remark Wikiquote carries without a letter citation: "The big classical compositions are finished; an ordinary view of daily life would be much more interesting." The Salon was administrative caprice -- "It is really too ridiculous for a reasonably intelligent person to expose himself to this kind of administrative caprice" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, c. 1869, Marandel/Daulte, pp. 179-180) -- and the answer was the group show he proposed in 1867, a dozen young men thinking the same way, inviting the elders they admired. The money is part of the logic, not outside it: the wealthy son who paid for the models, bought his friend's unsaleable canvas, and kept two hard-up painters in his rooms -- "Monet has popped up out of nowhere with a collection of magnificent canvases... With Renoir, that makes two hard-up painters I am putting up. It's quite an infirmary here" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, letter to his mother, February 1867, Schulman 1995, p. 354) -- and the same letter that complains about the cost also calls the arrangement an infirmary with affection. The short career is the wound in the logic and also its proof: in eight years of painting he showed great technical skill, originality and high promise, and the promise is visible precisely because it was never narrowed into a manner. Renoir remembered him after his death as "that pure-hearted gentle knight" (SECONDHAND, via Jean Renoir), and also, more bluntly, that he had not died "romantically, galloping over a Delacroix' battlefield... but stupidly, during the retreat, on a muddy road" (SECONDHAND, via Jean Renoir). The muddy road is the fact; the gentle knight is the judgement; the pictures are the evidence that both are true. He wrote to his friends, shortly before his death, "I have no intention of being killed, there's too much I still want to do with my life" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Puydebat 1993, p. 16). The work that survives is the work of a man who had too much still to do, and who did it in public, in daylight, with his friends looking out of the picture at us.
