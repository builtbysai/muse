# Bruno Corbucci: Study Notes

**Site:** Bruno Corbucci (b. Friday 23 October 1931, Rome, Italy; d. Saturday 7 September 1996, Rome, Italy, age 64), Italian screenwriter and film director, the younger brother of Sergio Corbucci -- the comic writer who became a director without changing genres, and then built the longest-running popular series character in Italian cinema, the foul-mouthed Roman policeman Nico Giraldi, eleven films in eight years, from a stolen purse. He wrote perhaps a hundred and fifty screenplays, directed fifty-five films, and is remembered for one thing his brother never had: a character audiences came back to, year after year, until the actor himself was tired of him. [C30145]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Enzo Barboni study: Barboni ends his study with the cinematographer who shot Sergio Corbucci's *Django* (1966) and then emptied the spaghetti western of its killing, and the hinge of that whole line is the man who wrote *Django* with his brother and then took Barboni's exact duo, Hill and Spencer, to Miami. Bruno Corbucci is the comedy brother behind the camera with Barboni's exact duo in *Miami Supercops* (1985), the screenwriter on the film Barboni shot, and the director of the series that did to the poliziottesco what Barboni did to the western: kept the furniture, took out the killing, and refilled the room with jokes. Where Barboni brings the pan of beans and the hammer punch, Corbucci brings the stolen purse, the Roman insult, and a cop who changes costume every year. The study starts where Barboni's leaves off -- with the *Django* credit, the Miami link, and the series that ran eleven films on one stolen purse. [C30146]

## Corrections to the brief

Four corrections to the #758 pick's wording, verified this run and carried into the study. (1) The *Django* flag is resolved, not withdrawn: the screenplay credit is real (see Doorway check D2), and the pick's observation that it is absent from Bruno's English-Wikipedia selected filmography is accurate -- the list is a selection, and Italian Wikipedia's full filmography does list it. (2) The De Angelis tie, "five of his films 1976-1979": the count holds, the span does not. The brothers scored the first three Giraldi films (1976-1977) and two non-Giraldi comedies, *Messalina, Messalina!* (1977) and *Il figlio dello sceicco* (1978, single-sourced via Blu-ray.com's De Angelis filmography); *Squadra antimafia* (1978) and *Squadra antigangsters* (1979) are Goblin's. Correct form: "the De Angelis brothers scored five Corbucci films 1976-1978, three of them Giraldi films." (3) The Micalizzi tie, "four Nico Giraldi films 1980-1988": wrong as stated. Micalizzi scored four Corbucci films 1980-1988, but only two are Giraldi films (*Delitto a Porta Romana*, 1980; *Delitto sull'autostrada*, 1982); the other two are the non-Giraldi *Il ficcanaso* (1981) and *Rimini Rimini - Un anno dopo* (1988). The full Giraldi music relay is De Angelis x3, Goblin x2, Carlo Rustichelli x1 (single-sourced), Micalizzi x2, Detto Mariano x1, Fabio Frizzi x2. Soundohm's label copy claiming Micalizzi was "the series' musical voice from the start" contradicts the film pages and is label copy, not a credit; do not repeat it. This also corrects two earlier studies in the chain: the #754 Micalizzi study's claim that Micalizzi was "the series' musical voice from the start", and the #755 De Angelis study's "the Giraldi series passes from the brothers (1976-77) to Micalizzi (1980s)" -- the handoff passes through Goblin, Rustichelli and Mariano first. (4) Two attributions in the #758 brief's orbit are wrong and are corrected here: *Cane e gatto* (1983) was directed by Bruno Corbucci, not Enzo Barboni (the #758 brief misattributed it to Barboni); and *Rimini Rimini* (1987) was directed by Sergio Corbucci, not Bruno -- Bruno co-wrote it and directed the sequel, *Rimini Rimini - Un anno dopo* (1988). Year conflicts resolved: *James Tont operazione D.U.E.* is 1966 (film page March 1966, Italian Wikipedia) against 1965 (English Wikipedia's Bruno filmography); *Il figlio dello sceicco* is 1978 (Titanus, MUBI, IMDb release 25 February 1978, Blu-ray.com) against 1977 (Italian Wikipedia); *Delitto in Formula Uno* is 1984 (film page, IMDb, Letterboxd, release 17 February 1984) against 1983 (the Tomas Milian filmography table); *Zum zum zum* is 1969 (film page) against 1968 (Italian Wikipedia), unresolved 1-1, use 1969 with the flag. *Ms. Stiletto* and *Isabella duchessa dei diavoli* are one film under two titles -- do not double-count. [C30149]

## Biography

Bruno Corbucci was born in Rome on 23 October 1931, five years after his brother Sergio (b. 6 December 1926, Rome; d. 1 December 1990). [C30150] Italian Wikipedia's biography: he began as a screenwriter of films, "soprattutto comici, con Erminio Macario e poi con Toto" -- mostly comedies, with Erminio Macario and then with Toto -- and that screenwriting vocation followed him through his whole career. The Macario part is a bio claim only: no Macario film appears in either Wikipedia's screenwriter filmography for him, so the study does not assert a Macario writing credit. [C30151] His directorial debut came in the mid-1960s with the James Tont diptych starring Lando Buzzanca; fame arrived with the Nico Giraldi series, begun 1976 with *Squadra antiscippo* and ended 1984 with *Delitto al Blue Gay* (Italian Wikipedia). [C30152] He made cameo appearances in his own films -- as the honourable Licanzi in *Il trafficone* (1974), a killer in the cinema-within-the-film in *Delitto al ristorante cinese* (1981), the vet in *La casa stregata* (1982), a distracted car owner in *Delitto sull'autostrada* (1982), the station master in *Cane e gatto* (1983) -- and as General Anselmi in the TV series *Classe di ferro* (1989-1991), which won the Telegatto for best telefilm in 1990 (Italian Wikipedia). [C30153] His last work was the seven-episode Raiuno variety show *Gran Casino* with Lino Banfi, broadcast a month after his death (Italian Wikipedia, citing *Il Manifesto*, 26 September 1996). [C30154] He had chronic renal failure and was already on dialysis during the shoot of *Superfantagenio* (1986); he died of cardiac arrest in a Rome clinic on 7 September 1996, the funeral three days later at the basilica of Santa Maria del Popolo, Piazza del Popolo, and he is buried in the family tomb at the Verano cemetery in Rome (Italian Wikipedia only, single source, carried not multiply verified). [C30155] IMDb counts 150 writing credits and 55 directing credits; WesternsAllItaliana says "over 130 writing credits" and lists pseudonyms Frank B. Corlish, Gordon Wilson Jr., Billy Michaels and Dean Whitcomb, used "to navigate international markets". [C30156]

## Works

**W01 -- *James Tont operazione U.N.O.*, 1965.** Eurospy spoof of *Goldfinger*, co-written and co-directed by Bruno Corbucci and Giovanni Grimaldi; stars Lando Buzzanca as James Tont, Agent 007 1/2 (from Italian *tonto*, "dumb"), with Loris Gizzi as Erik Goldsinger, a parody of Auric Goldfinger. Grade A. The inept Tont recovers microfilm in Trinidad, is painted gold by a femme fatale (surviving because she fails to cover him completely), and must stop Goldsinger -- employed by Red China -- from destroying the United Nations headquarters in New York. Score by Marcello Giombini; a talking white-mouse CIA agent (SOS 117, a ventriloquist) rescues him; his car turns into a submarine years before *The Spy Who Loved Me*. United Artists' lawyers objected to the 007 1/2 billing. Evidence: film page; Eurospy page; Screen Action Jazz; Fantastic Movie Musings. [C30157]

**W02 -- *James Tont operazione D.U.E.*, 1966.** Sequel, directed by Bruno alone; Buzzanca returns as Tont, Gizzi as Mr. Spring (the Emilio Largo spoof). Grade A. After foiling a Soviet break-in at the British Embassy in Moscow, Tont addresses a secret-agents' convention in Geneva while dodging assassination attempts, then must stop a plan to loot and destroy St. Peter's Basilica. Based on *Thunderball* with elements anticipating *You Only Live Twice*. Year wobble: English Wikipedia's Bruno filmography lists it as 1965, the film's own page says March 1966, Italian Wikipedia says 1966 -- use 1966. Evidence: film page. [C30158]

**W03 -- *Ringo e Gringo contro tutti*, 1966.** Western comedy: Raimondo Vianello is Sgt. Gringo and Lando Buzzanca is Pvt. Ringo, two Confederates who have sat in Fort Jackson for seven years unaware the Civil War ended; two Southern-belle sisters enlist them to fund a Confederate rebirth; they rob, bounty-hunt, impersonate settlers, Indians and famous gunmen (a Col. Mortimer / Stranger double-act), and end up meeting President Ulysses S. Grant. Grade A. Evidence: review; IMDb plot; cast. [C30159]

**W04 -- *Spia spione*, 1966.** Eurospy comedy. Grade B. No plot or cast detail located this run. Year wobble: English Wikipedia lists 1966, Italian Wikipedia lists 1967, a Sporcle Eurospy cast list says 1967 -- use 1966 with the flag. [C30160]

**W05 -- *Marinai in coperta*, 1967.** Grade B. No plot or cast detail located this run beyond title and year. [C30161]

**W06 -- *Peggio per me... meglio per te*, 1967.** Musicarello named after Little Tony's hit song "Peggio per me"; directed by Bruno, written by Dino Verde and Bruno, produced by Edmondo Amati, music by Willy Brezza; stars Little Tony as Tony Romanelli with Katia Christine, Gianni Agus, Aldo Puglisi. Grade A. IMDb's Bruno bio lists it as 1968 ("known for ... Peggio per me... meglio per te (1968)") against Wikipedia's 1967 -- use 1967. Evidence: film page. [C30162]

**W07 -- *Spara, Gringo, spara / The Longest Hunt*, 1968.** His first (and, per its Wikipedia page, except for the parody *Three Musketeers of the West*, his only) straight spaghetti western. Grade A. Written by Bruno and Mario Amendola; stars Brian Kelly as American adventurer Stark, Keenan Wynn as Major Charlie Doneghan, Erika Blanc, Folco Lulli as Don Hernando Gutierrez, Fabrizio Moroni as Fidel. Stark, sentenced to hang in a Mexican village, is saved by a rich rancher on condition he bring back the rancher's son Fidel from Doneghan's outlaw gang -- only to learn the rancher wants Fidel dead to whitewash the shame of an illegitimate son. Title song "Rainbow... vorrei... vorrei" performed by Little Tony; music by Vasili Kojucharov. Note: the film page's "only western" claim ignores the western-comedy *Ringo e Gringo* (1966) -- read it as "only straight western". Evidence: film page. [C30163]

**W08 -- *Ridera (Cuore matto)*, 1967.** Musicarello built on Little Tony's "Cuore matto" (Sanremo 1967, nine weeks at No. 1 in Italy, over a million copies, gold disc May 1967). Grade B. Do not confuse with Mario Amendola's *Cuore matto... matto da legare* (1967), a different film from the same song, which Bruno co-wrote but did not direct. No plot detail for Bruno's *Ridera* located this run. [C30164]

**W09 -- *Zum zum zum - La canzone che mi passa per la testa*, 1969.** Musicarello: Little Tony as Tony Bertozzini, Isabella Savona, Orietta Berti as Sister Teresa, Peppino De Filippo as Peppino Bertozzini. Grade A. The sons of a music-loving insurance broker take extreme measures to find happiness for themselves and others, with the help of the power of music. A contemporary review called it "an unpretentious work, whose thin plot is a mere pretext for the songs performed by the protagonists". Sequel *Zum zum zum n. 2* followed in 1969. Year wobble: Italian Wikipedia lists the first film as 1968, the film's own English page says 1969 -- use 1969 with the flag. Evidence: film page. [C30165]

**W10 -- *Django*, 1966 (screenwriter).** Directed by Sergio Corbucci with cinematography by Enzo Barboni (#758), starring Franco Nero, released April 1966. Grade A. Bruno's credit is story and screenplay with his brother (see Doorway check D2): Italian prints credit the Corbucci brothers with "story, screenplay & dialogue", and Bruno made "further screenplay contributions and revisions ... throughout production". Django trudges through mud in the rain dragging a coffin, rescues the prostitute Maria from Major Jackson's men, and the coffin is revealed to hold a rapid-fire Gatling gun. The film belongs to Sergio's "mud and blood" run, and its violence -- whipping, an ear sliced off, mass shootouts in the mud, Django's hands crushed with a rifle butt -- got it banned in England and several other countries on release. Evidence: "Django (1966 film)" read end to end (lines 0-204 of 710); IMDb; Plex; WesternsAllItaliana. [C30166]

**W11 -- *Il trafficone*, 1974.** *Commedia sexy all'italiana*, written by Bruno and Mario Amendola, starring Carlo Giuffre as Neapolitan chancer Vincenzo LoRusso, with Lino Banfi as Luigi Scardocchia; Bruno cameos as the honourable Licanzi. Grade A. LoRusso, selling a fake suede jacket at a Rome intersection, meets Laura, is caught in bed with her by her husband -- who pays him for it, having been cured of psychological impotence -- and, briefed on Masters and Johnson, opens a sexology centre to solve couples' problems and earn money. Released 28 November 1974. This is the hinge film: producer Galliano Juso recalled that while shooting it, his purse was stolen by thieves on Kawasaki motorcycles -- the incident that led him and Bruno to develop the anti-snatch-squad film *Squadra antiscippo* (1976), i.e. the birth of Nico Giraldi. Evidence: film page. [C30167]

**W12 -- *Squadra antiscippo / The Cop in Blue Jeans*, 1976.** First Nico Giraldi film. Grade A. Director Bruno Corbucci; screenplay and story Mario Amendola and Bruno Corbucci; music Guido and Maurizio De Angelis; produced by Galliano Juso (Cinemaster); cinematography Nino Celeste; edited by Daniele Alabiso; released 11 March 1976, Italy; 95 minutes. Undercover cop Giraldi is assigned to the anti-mugging squad and hunts the Rome thief Achille Bertinari, "Er Baronetto" (Guido Mannari); when Bertinari robs the American crime boss Richard Russo, alias Shelley (Jack Palance), Giraldi must resort to ruses rather than force to bring Russo down. The film grossed 2,013,807,160 lire, and historian Roberto Curti states it was among the most profitable domestic productions of the year. Giraldi is a foul-mouthed Roman policeman, first presented as a marshal (maresciallo) of the anti-mugging squad and nicknamed "Il Pirata" (The Pirate); later entries variously call him inspector and commissioner, and his rank drifts from film to film. Outburn's retrospective calls it the most strictly "traditional" entry of the series in Eurocrime terms -- still gritty, with bike stunts and a fast pace -- and notes its in-jokes to American crime cinema: Giraldi keeps a pet mouse named "Serpico" and a bird named "Inspector Callahan", with Pacino posters in his apartment. Evidence: "The Cop in Blue Jeans" (397 lines, read end to end); Outburn; Curti via Wikipedia. [C30169]

**W13 -- *Squadra antifurto / Hit Squad*, 1976.** Second Giraldi film. Grade A. Director Bruno Corbucci; screenplay and story Amendola and Corbucci; music Guido and Maurizio De Angelis; produced by Galliano Juso (Cinemaster), distributed by Titanus; released 29 October 1976; 103 minutes; grossed 1,825,316,810 lire. Marshal Giraldi of the anti-theft team deals with apartment and car thieves -- Blinds, a small-time thief prone to confessing; Zagaja, a stuttering thief; and the Sicilian Trapani brothers. Robbing the villa of a former CIA agent, the thieves steal documents exposing a US bribery system, "Deal Zebra Point"; Blinds is murdered after demanding money from the US emissary Ralf Douglas (Robert Webber), and Giraldi follows the trail to New York, where he arrests Douglas. Bombolo appears as Er Trippa (not yet Venticello); Lilli Carati, Giuseppe Pambieri and Giuliana Calandra co-star. Evidence: "Hit Squad (film)" (408 lines, read end to end). [C30170]

**W14 -- *Squadra antitruffa / Swindle*, 1977.** Third Giraldi film. Grade A. Director Bruno Corbucci; written by Amendola and Corbucci; music Guido and Maurizio De Angelis; edited by Daniele Alabiso; 96 minutes; 1977. Giraldi teams with English detective Robert Clayton (David Hemmings) to hunt a gang behind an enormous fraud against Lloyd's of London; the culprits eliminate witnesses, but the pair climbs to the head of the gang and jails him. Bombolo debuts as Venticello (Franco Bertarelli, aka Venticello); Massimo Vanni debuts as Brigadiere Gargiulo; Leo Gullotta plays Tarcisio "Er Fibbia" Pollaroli. European Trash Cinema notes Hemmings gives Giraldi a Sherlock Holmes-type hat as a present, and praises the De Angelis score. Release date in conflict: IMDb gives 12 November 1977, lamovie.app 18 November 1977. Evidence: "Swindle (1977 film)" (82 lines, read end to end); European Trash Cinema. [C30171]

**W15 -- *Squadra antimafia / Little Italy*, 1978.** Fourth Giraldi film. Grade B. Director Bruno Corbucci; written by Amendola and Corbucci; music Goblin; produced by Galliano Juso; cinematography Marcello Masciocchi; edited by Daniele Alabiso; 91 minutes; 1978. The two available plot summaries differ in detail: IMDb has undercover cop Giraldi travelling to New York and Las Vegas to find a crooked cop who gave his squadron a bad name; TV Passport has Marshal Giraldi infiltrating an underworld group to investigate the death of an informant and friend, the investigation taking him from Italy to Las Vegas. Cast: Enzo Cannavale as Salvatore Esposito, Eli Wallach as Don Girolamo Giarra, Bombolo as Venticello, Margherita Fumero as Maria Sole Giarra, Massimo Vanni as Gargiulo. The Goblin score is apparently lost -- Soundohm, reissuing the soundtrack performed by Girodivite, calls it a void in the Goblin discography that the label had tried for years to fill. Release date in conflict: IMDb 12 October 1978 vs TMDB 10 October 1978. Evidence: "Little Italy (1978 film)" (316 lines, read end to end); Soundohm. [C30172]

**W16 -- *Squadra antigangsters / The Gang That Sold America*, 1979.** Fifth Giraldi film. Grade A. Director Bruno Corbucci; screenplay and story Amendola and Corbucci; music Goblin; produced by Galliano Juso (Cinemaster), distributed by Titanus; released 1 March 1979; 90 minutes; grossed 569 million lire -- roughly a quarter of the first film's take three years later. Giraldi, now an agent of Interpol, goes to New York to help his friend Salvatore Esposito (Enzo Cannavale), an aspiring restaurateur in Little Italy squeezed by usurers; the boss's daughter Maria Sole (Margherita Fumero) pursues him. Asha Puthli plays Fiona Strike and sings two songs on the Goblin soundtrack. European Trash Cinema calls this the "bachelor party film" of the series: Amendola and Corbucci had already decided Nico would marry in the next film, so here they let him have fun first. Flag: plot summaries name the don Don Gerolamo, but the cast list gives Leo Gavero as Don Vito and Andrea Aureli as Don Mimi -- Don Gerolamo is Eli Wallach's character in *Little Italy*, and the summaries likely conflate the two films' dons; do not resolve. Evidence: "The Gang That Sold America" (368 lines, read end to end); European Trash Cinema. [C30173]

**W17 -- *Assassinio sul Tevere / Assassination on the Tiber*, 1979.** Sixth Giraldi film. Grade B. Director Bruno Corbucci; music Carlo Rustichelli (single-sourced -- his Wikipedia filmography lists the film; no second composer source was found); released 12 October 1979 (IMDb); 96 minutes. A black-out occurs during a meeting of a gang of criminals; when the light returns one of them is found stabbed in the back, and all the clues point to a penniless man who had argued with the victim shortly before. European Trash Cinema adds that the victim is a member of a navigation company's board of directors, that the widow is played by Marina Lante Della Rovere, and calls the film "easily the best Giraldi film so far". Cast: Marina Ripa di Meana, Roberta Manfredi, Alberto Farnese. No English Wikipedia page exists for this film; the plot rests on IMDb/MUBI plus the ETC account. Chapter order: Wikipedia's "Squadra antiscippo series" navbox lists *Assassinio sul Tevere* before *The Gang That Sold America*, but the film pages state the opposite order -- *The Gang That Sold America* is the fifth chapter (released 1 March 1979) and *Assassinio sul Tevere* follows it (12 October 1979), making it the sixth. The navbox order is not chronological and must not be used for the sequence. Evidence: IMDb; MUBI; European Trash Cinema. [C30174]

## Evolution: early vs late

**1956-1965 -- The comic writer.** Bruno enters cinema on a different path from Sergio: his main vocation is comic writing. He becomes one of the most assiduous screenwriters of Toto's mature phase, and his gift, per the Italian Toto site, is building farcical situations, parodies, misunderstandings and dialogue fitted to popular-cinema performers. He works continuously beside Sergio but "imposes himself as an independent author" at the same time. The through-line is Mario Amendola: Bruno co-writes dozens of Amendola films from *Toto di notte n. 1* (1962) onward, Amendola directing and Bruno co-writing, and the two later co-write Bruno's own directed films as well. Titanus's page for *Il figlio dello sceicco* calls them Bruno's "habitual partners in crime": "Bruno Corbucci behind the camera and Mario Amendola behind the typewriter." [C30187]

**1965-1975 -- The director who did not change genres.** He was still writing Toto and musicarelli for others in 1964-65 while co-directing his first film in 1965, and his own first directed films recycle exactly the genres he had been scripting -- Eurospy parody (James Tont), musicarello (Little Tony), western comedy (Ringo e Gringo), and one straight western (*Spara, Gringo, spara*, 1968). He did not change genres to direct; he took over the camera on the genres he already wrote. He kept writing for others after he started directing -- for Ruggero Deodato, Antonio Margheriti, Michele Lupo, Lucio Fulci (with Pupi Avati), Sergio Martino, Carlo Vanzina, Steno, and Sergio late (*Pari e dispari* 1978, *Mi faccio la barca* 1980, *Rimini Rimini* 1987, *Roba da ricchi* 1987). [C30188]

**1976-1984 -- The series machine.** A star (Milian) plus a writer-partner (Amendola) plus a producer (Juso) plus a rotating composer, repeating a formula with small variations and growing more farcical each time. *The Cop in Blue Jeans* was a major commercial success; the sequels "grew more comedic with each film" (Wikipedia, citing Curti); the box office slid from 2.01 billion lire (film 1) to 1.83 billion (film 2) to 569 million (film 5). The genre's violence drains out and the gag moves in. [C30189]

**1985-1996 -- The transplant.** The star system breaks. Milian is done with Giraldi (and visibly tired of it by 1984); Hill and Spencer are ageing out of the duo (their last non-Western together is Bruno's own *Miami Supercops*); the Italian theatrical market for lowbrow comedy is shrinking. Bruno's response is NOT reinvention but transplantation: the same ingredients (ensemble farce, Miami, the police squad, the anthology of episodes) moved to television -- *Classe di ferro* is Giraldi's squad without Giraldi, a barracks ensemble instead of a star cop; *Quelli della speciale* returns to the police-squad title. The anthology instinct of the early Toto episodic comedies returns in the Rimini films. What changed: medium (cinema to TV), scale (star vehicles to ensembles of unknowns), geography (Rome to Miami, then back to the Italian barracks). What stayed: the farce engine, the episodic structure, the Amendola partnership, the police/military milieu, the Roman dialect comedy. [C30190]

**Sergio's shadow.** Bruno never escaped it and never really tried to -- the shadow was also the doorway. He wrote many of Sergio's films (*Django* story and screenplay, 1966; *The Great Silence*, 1968; *I due marescialli*, 1961; *Gli onorevoli*, 1963), and Sergio directed Bruno's early scripts; the brothers' careers are braided, not parallel. But the record shows Bruno built an independent identity anyway: Sergio made 60+ films as director and is remembered as "the other Sergio" next to Leone, a western master with a cult afterlife; Bruno is remembered for a single thing Sergio never had -- a long-running popular series character, Nico Giraldi, 11 films over 8 years, and for the Hill/Spencer Miami comedies. The standard reference line -- "the vast majority of his directorial efforts are lowbrow comedies" -- is both the judgement and the defence: he was a popular-cinema professional, prolific from the late 1950s to the 1980s, not an auteur in Sergio's mould. He was defined by the shadow in prestige terms and free of it in commercial terms: his biggest success (Giraldi) is entirely his own. (The "shadow" analysis is a synthesis of the evidenced facts, not a cited critical position.) [C30191]

## M1 -- The parody transplant: keep the furniture, empty the violence

Bruno's first move as a director is the same move Barboni makes five years later with *Trinity*: take a genre at full strength, keep every piece of its furniture, and refill the space with jokes. *James Tont operazione U.N.O.* (1965) keeps the Bond film whole -- the gold-painted girl, the megalomaniac, the secret base, the gadget car -- and changes only the agent: Buzzanca's Tont is Agent 007 1/2, dumb by name (*tonto*), inept by design, rescued by a ventriloquist's white mouse. *Ringo e Gringo* (1966) keeps the western whole -- the fort, the Confederates, the bounty hunters, the famous gunmen -- and the joke is that the two soldiers never learned the war ended. *Spara, Gringo, spara* (1968) is the control experiment: the one straight western, proving he could play the genre straight and chose not to. The mechanism is subtraction by casting: change the hero's competence, keep everything else, and the genre tells on itself.

**Takeaway:** a genre is parodied by its hero, not its props -- keep the gold paint, the fort and the gadget car, and replace the competent man with a fool; the furniture does the rest. [C30192]

## M2 -- The song-first film

The musicarello runs on a mechanism Bruno uses four times in three years: the hit single precedes and names the film, and the plot is a pretext for the songs. "Cuore matto" is Sanremo 1967, nine weeks at No. 1 in Italy, over a million copies, a gold disc in May 1967 -- and then it is a film, *Ridera (Cuore matto)*, and also Amendola's *Cuore matto... matto da legare*, a different film from the same song, which Bruno co-wrote but did not direct. *Peggio per me... meglio per te* (1967) is named after Little Tony's hit song "Peggio per me". *Zum zum zum* (1969) is a song title first and a film second; a contemporary review called it "an unpretentious work, whose thin plot is a mere pretext for the songs performed by the protagonists". Little Tony is the centre of the run -- star of *Peggio per me*, *Ridera*, *Zum zum zum* and its sequel, title-song performer on *Spara, Gringo, spara* -- and the mechanism is the singer, not the story: the audience arrives knowing the song, and the film's job is to stage it three or four times with a plot in between.

**Takeaway:** when the song is the product, the film is the sleeve -- name the picture after the single, cast the singer, and let the plot be the excuse for the next number. [C30193]

## M3 -- The title machine: Squadra, then Delitto

The Giraldi series is named by a machine, and the machine changes once, exactly halfway. The first five films are all "Squadra anti-X" -- antiscippo (anti-mugging), antifurto (anti-theft), antitruffa (anti-fraud), antimafia, antigangsters -- each title naming the squad and therefore the milieu: the squad IS the premise, and the audience knows the crime before the first scene. From the sixth film the titles switch to "Delitto/Assassinio + a place": the Tiber, Porta Romana, a Chinese restaurant, the highway, Formula One, the Blue Gay. The place is the premise. The machine is what lets a series run eleven films on one character without ever explaining him again: the title tells the audience which world Giraldi is entering this year, and the film's job is to drop him into it. The one break proves the rule: film 8's title promises a place (the Chinese restaurant) and the film delivers a second character as well (the Mongolian cook), because the machine had run out of places and needed a new trick.

**Takeaway:** a series title is a promise of a world, not a plot -- name the squad, then name the place, and the audience will follow the same man into either. [C30194]

## M4 -- The undercover-disguise engine

Every Giraldi entry drops its hero into a new world in a new skin, and the disguise is the film: the plot is built to justify the costume, the accent and the location. Anti-mugging plainclothes cop, anti-theft marshal, fraud-squad partner to an English detective, mafia infiltrator, Interpol agent in New York, trucker among TIR hijackers, and, at the extreme, a second character altogether -- the Mongolian cook Ciu Ci Ciao in film 8, played by Milian opposite himself -- and finally an infiltrator of Rome's gay cabaret scene in film 11. The engine runs on the same logic as the title machine: a new world each year, a new skin to enter it, and the jokes come from the friction between the Roman cop and the world he has put on. The disguise is never a gag on its own; it is the premise that makes the gags possible, because every scene is Giraldi performing a role he is bad at in a place he does not belong.

**Takeaway:** give the hero a new costume every year and the plot writes itself -- the disguise is the premise, and the jokes are the friction between the man and the skin he is wearing. [C30195]

## M5 -- Romanesco and the insult as dialogue engine

The character's voice is the mechanism, and the mechanism is split across three men. Milian wrote his own lines in Roman slang -- his inventive use of romanesco made him a cult performer in Italy -- and his authorship was performative, not scripted: he holds no writing credit on any of the eleven Giraldi films; every screenplay credit reads Mario Amendola and Bruno Corbucci. Although his voice was dubbed most of the time by Ferruccio Amendola, Milian performed his lines in Italian and wrote his own lines in Roman slang. And Ferruccio Amendola was the nephew of Mario Amendola, the series' co-writer -- the words Milian invented in Romanesco were delivered to Italian audiences in the voice of the writer's nephew. The foul mouth is not decoration: the wisecrack in the heaviest situation is the scene's engine. Corbucci's own verdict on what Milian did to a cinema: "At the cinemas as soon as Tomas Milian appeared on the screen, when he made a wisecrack and in the heaviest situations, then it was a pandemonium, it was like being at the stadium." The gag is the event, and the audience's noise is the proof.

**Takeaway:** the voice is a three-man machine -- the actor invents the insult, the writer's nephew delivers it, the audience roars -- and the wisecrack in the worst moment is the whole scene. [C30196]

## M6 -- The repertory troupe and the family arc

The series is a repertory troupe, and the troupe is what lets the formula repeat without repeating itself. Bombolo as Venticello, the flatulent petty thief turned informant and friend (films 3-11, except that in film 8 he is called Bombolo); Massimo Vanni as Brigadiere Gargiulo; Olimpia Di Nardo as Angela, Giraldi's wife from the middle of the series on; Enzo Cannavale as Salvatore Esposito; editor Daniele Alabiso, credited on at least nine of the eleven films; and Amendola and Corbucci writing every one. The early films import a Hollywood name as heavy or partner -- Jack Palance as the crime boss in film 1, Robert Webber as the US emissary in film 2, David Hemmings as the English detective in film 3, Eli Wallach as Don Girolamo in film 4 -- a casting mechanism that fades as the series turns inward to its own troupe after 1979. And the series quietly domesticates its hero: the first five films show a bachelor cop (European Trash Cinema calls film 5 the "bachelor party film", because Amendola and Corbucci had decided Nico would marry in the next one); by film 7 Angela is in the cast as his wife; in film 8 she gives birth to a son, with Venticello as godfather; in film 9 the son, Rocky Giraldi, is a child character; in film 10 Giraldi's fear for his wife and son is the emotional stake. The troupe ages with the audience, and the family is the one arc the formula allows.

**Takeaway:** a series survives on its troupe, not its star -- keep the same faces around the hero, let them marry him off and give him a son, and the audience stays for the family, not the case. [C30197]

## M7 -- The music relay: six composers, no house sound

No single musical voice defines the Giraldi series; the relay itself is the signature. Six composers across eleven films, changing with the era: the De Angelis brothers' prog-funk with Roman folk roots (films 1-3), Goblin's prog-rock with disco songs (films 4-5), Rustichelli's veteran orchestral hand (film 6, single-sourced), Micalizzi's funky poliziottesco themes (films 7 and 9), Detto Mariano (film 8), and Fabio Frizzi's bouncy late themes (films 10-11). The relay is the correction this study carries into the chain: the #754 Micalizzi study and the #755 De Angelis study both present the handoff as brothers-to-Micalizzi, and the film pages say otherwise -- the handoff passes through Goblin, Rustichelli and Mariano first. The mechanism matters because it is the opposite of Barboni's: Barboni's Trinity has one whistled theme that is the contract with the audience, while Corbucci's Giraldi changes its sound every two films, and the audience accepts the change because the character's voice -- Milian's Romanesco, delivered by Amendola -- never changes. The music is the era; the voice is the series.

**Takeaway:** a series does not need one sound -- change the composer with the decade, keep the hero's voice, and the relay itself becomes the signature. [C30198]

## M8 -- The Miami transplant and the television turn

When the star system breaks, Corbucci does not reinvent himself; he transplants the machine. *Cane e gatto* (1983) takes the Spencer vehicle to Miami and pairs Spencer with Milian instead of Hill; *Miami Supercops* (1985) puts Hill and Spencer back together in Miami for their final non-Western film, closing the Miami cycle Barboni opened with *Crime Busters* (1977); *Aladdin* (1986) keeps Spencer in Miami as a genie, and Variety records the first clear commercial stumble. Then the transplant goes further: the same ingredients move to television, and the star is removed entirely. *Classe di ferro* (1989-1991) is Giraldi's squad without Giraldi -- a barracks ensemble of unknowns doing their military service, 24 episodes, with Bruno directing all of them and cameoing as General Anselmi. *Quelli della speciale* (1993) returns to the police-squad title. The mechanism is the same one that built the series: the squad is the premise, and the squad does not need its star. The tragedy of the transplant is that it worked as a machine and failed as a draw: the ensemble has no Milian, no stadium roar, and the theatrical career ends in 1988 with the Rimini sequel.

**Takeaway:** when the star leaves, keep the squad -- the premise outlives the hero, but the roar does not follow it. [C30199]

## Aesthetic logic

Corbucci's aesthetic is the professional's aesthetic: the joke is the product, the star is the delivery system, and the series is the factory. He spent a decade learning popular cinema from inside the typewriter -- Toto's farce, the musicarello's song, the Eurospy's parody, his brother's western -- and then he built a machine that could run for eight years on one incident (a stolen purse) and one voice (a Cuban actor's invented Roman slang, delivered by the writer's nephew). The logic runs: the title names the world, the disguise enters it, the insult runs it, the troupe holds it, and the composer changes with the decade. Everything in the films is built to be returned to rather than followed: the plot is a clothesline, the gags are the clothes, and the audience already knows the clothes by the second film -- which is why the box office could fall by three quarters and the series still ran to eleven. That is also why it stopped dead the moment the actor was tired of it: the formula was never Corbucci's, it was Milian's voice in Corbucci's machine, and when the voice left, the machine had nothing to say. The memorial fact is the aesthetic fact: the most durable popular character of the Italian seventies was built by a screenwriter who never stopped writing for others, directed by a man who took over the camera on the genres he already wrote, and voiced by a third man who was not the man on screen.
