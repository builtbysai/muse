## The life: pick a rule, paint against it until it breaks

Albert Oehlen was born September 17, 1954, in Krefeld, West Germany. The date is agreed across Wikipedia, artnet, the Guggenheim Bilbao, and Christie's. His father was also an artist, and his younger brother Markus Oehlen is a fellow artist; the two have shown together repeatedly, including a joint show at the Stadtisches Museum Abteiberg, Monchengladbach, in 2000. He lives with his wife, Esther Freund, and their three children in a village near Buhler, Switzerland, and divides his working life between Buhler and Segovia, Spain. (C24898, leg 1 C24802; leg 3 C24864; synthesis C24898)

In 1977 he moved to Berlin, where he worked as a waiter and decorator with his friend, the artist Werner Buttner. The early Berlin years were deliberately unruly: he and Buttner were officially charged for painting a mural on a local bookshop, and together they formed the tongue-in-cheek "League for the Prevention of Contradictory Behaviour". He first met Martin Kippenberger in the late 1970s, but the close friendship that drove much of both men's best work dates to the 1980s. Both were represented by Max Hetzler in the gallery's formative years, in the riotous group Hetzler affectionately called the "Hetzler boys". Oehlen was a member of the Lord Jim Lodge, along with Kippenberger among others. (C24899, leg 1 C24803, C24808; leg 3 C24870, C24872; synthesis C24899)

He studied at the Hochschule fur Bildende Kunste in Hamburg, and Sigmar Polke was his professor there. This is the claim the study was asked to verify carefully, and it holds, on four independent sources: the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao bio states plainly that at the Hamburg school "Sigmar Polke was his professor"; the Serpentine exhibition text says he studied there "under painter Sigmar Polke"; Christie's says he was "taught by Sigmar Polke at the Hochschule fur Bildende Kunste between 1978 and 1981"; and the International Film Festival Rotterdam bio says he studied under Claus Bohmle and Sigmar Polke. The dates split: the weight of evidence says 1978 to 1981 (Hetzler's vita, Gagosian, the Serpentine text, Christie's), while the Guggenheim Bilbao says 1977 until 1981. Against all of these, Wikipedia and artnet both say he "graduated in 1978" -- which cannot stand beside a 1978-1981 study span and is almost certainly a confusion of the start year with the graduation year; the 1978-1981 dating is used here and the 1978 graduation is carried as unresolved (U-1). A further outlier, Prabook, claims he entered in 1974, studied under Polke for seven years, and was taught by Joseph Beuys at the start of his education; none of it is corroborated by any other source read and all of it is carried unresolved (U-5), not printed as fact. (C24900, leg 1 C24804, C24805; leg 3 C24868; synthesis C24900)

His first solo exhibition was at Galerie Max Hetzler in 1981, under the title "Bevor ihr malt, mach ich das lieber" (Before you paint, I'd rather do it myself). The city is disputed: Hetzler's own exhibition list and the Guggenheim Bilbao place it in Stuttgart, while artnet and Christie's say Berlin; Hetzler's list is the primary source and Stuttgart is favoured (U-2). The work of this period is dark in every sense. The Bilbao bio says dark brown tones predominate and the paintings represent interior spaces, cellars, apartments, and museums, always deserted. The mumok text says the same thing from the other side: his initial turn to painting was characterized by restrictions and impertinence -- drab browns, hackneyed symbols (such as the mirror), ideologically negative themes (like the self-portrait), tactical steps with which he drew painting into embarrassing situations. (C24901, leg 1 C24806; leg 2 W01, W02; synthesis C24901)

The self-portrait is the thread that runs through the early work and never quite leaves it. "Selbstportrait mit verschissener Unterhose und blauer Mauritius" (Self-portrait with shitty underpants and Blue Mauritius), 1984, is described by the ZKM catalogue text as his first radical break with the notion of the romantic artistic genius and its compensatory function for the middle classes. "Fruhstuck Now (Self-Portrait)", also 1984, was one of eight self-portraits in the "Self-Portraits" show at Skarstedt, New York, in 2002; the New Museum's "Home and Garden" in 2015 was built in large part from the 1980s and 1990s self-portraits. "Selbstportrait beim Topfern" (Self-portrait while Potting), 1986, lacquer on canvas, 240 x 200 cm, shows the artist as a craftsman sitting on a bench making pottery, in a simplified room with a distorted perspective. The mirror paintings of the mid-1980s, including the large "Flur" (Corridor), 1982, oil, lacquer, and mirror on canvas, 250 x 200 cm, glue real mirrors to the canvas and paint partly over them, so colour surfaces, perspectival space, and mirrored real space overlap into dark scenarios. The earliest dated work in this study, "Eingebung", 1982 (W01), is wood and nails, 178 x 179 x 38 cm, not paint on a flat support -- a relief that already treats the picture as a built thing. (C24902, leg 1 C24807; leg 2 W01-W05; synthesis C24902)

The "bad painting" tag attaches to a specific series: paintings limited to the three primary colours red, yellow, and blue, which included his infamous 1986 portrait of Adolf Hitler. The provocation was programmatic, not accidental -- the same logic as the grey-only paintings and the drab-brown interiors, a self-imposed rule that forces the picture into an embarrassing corner and then asks it to survive there. Oehlen is grouped with the Junge Wilde (young wild artists) -- Buttner, Kippenberger, Georg Herold -- who sought work that defied categorisation and refuted the artistic status quo. The label is real but it is also a trap: Gagosian's bio stresses that his work deconstructed the medium to its constituent elements -- colour, gesture, motion, and time -- and evolved out of constraints he applied to his own process, which is a cooler, more deliberate project than the "wild" label suggests. In the late 1980s he coined the term "post-non-representational painting" for his basic conceptual approach: to deconstruct the oppositions between figurative and non-figurative, figure and background, and colour and line, so that the polemical jabs spread across his early groups of work were brought into the picture itself, in mostly very large tableaux with an inexhaustible arsenal of painterly signs. The Kunstkritikk review adds a hinge: in about 1988, four years before the computer turn, Oehlen had abandoned his signature figurations and started painting abstractions -- intentionally lacklustre and unrehearsed -- and found himself struggling for a motif. The machine arrived at exactly that moment. (C24903, leg 1 C24808, C24809; leg 2 M4, W06, W08; synthesis C24903)

In 1988 Oehlen moved to Andalusia for a year with Kippenberger, and then to Madrid. The Bilbao bio dates his first drawings and paintings of trees to this stage. The tree then becomes the longest thread in his work: the Bilbao bio says he is still working on a series of tree paintings he had begun in 1989, and Hetzler's 2024 "Schweinekubismus" release says the tree motif has fascinated him as a form and system since the early 1980s -- a start-date variant carried unresolved (U-3), with 1988-1989 the better documented of the two. The tree paintings (Baumbilder) work by a rule Gagosian states precisely: perambulating black lines combined with carefully blended gradations, a recognisable figurative element (a trunk, a branching line) held inside an otherwise abstract field. Major tree moments: "Tree-drawings" at Richard Telles, Los Angeles, 2003; "Woods Near Oehle" at the Cleveland Museum of Art, 2016-2017; the duo show with Carroll Dunham, "Baume / Trees", at Kunsthalle Dusseldorf in 2019, travelling to Sprengel Museum Hannover in 2020. The Gagosian tree works run large: "Untitled (Baum 30)", 2015, oil on Dibond, 300 x 200 cm; "Untitled (Baum 84)", 2016, oil on Dibond, 250 x 250 cm. (C24904, leg 1 C24810; leg 2 W08, W19, M8; synthesis C24904)

In 1990, during a six-month stay in Los Angeles, he bought a computer -- a Texas Instruments laptop, per the Fondation Louis Vuitton text -- and began making drawings that in 1992 would lead to the Computer Paintings, a series the Bilbao bio says he ended in 2008. The process, as the sources agree on it: he drew with rudimentary graphics programs on the laptop, using a mouse, choosing every brush shape and size from a menu, working with stamp tools and a small selection of basic motifs; he then had the drawings printed and enlarged to the format he wanted to paint on, accentuating the extreme pixilation of the low-resolution black-and-white originals; the enlarged image was screen-printed onto canvas or paper and then reworked by hand in acrylic, oil, or ink. The first works from 1992 are black and white, silk-screened on paper and overpainted ("Untitled" and "Das Lampchen im Inneren der Erde", both 1992); later works revisit the same early drawings in colour -- "Blind in Texas" (1995, screen printing and oil on canvas, 240 x 200 cm) in purple and white, "Fleisch" (1995) in flesh colours, then the garishly colourful "Captain Jack" (1997) and the huge "Annihilator" (2001/2006). The Hamburger Kunsthalle's 2024-2025 "Computerbilder" show, marking his 70th birthday, presented twenty large-format works from a first series in the early 1990s and a second in the early 2000s. His own line on the method, quoted in the Palazzo Grassi catalogue: "Normally the computer helps you to do something that otherwise you wouldn't do. Computers open a window onto the future. Here things are reversed. The painter corrects the pixels, and ultimately the computer image gives rise to a hand-painted picture." The start year carries a small variant (U-4): 1992 is best supported for the first works, but Hetzler's 2026 text says "first created between 1991-2008" and Kantor's Artforum piece calls it an eighteen-year series begun in 1990; 1990 is plausibly the laptop purchase and first experiments, 1992 the first exhibited works. (C24905, leg 1 C24811, C24812; leg 2 M1, W10; synthesis C24905)

Running alongside the computer work, Oehlen began his grey paintings in 1997, a series the Bilbao bio says ended in 2008 -- the same end year as the Computer Paintings, which suggests 2008 was a deliberate clearing of the decks. The grey paintings are grey fields crisscrossed by brushwork, a restriction of the same kind as the drab browns and the three primaries. In the late 1990s he also began spraying paint over collaged imagery transferred to canvas by the gigantic inkjet printers used to manufacture billboards. In 2002 he began working with collage in the Bilbao bio's dating (collage is also dated to 1982 in the same bio, for the mirror paintings' glued elements -- the two dates describe different collage practices and are not treated here as a contradiction). In 2007 he began a new series using advertising posters: colour-blocked advertisements become an extension of the canvas, providing fragmented, readymade surfaces for his markings. The 1994 group show at the Renaissance Society, "Albert Oehlen: Recent Paintings", holds a dozen canvases in nearly one fixed format around 94 by 79 inches -- "Little Symphony", "Check-up", "Twins", "Kaleidoscope", "Embraceable You", "Folk Tale" among them -- mixing oil, enamel, acrylic and silkscreen ink, so the register can swing from gestural abstraction to silkscreened figuration without the scale changing. (C24906, leg 1 C24813; leg 2 W11, W12, W15, W16, W17, M3, M5; synthesis C24906)

After visiting the exhibition "Action Painting" at the Fondation Beyeler in Riehen (Basel) in 2008, Oehlen began painting directly with his fingers -- Fingermalerei. He jettisons the brush and applies paint to the canvas surface directly with his bare hands. Jordan Kantor's Artforum piece (2011) lays out the layered logic: the graphic elements -- collaged advertisement posters, text fragments, printouts of digital images, a computer-generated rainbow sunburst -- are applied to the canvas ground beforehand with water-soluble materials, and the oil paint can only be applied subsequently, when manipulation of that earlier stratum is no longer possible. So the finger painting, applied last, responds to printed material beneath it that the artist can no longer change: in "FM 17" (2008), sweeping areas of purple, white, and green paint rearticulate a computer-generated rainbow sunburst and the ruled outlines of tilting text. Kantor counts four dozen paintings in the series to date (at 2011); later, sparser works such as "FM 45" (2011) put the finger-painted marks on an otherwise unarticulated white ground, where they are the primary visual action. The Fondation Louis Vuitton notes a telling split: the 2008 Finger Paintings are partially computer-produced, while the same series in 2014 is free of any manufactured visuals. Oehlen's own summary, quoted by Kantor: all of his abstract paintings come from an inability to paint what he wants to paint. (C24907, leg 1 C24814; leg 2 M2; synthesis C24907)

In 1997 Oehlen began the John Graham Remix series, which he has returned to ever since: he takes elements from the American modernist John Graham's painting "Tramonto Spaventoso" (Terrifying Sunset, 1940-49), first found as a small illustration in a Dore Ashton book on the New York School, and remixes them across multiple compositions. The series became the spine of his Serpentine Galleries show in 2019-2020, where new paintings based on Graham's picture matched the scale and size of the four horizontal paintings by Mark Rothko in the Rothko Chapel in Houston, installed in the Serpentine's domed central space as Oehlen's interpretation of the chapel, with a newly configured soundtrack by the hardcore trio Steamboat Switzerland playing at intervals. His remark: "I am not interested in the idea of staging my work in a space specifically conceived for it. I think that art should adapt to the architecture or fight with it." The same series supplied the "Tramonto Spaventoso" shows at the Marciano Art Foundation and Gagosian Beverly Hills in 2021. The centrepiece triptych, "never sleep again / at the steering wheel a man is a man / starter - turn me on" (2019, W20), is charcoal and acrylic on canvas in three parts, overall 370.4 x 624.3 cm per Gagosian (the Brooklyn Rail gives the height as 364.5 cm). The "Elevator Paintings" of 2016 are a single work in nine parts -- eight paintings from 2016 and one from 1996 -- using erasure and layering to juxtapose bright and muddy colours. (C24908, leg 1 C24815; leg 2 W20, M9; synthesis C24908)

The 2010s also brought the institutional consolidation: a retrospective of over 80 works from the 1980s to 2005 at mumok, Vienna, June 8 to October 20, 2013; inclusion in the 55th Venice Biennale in 2013, in Massimiliano Gioni's central exhibition "The Encyclopedic Palace" (not the German pavilion); "Die 5000 Finger von Dr. O" at Museum Wiesbaden in 2014; "Home and Garden" at the New Museum, New York, June 10 to September 13, 2015, described as his first New York museum exhibition; "Woods Near Oehle" at the Cleveland Museum of Art in 2016-2017; "Recent Works" at the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao in 2016; and the career-spanning "Cows by the Water" at Palazzo Grassi, Venice, April 8, 2018 to January 6, 2019, organised around his relationship with music. No earlier Venice Biennale appearance was found in any source read this run: only 2013 is documented (U-6). Two corrections to the record are carried here: the Serpentine show was 2019-2020, not 2006 (2006 was the Whitechapel show "I Will Always Champion Good Painting", with "I Will Always Champion Bad Painting" at Arnolfini, Bristol), and the 2015 New York museum show was the New Museum, not the Whitney -- no Whitney solo show for Oehlen was found in any source read this run. (C24909, leg 1 C24801, C24816; leg 2 W20; synthesis C24909)

Music is not a side interest but a working method and a second career. In the 1990s Oehlen briefly ran his own independent label, Leiterwagen, putting out experimental electronica. Since the late 1990s he has played in the bands Red Krayola and Van Oehlen, and his artwork is on CDs by Gastr del Sol, Arthur Russell, and Child Abuse. The Palazzo Grassi show used free jazz as the key to his whole trajectory: the improvising musician risks failure in each improvisation to find a new sound, and Oehlen's own line is that his painting "always suggests that it was done quickly, and is therefore comparable to improvised music". The "Conduction" series, named after the structured improvisation developed by the musician Lawrence "Butch" Morris, is the most literal form of that borrowing. He was Professor of Painting at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf from 2000 to 2009, a date agreed by every source read. The market followed late and then fast: a 1984 self-portrait sold at Christie's London in July 2014 for $1.8 million, roughly three times its $670,000 high estimate; "Self-Portrait with Palette" sold at Christie's in March 2017 for $3,623,230; "Self-Portrait with Empty Hands" sold at Sotheby's London in June 2019 for $7,542,157, a new record for the artist. The critical verdict is split and is not smoothed over here: Peter Schjeldahl wrote in 2015 that his pictures "possess no unity of composition, only unremitting energy", with "stabs of beauty in passages that reveal Oehlen to be, almost grudgingly, a fantastic colorist"; Philippe Dagen, writing in Le Monde about the 2011 Nimes show, concluded he was "of only limited importance". (C24910, leg 1 C24817, C24818; synthesis C24910)

The recent years are a return to old series with new means rather than a new departure. The "Fn Paintings" showed at Skarstedt, New York, in 2019; the "u.b.B." paintings ran 2020-2022; the "Omega Man" series began in 2021 and is ongoing, shown at Reena Spaulings, Los Angeles, in 2021 and at the Friedrichs Foundation, Weidingen, in 2023. The Computer Paintings were revisited from 2024 onward: "Computerbilder" at Hamburger Kunsthalle, September 13, 2024 to March 2, 2025, and a further Hetzler Berlin exhibition announced for June 12 to August 8, 2026. "Schweinekubismus" showed at Galerie Max Hetzler, Berlin, in 2024; "New Paintings" at Gagosian, London, in 2024; and "Endless Summer", a show of new and recent paintings on the theme of the bather, at Galerie Max Hetzler and Gagosian in Paris in 2025, with a duo show with Kim Gordon at Corbett vs. Dempsey, Chicago, the same year. He continues to live and work in Switzerland, exhibiting regularly at Galerie Max Hetzler since 1981. (C24911, leg 1 C24819; leg 2 W09; synthesis C24911)

## M1 -- Computer-aided composition: draw on the machine, print, paint over

Oehlen bought his first laptop in 1990 and began the Computer Paintings, formulating a small set of digital motifs within four years and using them in variation until the series ended in 2008; a second series followed in the early 2000s. The method, as Sotheby's, Skarstedt, the Hamburger Kunsthalle and the Guggenheim Bilbao all describe it: draw with a mouse in an early graphics program, print the drawing enlarged by inkjet plotter, transfer it to canvas, then paint over the print in acrylic and oil, sometimes with silkscreen. The crudeness is the point and also an accident of the tools: the lines come out thick, pixellated, squared off and angular, and the printed image is so coarse that Oehlen had to fill in the squares by hand -- the human hand finishing what the machine could not. The computer is a distancing medium, an "anti-expressive" factor: it replaces the unconscious wandering of the hand with a drawn motif that can be repeated, varied, scaled and reprinted. Oehlen: "The plan was to be avant-garde on the technical side too." Works: W10 (Untitled, 1992, oil and enamel on canvas, 200 x 200 cm, MoMA); the Untitled (1) computer painting in the Sotheby's lot essay; the black-and-white grisaille series as a whole. (C24913, leg 2 M1, W10; leg 1 C24811; synthesis C24913)

The watchable behaviour is a line that is too straight and too stepped to be a brushstroke, interrupted by a lyrical sprayed or washed line that could only be a hand -- the picture keeps flipping between machined and made. A generative piece could draw a motif with a coarse grid tool, print it at low resolution, then paint over it with a loose hand, letting the pixel steps stay visible where the paint stops. Never a clean vector look; the steps are the evidence. (C24914, leg 2 M1; synthesis C24914)

**Takeaway:** draw the motif with a coarse machine, print it, then paint over it by hand -- let the pixel steps stay visible where the paint stops. (C24915)

## M2 -- Finger painting as a constraint system

After visiting the "Action Painting" exhibition at the Fondation Beyeler in 2008, Oehlen began painting directly with his fingers (Fingermalerei). The finger paintings use colour-blocked advertisements as an extension of the canvas: damaged or torn signs and magazine ads form fragmented, readymade surfaces, and Oehlen marks them with his hands as well as brushes, rags and spray-cans. The 2008 finger paintings are partially computer-produced; the 2014 series is free of any manufactured visuals. The finger is a constraint, not a gimmick: it cannot make a fine line, it smears, it leaves a print of the body, and it forces the painter to work at the scale of the hand rather than the wrist. The tension the paintings stage is between man-made and machine-made imagery -- the ad's slick printed colour blocks against visceral smudges, swipes and erasures. Works: the Finger Paintings series, 2008 and 2014; W16 and W17 show the same ad-collage ground with brushwork rather than fingers. (C24916, leg 2 M2; leg 1 C24814; synthesis C24916)

The watchable behaviour is a mark with a fingerprint's width and a smear's edge, dragging printed colour into painted colour until the ad is half-legible and half-abstract. A generative piece could take a flat colour-blocked ad layout and let a broad, soft, finger-wide tool smear across it, revealing the print beneath in streaks -- the print must stay partly readable, or the tension is gone. (C24917, leg 2 M2; synthesis C24917)

**Takeaway:** ban the fine tool, use a finger-wide smear on a printed ad, and let the ad stay half-legible under the marks. (C24918)

## M3 -- Collage and transfer of printed imagery

From the late 1990s Oehlen sprayed and brushed over collaged imagery transferred to canvas by the gigantic inkjet printers used to manufacture billboards; from 2007 he built paintings on advertising posters themselves. The transfer is mechanical and large: a magazine photograph, a poster, a piece of photowallpaper is enlarged, printed and pasted or transferred onto the support, and then painted over, erased, sprayed and re-drawn. In W16, "Der rosa Salon" (The Pink Parlor), 2004, oil and paper on plastic, 280 x 300 cm, a magazine cutout of a woman curled up in a reclining chair is pasted into a sketchily contoured room, with a second, crudely painted chair beside her -- a collage interior in which the printed photograph and the awkward painted drawing share one room and neither wins. The support matters: oil and paper on plastic, not canvas -- the painting is built on a slick, non-absorbent ground that refuses the paint the way a magazine page refuses it. In W20 patterned fabric is worked into the canvas alongside charcoal and spray paint. The printed image is never an illustration to be respected: it is a ground to argue with, and the painting is finished when the print and the paint have fused into a single surface where you can no longer tell which came first. Works: W14 (silkscreened elements in the 1994 group), W16, W17, W20, "Self-Portrait as Spring". (C24919, leg 2 M3, W16, W20; leg 1 C24813; synthesis C24919)

The watchable behaviour is a recognisable printed fragment -- a chair, a face, a letter -- sinking under paint and then resurfacing a few inches away, so the eye keeps finding the same image at different depths. A generative piece could start from a real printed layout, then let paint pour over it in washes and erasures until only fragments survive. Never a filter over a photograph; the print must be a ground the paint argues with. (C24920, leg 2 M3; synthesis C24920)

**Takeaway:** print a real image as the ground, then paint and erase until only fragments survive -- the print and the paint must fuse into one surface. (C24921)

## M4 -- The "bad painting" deliberate-awkwardness system

Bad painting is not sloppiness; it is a system with rules. Oehlen and Kippenberger adopted it in the early 1980s as a deliberate rejection of aesthetic standards, aiming, in Oehlen's words, "to get as far away from meaning as possible". The moves are specific: paint very fast; layer colours and shapes that do not make sense together; draw badly on purpose, especially the hands; use crude cartoons, awkward ladders, bloodshot eyeballs; let the picture fail at its own stated task. The MoMA label on W08 states it plainly, and Christie's quotes his governing line: "The artist's 'freedom' is the duty not to be boring. You have to put painting under stress, that's what matters." The awkwardness is measured against the history it refuses: the self-portrait is posed as a problem because of the "huge historical apparatus attached to it". Works: W03, W04, W05 (bad hands, bad drawing, monumental scale), W08, W09 (the redacted pointing hand, the eyeball), W18 (the obstructed text). In "Fn 33" (W09, 1990), a cartoon-like, disembodied, bloodshot eyeball hovers in the upper right corner, surveying a grand landslide-like downward movement of bright yellow and dirty white brushwork against a muddy yellow-ochre ground; The "Fn" of the title means "footnote": the grand ambition of painting reduced to a footnote or academic citation. (C24922, leg 2 M4, W09; leg 1 C24809; synthesis C24922)

The watchable behaviour is a picture that looks wrong in a way that is clearly decided, not accidental -- a hand drawn badly next to a passage of exquisite glazing, a ladder that cannot be climbed, a finger blotted out. A generative piece could draw a figure with one deliberate defect -- a hand with the wrong number of fingers, a perspective that collapses -- and keep the rest of the painting competent, so the defect reads as a decision. Never uniformly sloppy; if everything is bad, nothing is. (C24923, leg 2 M4; synthesis C24923)

**Takeaway:** make one thing deliberately, legibly wrong next to passages of real competence -- the defect must read as a decision, not an accident. (C24924)

## M5 -- Palette as system

Oehlen works by imposing a palette and then obeying it until it breaks. Until 1987 he restricted himself largely to grey and brown; the 1986 Hitler portrait broke the rule in three primary colours, loudly, on the most loaded subject. The Computer Paintings are black and white only -- the Guggenheim Bilbao reads the exclusive use of black as one of the challenges the artist poses himself, and the grey scale lets gradients and veils of pigment do the work of colour. The Grey Paintings (1997-2008, in two phases) ban colour entirely: no colour, only grey, so the constraint is perfectly evident even without being told -- and inside that ban, figuration is allowed back in, because the grey makes a portrait read as a quotation rather than a likeness. "C.C." (W15, 2003), a grey bust-length portrait, was called by the critic Nils Norman "a botched Gerhard Richter, a lazy parody that gives up half way home". The tree paintings restrict themselves to red, black, blue and white in various combinations. The 1994 Renaissance Society group, by contrast, is the palette let loose: corals, peaches, pastels, emerald, khaki, bright yellow. Works: W06 and the 1986 Hitler portrait (grey-brown rule and its break); W10 (black and white); W15 (grey only); W19 (red, black, blue, white); W13 (palette let loose). In "3rd Gear - It's All Right" (W13, 1998), titled from the Beach Boys' "Little Honda", Christie's describes a churning galaxy of corals, peaches and pastels beset by swathes of emerald and khaki, strafed with dark brown rays. (C24925, leg 2 M5, W13, W15; leg 1 C24813; synthesis C24925)

The watchable behaviour is a picture whose colour decisions feel like a rule being followed -- you can see the palette before you can see the picture -- and then one colour that violates it. A generative piece could pick two or three colours, forbid everything else, then let one forbidden colour in for a single mark and watch the whole picture reorganize around it. Never a full-spectrum default; the palette must be a visible constraint. (C24926, leg 2 M5; synthesis C24926)

**Takeaway:** impose a visible palette rule, obey it long enough to feel, then let one forbidden colour in for a single mark. (C24927)

## M6 -- Scale shifts

Oehlen changes scale the way other painters change colour: it is a dial, and the same motif at a different size is a different painting. The 1984 self-portraits run to more than two and a half metres wide (W04), while the 1982 mirror self-portrait is 60 cm square (W02); the 1994 group holds a fixed format around 94 x 79 inches (W11, W12) while the 1988 Arrows canvas is 276 x 375 cm (W07); the tree paintings run from 250 cm square to 375 x 250 cm (W19); the 2019 triptych is over six metres wide (W20). Inside a single painting he does the same thing: the Sotheby's essay on "Geigenbau (Lutherie)" (W14, 2003, oil on canvas, 110 1/4 x 133 7/8 inches) notes that he introduces fragments of representational images in inconsistent scales and varies the size of the abstract units, so the relative size of each shape moves the viewer's attention towards, away from, and across the picture plane in rapid succession. A head of hair becomes a woman in profile; a dark rectangle becomes a portrait if the canvas is rotated. Arcing lines of brown and beige wood refer to the title -- lutherie, violin-making. Oehlen's own account: "I had never composed a painting; that was something I didn't want to bother with. You grease the whole canvas evenly anyway." (C24928, leg 2 M6, W14; synthesis C24928)

The watchable behaviour is the eye zooming without moving: a small motif suddenly reads as a landscape, a large shape suddenly reads as a detail. A generative piece could take one motif and render it at three wildly different sizes in the same frame, so scale itself becomes the composition. Never a uniform zoom; the jumps must be abrupt. (C24929, leg 2 M6; synthesis C24929)

**Takeaway:** render one motif at three wildly different sizes in the same frame -- let scale jumps, not colour, compose the picture. (C24930)

## M7 -- Self-portraiture as a serial system

The self-portrait is not a likeness; it is a series with a fixed subject and a changing rule, run for forty years. Eight self-portraits from 1983-84 were shown together at Skarstedt in 2001-02, plus one made for the occasion; the 1980s guises are potter, ethnographer, stable-boy, painter and cyclops. Each portrait changes the painter's job: making a vase (W04), sitting as a marble bust with spring onions (W03), standing with a horse (W05), wearing shitty underpants next to a Blue Mauritius stamp. The self-portraits of this period used a technique Oehlen called "glazing painting": many layers of thin oil paint, modelling the subject slowly instead of putting the right colour down at once. The joke title -- "Breakfast Now" -- sits against a method that is patient and old-masterly, and that contradiction is the work. The later portraits keep the serial logic: "Self-Portrait with Open Mouth" (2001), "Self-Portrait as Spring" (2006, photowallpaper and oil, 265 x 385 cm), "Self-Portrait with Palette" (sold at Christie's, March 2017, for $3,623,230), "Self-Portrait with Empty Hands" (sold at Sotheby's, June 2019). The subject never changes -- it is always him -- so every change in the picture is a change in the rule: glazing painting in 1984, mirror in 1982, photowallpaper in 2006. Works: W02, W03, W04, W05, plus the later portraits named above. (C24931, leg 2 M7, W03; leg 1 C24807; synthesis C24931)

The watchable behaviour is recognition without likeness: the same face returns, but the face is never the point -- the job, the prop and the technique are. A generative piece could fix one face and re-render it under a different rule each time -- as a potter, as a bust, as a stamp, as a wallpaper -- so the series, not the single image, is the work. Never a single flattering likeness. (C24932, leg 2 M7; synthesis C24932)

**Takeaway:** fix the face, change the rule every time -- the series is the work, not any single portrait. (C24933)

## M8 -- Tree paintings as a repeated motif with variation

The tree is Oehlen's longest-running motif: begun in 1989, when he decided to become an abstract painter, and still underway at the last report. The mechanism is variation inside a fixed schema. The early version is literal and isolated -- a single twisted tree in a misty, dripping landscape (W08, 1989, oil and enamel on canvas, 240 x 200 cm, MoMA), undermining the identifiable image through bad painting -- the MoMA label states plainly that Oehlen wanted to create "bad paintings" on purpose. The 2013-16 version is schematic: black trunk-and-branch silhouettes on aluminium panels, hand-painted in oil to look like the digital marks of design software, in palettes of red, black, blue and white. The variation runs across scale, palette combinations, and support (canvas, Dibond, foil, magazine ads, a real sapling projected as a shadow in the 2015 Gagosian installation). Mondrian is the named precedent: he investigated the dissolution of the figurative shape based on a tree, and Oehlen uses the same resource "as vehicles for a methodical deflation of content" -- a recognizable thing, drawn so cleanly and so often that its meaning drains out. Works: W08 (the literal tree), W19 with Baum 42 and Baum 37 (the schematic trees), the 2015 Gagosian works on paper and the sapling installation. (C24934, leg 2 M8, W08, W19; leg 1 C24810; synthesis C24934)

The watchable behaviour is a tree that stops being a tree: at first you read trunk and branches, then the silhouette flattens into a diagram, then into a digital mark, then back into a tree. A generative piece could grow one branching structure, then re-render it under stricter and stricter rules until the tree is a logo, then let it grow wild again. Never a naturalistic tree; the motif must be a diagram that remembers being a tree. (C24935, leg 2 M8; synthesis C24935)

**Takeaway:** take one branching structure and re-render it under stricter rules until it is a diagram -- then let it grow wild again. (C24936)

## M9 -- Remix: one source painting, endlessly reinterpreted

The John Graham Remix is Oehlen's most disciplined appropriation system: a single source painting, John Graham's "Tramonto Spaventoso" (Terrifying Sunset, 1940-49), discovered by Oehlen in the 1990s, reinterpreted across dozens of compositions over thirty years. The source is puzzle-like -- a mermaid, a mustachioed man with a monocle -- and Oehlen takes its motifs apart and reassembles them: the moustached face becomes a profiled head buzzed by four small identical faces; the mermaid becomes a female figure in watercolour; the whole image is remade at the scale of Rothko's chapel paintings, in charcoal, watercolour, acrylic, spray paint and patterned fabric. The Serpentine show (2019-20) framed the project as an interpretation of the Rothko Chapel itself, with a soundtrack by the hardcore trio Steamboat Switzerland playing at intervals. The rule is strict -- one source, no other -- and the freedom is total inside it: any medium, any scale, any degree of legibility. Works: W20 and the other seven paintings of the Tramonto Spaventoso project (2019-20); the earlier Graham-derived works from the 1980s to the present shown at the Serpentine. (C24937, leg 2 M9, W20; leg 1 C24815; synthesis C24937)

The watchable behaviour is a motif you almost recognize: a moustache, a monocle, a mermaid's curve, appearing and disappearing across compositions like a tune you know but cannot name. A generative piece could take one fixed source image, extract three motifs from it, and recombine them under a different rule each round, so the source is always present and never intact. Never a copy; the source must be a vehicle, not a picture. (C24938, leg 2 M9; synthesis C24938)

**Takeaway:** fix one source image, extract three motifs, and recombine them under a new rule each round -- always present, never intact. (C24939)

## M10 -- The self-imposed rule as engine

Underneath every series above is the same device: Oehlen begins by imposing a set of rules or structural limitations, then works inside them until something surprising happens. The rules are concrete and often absurd: restrict the palette to grey and brown; work only in black and white; paint slower; paint only with fingers; use only a tree; use only one source painting; make the computer do the drawing. Gagosian's formulation, repeated across the 2014, 2015 and 2016 shows, is that he "often begins by imposing a set of rules or structural limitations -- restricting his palette or deliberately working at a slow pace -- while challenging himself to approach each painting differently." The rule is not a style; it is a problem generator. The solution is never the same twice: the same grey rule gives a botched Richter (W15) and a bulbous-headed creature ("Smoker", 1999); the same tree rule gives a misty landscape (W08) and a digital-looking diagram (W19); the same computer rule gives a black-and-white grisaille (W10) and a colour explosion (W18). (C24940, leg 2 M10; leg 1 C24808; synthesis C24940)

The watchable behaviour is a system visibly straining against its own limit -- the rule holds, the picture pushes, and the interesting moment is where the rule bends without breaking. A generative piece could declare its rule on screen -- three colours, one motif, one tool -- run it until the picture is nearly exhausted, then change exactly one term and watch it reorganize. Never a hidden rule; the viewer must see the constraint to enjoy the escape from it. (C24941, leg 2 M10; synthesis C24941)

**Takeaway:** declare the rule on screen, run it until the picture is nearly exhausted, then change exactly one term and watch it reorganize. (C24942)

## The aesthetic logic: freedom is a duty, and the rule is how you earn it

Oehlen's career is one act performed fifty ways: set a rule, then paint against it until the rule breaks in an interesting place. He said it himself in two sentences that belong together: "The artist's 'freedom' is the duty not to be boring. You have to put painting under stress, that's what matters" -- and, on meaning, "Think whatever you like. It is boring to talk about meaning. I'm not looking for the public's connection or understanding." The stress is applied by the rule, not by the subject: a grey palette, a computer, a finger, a tree, a single source painting, a mirror, a poster. Each rule is a different way of putting the painting under stress, and each series is the record of what happened when the stress was applied. That is why the work looks so various -- self-portraits, computer paintings, grey paintings, finger paintings, tree paintings, remixes -- and why it is, underneath, a single practice: the freedom is not the absence of rules but the choice of which rule to break next. His own summary of what he wants in a painting -- "delicacy and coarseness, colour and vagueness, and, underlying them all, a base note of hysteria" -- is a rule for the viewer too: look for the delicate thing next to the coarse thing, and the hysteria underneath both. (C24943, leg 2 aesthetic logic; leg 1 C24809, C24818; synthesis C24943)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the rule is the subject: the palette, the tool, the motif, the source is a co-author, and changing the rule is how he changes what painting can be -- Schnabel changes the support, Oehlen changes the constraint. Second, badness is a dial: the hand that draws badly on purpose also lays down the exquisite glazing of "Fruhstuck Now". Third, time is a material: thirty years from finding Graham's picture to remaking it at chapel scale, eighteen years of the computer series, forty years of the same face under a different rule. Bleckner paints the light that survives inside the dark; Schnabel paints the thing that survives inside the picture; Oehlen paints the rule that survives inside the breaking of it. (C24944, leg 2 aesthetic logic; leg 1 C24818; synthesis C24944)

The evolution is the proof. 1981-85: deserted interiors in dark brown, then the self-portrait as a problem, painted badly on purpose at monumental scale. 1986-89: the palette rule and its loud break, abstraction with a literal tree inside it. 1990-2008: the computer paintings, the hand partly removed, the pixel filled in by hand. 1994-2004: the mixed-media group paintings, silkscreen and enamel, the white ground and the post-non-representational turn, the grey paintings running in parallel. 2007-14: advertising posters as ground, finger painting, the ad half-legible under the smear. 2013-16: the tree as diagram, hand-painted to look digital. 2019-20: one source painting remixed at chapel scale. What stays constant is the question the painting asks without answering: what happens when you set yourself a rule you cannot quite obey, and then paint anyway? (C24945, leg 2 evolution; leg 1 C24820; synthesis C24945)
