# Cesar Moro: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-04. ig-artist-study #333.

**Site:** Cesar Moro, pseudonym of Alfredo Quispez Asin (Lima, 1903 - Lima, 10 January 1956, age 52), Peruvian poet and painter, the only Hispanic poet to write for Andre Breton's surrealist journals of the 1920s and 30s, and the first Latin American to join the surrealist group on his own initiative rather than by Breton's recruitment. Over 300 compositions in French and about 70 poems in Spanish; lifetime books: Le chateau de grisou (Mexico, 1943), Lettre d'amour (Mexico, 1944), Trafalgar Square (Lima, 1954); everything else posthumous via his executor Andre Coyne. Translator of the French surrealists into Spanish (Peret, Carrington, Arp, Eluard, Picasso, Dali, Reverdy, Breton, Artaud); organizer of the first surrealist exposition in South America (Lima, 1935) and co-organizer of the 1940 International Exposition of Surrealism in Mexico City; art teacher to the mentally ill at the Hospital Larco Herrera. Identity anchors: the self-renaming; the foreign tongue as optic; the love-letter as book form; the impossible animal; translation as lifeline; the pun engine; the breathless enumeration; the dated break; the exhibition as weapon; the invective as craft; the aphorism ledger; the posthumous machine.

**Doorway:** Hop from the benjamin-peret study: from the French poet who wrote the marvelous into an operating system to the Peruvian who translated him into Spanish. Aldo Pellegrini's 1961 Antologia de la poesia surrealista prints Moro's versions of Peret's poems under the titles "Yo sublime" (Je sublime) and "Yo no como de ese pan" (Je ne mange pas de ce pain-la), with the Advertencia explaining that Moro's translations of Peret were made in Mexico in consultation with Peret himself, who was there at the time, and that Westphalen's Spanish version of Moro's own Lettre d'amour was likewise examined and approved by Moro. #332 closed with a Breton-vs-Peret correction and a v2 research supersession; #333 sits at the other side of that same Mexico table. Moro is the one who carried Peret's engine across languages while breaking with Breton himself, publicly, in 1944 - a break he made on two stated levels, formal and moral, and dated like a legal filing.

**Depth:** Thirteen sources read end to end: EN and ES Wikipedia pages, the EBSCO biography, the Getty Research Institute finding aid 980029 (Moro papers), Jason Wilson's long "The Sole South American Surrealist" essay, Americo Ferrari's two Aurora Boreal studies (translation/bilingualism; freedom of the word), Omar Castillo's Revista Altazor survey, Sarah Yan's 2025 Full Stop review of the Cardboard House bilingual Equestrian Turtle edition, Pellegrini's 1961 Advertencia, the antologiaspoeticas.com poem archive, and the Infobae 2024 profile. Works inspected: 9 full poems read complete (El fuego y la poesia I-III, A vista perdida, Abeja negra, Batalla al borde de una catarata, Carta a Antonio, Carta de amor in Westphalen's version, Como un piano de cola, El agua lenta el camino lento, El dominio encantado), 5 more full texts via Ferrari (Eleve age de l'air, the belle/baie/abbe word-play poem, Lettres, Vie de l'air, Oraculo's homophony), excerpts of 10 more (Infancia, Viking, Despacio, Odor and Gaze, The Faint Footstep of the Nocturnal Demon, An Earthen Path, the last poem of 8 August 1955, Viaje hacia la noche, Amo el amor, Adresse aux trois regnes), and close descriptions of 8 more (Renommee de l'amour, the Violette Noziere poem, Pierre mere, Fata Alaska, Varios leones al crepusculo, the Biografia peruana, the indigenista attack, the 1940 exposition manifesto). His paintings were NOT viewed first-hand: only titles are on record (Pedestrian 1926, Untitled Painting with the inscription "Eluard" 1926, The Art of Reading the Future 1935, Cover for the Blind 1939, two undescribed collages in the Getty papers), and the Trafalgar Square Varo cover and the Lettre d'amour Alice Paalen frontispiece are unviewed. No monograph read end to end. Carried unresolved: the birth date (9 vs 19 vs 31 August 1903 across sources), the death cause (leukemia vs unknown illness), and the single-source ES Wikipedia claim that Pierre de Soleils suggested the title of Octavio Paz's Piedra de sol. No mechanism invented; everything below is footed to the reads above.

## M1. The self-renaming

The signature identity move, performed before any poem. Born Alfredo Quispez Asin in Lima in 1903, he signs a modernist drawing in 1921 as "Cesar Moro" - a name taken from a character in Ramon Gomez de la Serna's Reverte I - and from 1923 the birth name never returns for poetry or life. Jason Wilson reads the pseudonym as an identity pun: Caesar and Moor, not Peruvian-Spanish but Roman-Moorish, with oro (gold) hidden inside. The mechanism: choose the name as the first artwork, then let the life be edited to fit it. It precedes the Paris move by four years; the reinvention is Lima-made, not Paris-borrowed. Against the chain: Rahon (#331 M6) renamed herself repeatedly, a new self per life chapter; Moro renames once, permanently, and then defends the single invented name for 33 years - the rename is a constitution, not a costume. Felguerez (#330) built a machine that generated ideograms; Moro's first ideogram was his own name. **Takeaway:** Name yourself as the first work, then live inside the name until the birth record looks like the draft. Watch the signature: when the 1921 drawing already says Moro and the 1955 poem still does, the mechanism is on.

## M2. The foreign tongue as optic

The central technical decision. In 1928 Moro joins the Paris surrealists and, per Coyne's testimony, immediately stops writing in Spanish and adopts French as the lingua prima of his poetry - though no French poem of his is known before 1930 and his real French production starts 1932-33. He lived about 8 years in a francophone country against 45 in Spanish-speaking ones, yet wrote over 300 compositions in French and wrote even to Spanish-speaking friends, and dedicated books, in French. His own line from Le chateau de grisou names the optic: "...lengua extranjera / Esta lengua colgante que habla por encima de mi cabeza" - the foreign tongue, this hanging language that speaks above my head. The mechanism is distance as instrument: French puts the images one remove from the mother tongue, and the remove is where the surrealism happens. Ferrari documents the cost and the texture: a French "cada vez mas personal" as Paris receded, studded with hispanisms (inaverti for inapercu, abonnent for repandent du fumier), phonetic puns that only work if the reader mis-hears French in Spanish (Vous etes dans le 3 read as dans l'etroit), and gallicisms leaking the other way into his Spanish (voltigea, resbalando perlas). Coyne watched him slide new verses under his door in Lima not for opinion but to have the linguistic faults flagged - the poet used a native informant as a calibration tool. Against the chain: Peret (#332) ran the marvelous engine in his birth language; Moro runs it in a language he had to master painfully, and the friction is part of the fuel. Rahon (#331 M1) scraped through matter to find the image; Moro scrapes through a second language. **Takeaway:** Write in the language you had to earn, and let its faults become texture. Watch the optic: when the hanging tongue speaks above the head and the reader must lean to hear it, the mechanism is on.

## M3. The love-letter as book form

The master form. La tortuga ecuestre (1938-39), his only book in Spanish and the work Ricardo Silva-Santisteban judges his masterpiece, is not a collection but one sustained address: 13 poems plus the Cartas-poemas to Antonio Acosta, the young Mexican soldier who was its inspirer, with seven actual love letters appended in some editions. Coyne's observation, quoted by Ferrari: the book itself constitutes a single love letter. The mechanism repeats in French: Lettre d'amour (written 1942, Dyn press 1944, 50 copies, cover by Alice Paalen) is one long poem-letter, and Moro's 1945 letter to Coyne gives the performance rule - the poem must be read in one single breath, to feel its excessive intimacy. The closing tercet of Carta de amor states the form's law: "en la prision en que tu ausencia me deja / en la soledad en que este poema me abandona / en el destierro en que me encuentra cada hora" - the poem as prison, solitude, and hourly exile, all three at once. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M2) sublimated love into physics with no liquid phase; Moro keeps the liquid - the body, the armpits, the blood drunk slowly - and the sublimation happens inside the address itself, lover to absent lover. Felguerez (#330 M8) titled geometric paintings to trigger readings; Moro titles the book as a salutation and the reading is the love affair. **Takeaway:** Make the book one letter, addressed, and never break the address. Watch the salutation: when every poem is a "tu" and the breath runs out before the sentence does, the mechanism is on.

## M4. The impossible animal

The signature image-system. The book's title, La tortuga ecuestre - the equestrian turtle - is an animal that cannot exist ridden by a rider who cannot ride it, and the household truth behind it is domestic: the poems were inspired by Antonio the lover and by a real Peruvian tortoise named Cretina (cretin being Moro's favorite term of abuse). The mechanism is the menagerie of the impossible: "Tu ausencia en el caballo de los dias" (your absence on the horse of days), "Al salir de mi sueno que un animal antediluviano perdido / en la sombra de los dias / Como una bestia desdentada que persigue su presa", "gran tigre negro de semen inagotable de nubes inundando el mundo". The animals arrive already impossible and are then given erotic jobs to do. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M3) built a three-voice bestiary with Linnaean paperwork; Moro's beasts have no file numbers, only lovers' bodies - the turtle is slow, armored, and carries the rider nowhere, which is the whole point of desire as Moro writes it. Carrillo (#327) painted the same object until it confessed; Moro's turtle confesses nothing and keeps walking. **Takeaway:** Put the beloved on an animal that cannot carry him. Watch the mount: when the turtle keeps its own pace through the turbulence of desire, time, and language, the mechanism is on.

## M5. Translation as lifeline

The transmission mechanism, and the doorway's engine. In Mexico, Moro translated Peret's Je sublime poems into Spanish in consultation with Peret himself - Pellegrini's Advertencia is explicit - and those versions ("Yo sublime", "Yo no como de ese pan") are the ones Pellegrini chose to print in 1961 rather than make his own. The practice was larger: in 1943 Moro published a surrealist-poetry anthology in translation in the Mexican magazine Poesia (Neftali Beltran's), with versions of Peret, Carrington, Arp, Eluard, Picasso, Dali, Reverdy, Breton, and Artaud; it was republished as a homage in Estaciones in 1956 and as a book in Spain in 1974. Julio Ortega's line, quoted by Wilson, names the function: translating was compensation for the exile Moro lived in "this mortal prison of Peru," a way to remain in mental touch with his friends. And the approval ran both ways: Westphalen's Spanish Lettre d'amour was examined and approved by Moro, which is why Pellegrini printed it unaltered. Against the chain: Felguerez (#330 M4) quit the computer because its designs came out identical; Moro's translations are deliberate re-makings where the author's consultation is part of the medium. Rahon (#331) scratched matter; Moro ferries poems across languages the way she ferried images across matter. **Takeaway:** Translate your friends in consultation with them, and let the translation be the friendship continuing by other means. Watch the ferry: when the Spanish version carries the author's approval and the exile's address at once, the mechanism is on.

## M6. The pun engine

The word-level machine, running in both languages. The 1934 Violette Noziere homage poem turns on two puns: Violette/violet/violer (the flower, the color, to violate - the whole trial in three syllables) and Noziere/biere/riziere (the name rhymed into coffin and rice-paddy). Pierre mere puns pere/pierre/mere/mer (father/stone/mother/sea) into a single title. The 1938 homage to Breton, "Como un piano de cola," runs a chain: piano de cola (grand piano) to cola de caballo (horse's tail) to cauda de estrellas (train of stars) - cola as hinge, the tail that leaps to a stellar link. In French he plants phonetic traps: "Vous etes dans le 3 / Un 20 grandissant sans cesse" asks the French reader to hear l'etroit and vent where the letters say le 3 and 20. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M4) rewrote proverbs by replacing one gear; Moro rewrites the dictionary by sounding one word until it becomes another - the same combinatorics, in phonetics. Rahon (#331 M4) inscribed dot-cluster asemic text; Moro's asemic layer is homophony. **Takeaway:** Sound a word until it becomes its neighbor, then build the poem on the hinge. Watch the hinge: when cola becomes tail becomes star-train and the poem follows the sound, the mechanism is on.

## M7. The breathless enumeration

The formal surface. Moro's poems are automatic texts without punctuation and without breathing spaces - Wilson on Renommee de l'amour (48 lines, formless, May 1933, LSASDR n.5). The mature form is the catalogue: "A vista perdida" runs a litany of "El estupor..." - the stupor of crystal beads, of glass vapor, submarine and sleek, leaning left flaming right - each entry a complete image, never connected, never closed. Lettre d'amour's performance rule (read in one single breath) makes the form bodily: the enumeration is scored for lung capacity, not for the eye. Ferrari's late-phase testimony completes the arc: in Amour a mort (1949-50) the words are freed even from liberating images - "le basta jugar," play is enough - and the reader becomes a player collaborating with the word-rabble on the page. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M5) stacked automatic lines that must never explain each other; Moro stacks them until the breath gives out, which is the explanation. Felguerez (#330 M3) varied eight shapes inside a field; Moro varies one stupor inside a lungful. **Takeaway:** List without punctuation until the breath is the only structure. Watch the breath: when the reader must inhale mid-image and the poem does not wait, the mechanism is on.

## M8. The dated break

The rupture as procedure. In 1944 Moro breaks publicly with orthodox surrealism, "con lugar y fecha" - with place and date, as Ferrari stresses. Wilson reconstructs the two-level challenge Moro issued to Breton in a long book review: formally, Breton's prose was too precious, too obviously manipulating the reader; morally, 1945 demanded tougher analysis, and Breton was stuck in "the moral dogma of unique love with someone from the opposite sex," while Moro held that Freud had shown love obeyed a fatal law from childhood of which heterosexual love was just one version. The verdict: Breton "could no longer be a guide; he's a leader who has become lost." The break follows Paalen's 1942 "Farewell au Surrealisme" and leads Moro to the Mexican Contemporaneos circle. Against the chain: Rahon (#331) never broke with anyone, she outlived the movement into silence; Peret (#332) never broke with Breton at all ("the only artist who never quarreled with him," per the v2 notes). Moro is the chain's breaker - the one who kept the engine and returned the brand, dated and signed. **Takeaway:** Break in writing, on two levels, with place and date. Watch the filing: when the homage poem to Breton (1938) and the obituary of his leadership (1944) are both on record, the mechanism is on.

## M9. The exhibition as weapon

The curatorial mechanism. In 1935 Moro and Westphalen mount the first surrealist exposition in South America at Lima's Academy Alcedo: 52 paintings, 38 of them Moro's, with a catalogue prologue brandishing a Picabia epigraph about art as a pharmaceutical product for idiots. Wilson: Moro's aims were to discredit painting in America and provoke the public into scorn and anger - "if you don't like it, go and drink water in the horse trough outside." In 1940, with Paalen and Breton's distant guidance, he co-organizes the International Exposition of Surrealism at Mexico City's Galeria de Arte Mexicano: 15 countries represented while the war raged, catalogue cover a Manuel Alvarez Bravo photograph, Moro's own manifesto inside - surrealism as "a castle with a thousand secret doors not yet tried out," art that "placed the spectator brusquely in front of himself." The manifesto's closing allusion, "el cisne de Montevideo," Latin-Americanizes Lautreamont (born in Montevideo) inside the movement's own catalogue. Against the chain: Felguerez (#330) made machines that generated art; Moro made exhibitions that generated scandal - the show is the work. Rahon (#331 M11) built a miniature theatre; Moro built the theatre the whole continent walked into. **Takeaway:** Curate the provocation, not the collection. Watch the door: when the catalogue insults the visitor and the manifesto ends on a swan from Montevideo, the mechanism is on.

## M10. The invective as craft

The prose weapon. Moro's criticism is a literary form in its own right. The Huidobro affair: Moro accuses Huidobro's "La jirafa" of plagiarizing Bunuel's "Girafe" (LSASDR 1933); Huidobro answers in Vital (June 1935) with homophobic and racist abuse ("Morito de calcomania," "homosexual louse," "a prostitute who chased like a dog after the surrealists"); Moro retaliates in February 1936 with "Vicente Huidobro o el obispo embotellado" - the bottled bishop. In Los anteojos de azufre (written 1934, updated 1938, posthumous 1958) he declares the only real poetry in Lima is written by the insane in the Hospital Larco Herrera, calls the local literati "literatoides" writing for Sunday supplements, and closes on the city's poets: "Batrachians, you cannot be further off from poetry! Where you end, poetry begins!" His 1939 El uso de la palabra attack on indigenista painting proposes the program: "Art begins where tranquility ends" - for an art that stops you dreaming (quita-sueno), against an art that lulls you. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M7) named enemies in pamphlets; Moro names them in exhibition catalogues and magazine broadsides, and his favorite insult (cretin) gets a pet tortoise named after it - the invective is domesticated into the household. Gerzso (#329) distilled toward one wall; Moro distills toward one insult, perfectly aimed. **Takeaway:** Insult precisely, in print, and keep a pet named after your favorite word. Watch the aim: when the tortoise is called Cretina and the bishop is bottled, the mechanism is on.

## M11. The aphorism ledger

The compressed philosophy. Moro's late notebooks and letters mint maxims that outlive the poems they annotate: "Uno da todo para no tener nada. Siempre para comenzar de nuevo. Es el costo de la vida maravillosa" - one gives everything to have nothing, always to begin again; that is the cost of the marvelous life (last poem, 8 August 1955, in Silva-Santisteban's translation). And: "no es tanto la libertad lo que necesitamos, sino ser encadenados por lo que amamos" - it is not so much freedom that we need, but to be chained by what one loves. He adopted Reverdy's: truth doesn't emerge from a well, but pulls whoever seeks it into the depths of himself. The mechanism: the aphorism is the poem's balance sheet - the whole erotic-metaphysical system compressed to one line, portable across languages, quotable at dinner. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M8) deflated the sublime on purpose with black humor; Moro's aphorisms inflate the abject on purpose - chains, wells, costs - until the ledger reads as triumph. Rahon (#331 M5) called painting magic and a key to the invisible; Moro's maxims are the key cut small enough to carry. **Takeaway:** Keep a ledger of one-line verdicts beside the poems. Watch the balance: when the cost of the marvelous life is stated as a price and paid gladly, the mechanism is on.

## M12. The posthumous machine

The publication engine that only starts at death. In his lifetime Moro published almost nothing: three books in tiny runs - Le chateau de grisou (200 copies, 1943), Lettre d'amour (50 copies, 1944), Trafalgar Square (200 copies, 1954) - plus poems in magazines he considered honest. La tortuga ecuestre, written 1938-39, was announced in 1942 with a planned Manuel Alvarez Bravo cover and died for lack of subscribers. After 10 January 1956, Andre Coyne - friend, last lover, executor - sells a series of pastels Moro painted in his final year and funds the editions: Amour a mort (Paris, 1957), La tortuga ecuestre y otros poemas (Lima, 1957), Los anteojos de azufre (1958), Obra poetica (1980), Ces poemes/Estos poemas (1987), the Getty papers acquisition, the slow canonization to the 2025 Cardboard House bilingual edition. Vargas Llosa, his former pupil at the Leoncio Prado military academy, becomes the posthumous character witness: Moro is Fontana in La ciudad y los perros (1962); Vargas Llosa translated him in 1958 and called him a pure, authentic poet because he never commercialized his art. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M12) lived the line breaks; Moro's line breaks were lived by his executor - the biography's final stanza is someone else's devotion. Felguerez (#330) built La maquina estetica; Moro built the slower machine: die, and let the faithful friend print you. **Takeaway:** Publish almost nothing, write everything, and choose the executor as carefully as the poems. Watch the machine: when the pastels pay for the poems and the pupil writes the defense, the mechanism is on.

## Aesthetic logic

Moro's engine is the exile's double-entry bookkeeping: every move is made twice, once in each language, once in each country, and the two entries never reconcile - that is the poetry. The self is renamed once and defended forever (M1); the tongue is foreign and therefore precise, its faults kept as texture (M2); the book is a letter that never arrives, so it keeps being written (M3); the animal is impossible, so desire never has to dismount (M4); the friend's poem is translated in consultation, so exile becomes correspondence (M5); the word is sounded until it turns into its neighbor (M6); the list runs until the breath fails (M7); the master is honored, then broken with, on the record (M8); the exhibition is staged as an insult with a catalogue (M9); the insult is crafted until it can be kept as a pet (M10); the system is compressed into maxims (M11); and the publication is delegated to love, after death (M12). Peret supplied the marvelous as doctrine; Moro is what the doctrine looks like when it is lived at triple distance - from the language, from the country, from the permitted love - and written anyway, in the hanging tongue, above his own head.

## Evolution

1903-1925 formation (Lima; Jesuit Colegio de la Inmaculada French; first poems 1924; first "Cesar Moro" signature on a modernist drawing, 1921) -> 1925-1933 Paris (arrives 30 August 1925 for ballet; painting first - Brussels 1926, Paris 1927; cubist detour against primitivist expectations; surrealism 1928; Spanish poems in Amauta 1928; the lost notebooks entrusted to Eluard and Breton 1932; Renommee de l'amour, LSASDR n.5, May 1933; Violette Noziere homage 1934; poverty, cheap hotels, cold - "hell" - and the ethical home) -> 1933-1938 Lima (1935 first South American surrealist exposition with Westphalen, 52 works; Los anteojos de azufre 1934; El uso de la palabra announced; Hospital Larco Herrera teaching; Cadre's 5 clandestine numbers 1936-37; police confiscation; flight 1938) -> 1938-1948 Mexico (cultural ambassador; Breton's "mon ami Moro" 1938; La tortuga ecuestre and the Cartas 1938-39; 1940 International Exposition of Surrealism; Le chateau de grisou 1943; the Peret translations in consultation; Lettre d'amour 1942/44; the 1944 break; Contemporaneos friendships - Villaurrutia, Lazo; Dyn poems in all 6 numbers; the Alvarez Bravo review) -> 1948-1956 Lima (Alianza Francesa and Colegio Militar Leoncio Prado teaching; Trafalgar Square 1954; Amour a mort 1949-50; the late word-play phase; the adobe house in Barranco with Coyne; last poem 8 August 1955; death 10 January 1956) -> 1957- rediscovery (Coyne's editions 1957-58; Pellegrini's anthology 1961; Bedouin's 1964 surrealist anthology; Obra poetica 1980; Getty papers; Full Stop/Cardboard House bilingual edition 2025).

## Study-method note

This study was built by the runner's own deep read - 13 sources end to end, ~35 texts inspected - after the #332 leg taught the cost of sandbox-lost notes: no subagent research leg was dispatched, and the notes were written to disk by the runner before composition. Load-bearing facts were cross-checked across at least two sources wherever the record allowed; the birth-date, death-cause, and dating variants are carried as conflicts, not resolved by vote. Visual works are carried as an honest gap: no Moro painting or collage was viewed first-hand this run.
