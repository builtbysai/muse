# Garrett Mattingly: Study Notes

**Site:** Garrett Mattingly (1900-1962), American historian of early modern Europe -- born Sunday 6 May 1900 in Washington, D.C., son of Leonard Howard Mattingly, civil servant and industrialist, and Ida Roselle Garrett; family moved to Kalamazoo, Michigan, in 1913; Harvard College, A.B. summa cum laude 1923, M.A. 1926, Ph.D. 1935 under Roger Bigelow Merriman; instructor in English at Northwestern University, 1926-1928, where he met Bernard DeVoto; instructor in English and history at Long Island University, 1928-1942; lieutenant commander, U.S. Naval Reserve, 1942-1945, instructing intelligence officers in Washington, D.C.; professor of history and chairman of the Division of Social Philosophy at Cooper Union from July 1946; professor of European history at Columbia University from 1947 (the sources disagree, 1946/1947/1948, see C3), William R. Shepherd Professor of European History from 1959 (first incumbent, January 1960 per Encyclopedia.com); George Eastman Visiting Professor at Oxford, 1962-1963; married Gertrude L. McCollum on Friday 22 June 1928, no children; author of Catherine of Aragon (1941), Renaissance Diplomacy (1955), and The Armada (1959), which won a special Pulitzer citation in 1960; author of the AHR tribute "The Historian of the Spanish Empire" (October 1948), of "Machiavelli's Prince: Political Science or Political Satire?" (American Scholar, Autumn 1958), and of "No Peace Beyond What Line?" ## M1. The Curtain-Raiser Opening
Open the book not with background but with a single violent, decisive scene staged like the first act of a play, and make that scene the key to the whole argument. The Armada opens with "Curtain Raiser: Fotheringhay, February 18 1587" -- the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots. Mattingly's insight, as the thriftbooks review puts it, was that the execution freed Philip to launch the Armada; sending it while she was alive would have risked putting a pro-French queen on the English throne. Stephen Brumwell: "The opening chapter, describing the execution of Mary Stuart, Queen of Scots in 1587, is brilliantly written." EBSCO: Mattingly "noted, for example, the haunting half-smile of Mary, Queen of Scots, as she faced execution." What it IS: the argument stated as an event. The reader does not need to be told why the Armada happened; the opening scene shows the lock turning. **Takeaway:** start with the scene that makes the argument, not the background that explains it.

## M2. Simultaneity / History-in-the-Round
Cut the narrative into short chapters, one per regime or capital, and run them in parallel across the same months, so the reader watches the same crisis from Madrid, Paris, Rome, Brussels and London in turn. EBSCO: "A series of short chapters on each regime produced a kaleidoscopic 'history-in-the-round' effect... Mattingly borrowed this device, known as 'simultaneity,' from fellow historian Bernard DeVoto." The operationsports review: "He shapes the world around the major cities of the period -- Madrid, Paris, Rome, Brussels, London, and, although not a city, the English Channel -- shifting from one city to the next and deftly tying the multifarious Machiavellian plots together without knotting them up." What it IS: the whole board held at once instead of one fleet followed. **Takeaway:** cut between capitals on the same dates and the reader holds the crisis whole.

## M3. The Chapter-End Foreshadow (Cliffhanger)
End each chapter with a teasing glimpse of what comes next, so the reader must turn the page even though the ending is known. The technoworldinc critique: "He kept my interest by ending his chapters with a teasing foreshadowing of what would follow in the next chapter. For example, in chapter eleven, which detailed Sir Francis Drake's raid of the Spanish coast, Mattingly wrote: 'Drake had already so confused and disrupted Spanish plans that few supplies moved for a month after he was gone, and no Spanish Armada could sail that year for England, whether the English were still off Cape St. Vincent or not' (128)." The operationsports review notes the book employs "cliff-hangers, flashbacks, etc." What it IS: suspense used honestly -- the foreshadow is a fact from the sources, placed where it pulls the reader forward. **Takeaway:** let the next chapter's first fact arrive at the end of this one.

## M4. Theatrical Chapter Architecture
Title every chapter as a scene with a place and a date, so the book is a sequence of staged episodes rather than a chronology. The Armada contents (Google Books, read in search results): "Curtain Raiser", "Perplexity of a Queen", "The End of a Gay Season", "Gods Obvious Design", "Barrel Staves and Treasure", "An Arm Is Cut Off", "Plans of Operations", "The Day of the Barricades", "Entrance to the Arena", "First Blood", "Not a Whit Dismayed" -- each with a place and date-range subtitle. A MyUS reviewer: "The chapter titles are romantically inspired." Renaissance Diplomacy does the same in miniature: "The Game at Chess", "Men Sent to Lie Abroad", "The Perfect Ambassador". What it IS: the table of contents as a playbill. The reader knows, before reading a word, that this book is a drama with entrances and exits. **Takeaway:** name the chapters like scenes and the book stages itself.

## M5. Portraiture in a Few Lines
Fix each person with one unforgettable thumbnail, painted in a sentence or two, rather than a full character study. Encyclopedia.com on Catherine: "Mattingly was able to paint, in a few lines, unforgettable portraits of remote figures" -- Pope Clement VII as a "sensitive, delicate, nervous man, with a talent for log-rolling and devious, personal intrigue, and the soul, if not of an artist, at least of a good, second-rate art critic; he was temperamentally unfitted for the dangerous decisions called for by these iron times." EBSCO: Philip II "a sad, conscientious, and 'terribly industrious' monarch"; Medina Sidonia "an honorable man who was the unhappy victim of circumstance". EBSCO on Mary: "the haunting half-smile of Mary, Queen of Scots, as she faced execution." What it IS: character as a single vivid sentence that the reader carries for the rest of the book. **Takeaway:** one sharp portrait sentence per person, no more, and let it do the work of ten pages.

## M6. Sympathetic Even-Handedness (No Cartoon Villains)
Give the enemy's case from the inside; refuse to make the Spanish into villains. Encyclopedia.com: "Unlike most other historians writing in English, Mattingly did not make the Spanish into cartoon villains." EBSCO: "Mattingly strove in The Defeat of the Spanish Armada for an objective, even-handed approach, rather than the undisguised bias so often evident in previous accounts. He was consistent in following the evidence where it led." What it IS: the drama survives the fairness, because the Spanish are given real motives, real competence and real bad luck, and the English victory has to be earned against them rather than assumed. **Takeaway:** let the other side be competent and sympathetic, and the victory means more.

## M7. Concealed Erudition (No Footnotes)
Do the archival work in six languages, then hide the scaffolding: no footnotes in the text, the apparatus pushed to the back as a general note on sources. Encyclopedia.com on Catherine: "written novel-fashion without footnotes and in a seamless, compelling, and often witty narrative"; Baumer (via Wikipedia): Catherine was "extremely careful and accurate and enormously erudite" but with traces of the care, accuracy and erudition "carefully concealed or utterly obliterated." EBSCO on The Armada: "Because Mattingly wrote for nonspecialist readers, he dispensed with the usual scholarly footnotes. For specialists, he did list the basic archival and printed documentary sources on which his narrative was based." The contents list "A General Note on Sources" (p. 405) and "Notes" (p. 410). Brinton, reviewing Catherine: the apparatus criticus "is never obtruded, and need never disturb that mythical creature, the general reader", yet "he makes no statements not based on thorough, painstaking research." What it IS: scholarship that refuses to show its work on the page, so the story reads like a novel and checks like a monograph. **Takeaway:** do the research in the archives and hide it in the back; the surface stays clean.

## M8. The Fresh-Eye Re-Reading
Take a story that has been told a hundred times and re-read the whole primary-source base with a fresh eye, putting the campaign back into the European context it had lost. Mattingly's own preface, p. v, quoted in the technoworldinc critique: "it seemed there might be some interest in replacing the narrative of their naval campaign in the broader European context in which it had once been viewed but from which, in the peaceful years before 1914, it had become more and more detached." Encyclopedia.com: "His purpose was to look at the vast array of primary sources with a 'fresh eye' and to place the 'narrative of the naval campaign in the broader European context.'" What it IS: the familiar story made new not by new facts but by a wider frame -- the same campaign, read again with the whole continent in view. **Takeaway:** re-read the known sources in a wider frame instead of hunting for a new fact.

## M9. Ideology, Not Economics, as the Motor
Reject the economic explanation of the war (command of the seas, routes to Asia) and make the motor the clash of two systems of ideas -- a holy war, each side bent on unifying Europe religiously. The technoworldinc critique: "To historians such as A.T. Mahan, the conflict 'seemed to be the command of the ocean seas and the opportunity to exploit the newly discovered routes to Asia and the Americas'... Mattingly's predominant theme was to show how the European countries were still so deeply divided due to conflicting ideologies and how each side strove to unify Europe religiously." The combatants believed they fought "a final struggle to the death between the forces of light and forces of darkness" (Mattingly, preface p. v, quoted there). Encyclopedia.com: The Armada's subject is "the 'first international crisis of modern history'." What it IS: the motive as belief, not interest -- the fleets sail because each side thinks God is on its side, and that is what makes the story tragic rather than commercial. **Takeaway:** make the clash of beliefs the engine, and the economics the weather.

## M10. The Multi-Archive Source Base
Build the narrative from the state archives of every party -- England, Spain, France, the Netherlands, Italy and the Vatican -- so each side's version can be checked against the others. The technoworldinc critique: "Mattingly very wisely studied the documents held in the state archives of all the primary countries involved in this conflict, i.e. England, Spain, France, The Netherlands, Italy, as well as the Catholic archives in the Vatican... each side would present their own versions of events to put themselves in the best possible light." Wikipedia: "In order to read the primary sources, Mattingly taught himself several foreign languages as well as sixteenth-century script." Encyclopedia.com: documents "in several 16th-century languages and local variations thereof ranging from Latin to Spanish to French to German to Flemish to English." What it IS: the simultaneity of M2 made possible by the archives -- you can only cut between Madrid and London if you have read both sides' papers. **Takeaway:** gather every side's archive first; the cross-cutting is earned by the research.

## M11. The Myth After the Battle Matters More Than the Battle
Treat the outcome as less significant than its effect on prestige and on the myth that grew from it. Encyclopedia.com: "Despite his attention to the details concerning the Armada's defeat, Mattingly emphasized that the actual outcome of the battle was far less significant than the impact it had on Spanish and English prestige throughout Europe and on the growth of the myth of the Armada defeat, a 'victory of freedom over tyranny, of David over Goliath.'" EBSCO: "the history of Europe and realms far beyond, like America, would likely have been very different had the Armada vanquished the English fleet in 1588." What it IS: the book's real subject is the story the battle became -- the myth did more work in history than the cannon did. **Takeaway:** follow the story the event became, not just the event.

## M12. The Counterfactual Hinge ("Things Might Have Happened Differently")
Keep the reader aware that the outcome was not fated: at every turn, admit the road not taken, and let the contingency do the dramatic work. AHR tribute, p. 41: "Merriman is always admitting, even insisting, that things might have happened differently, and then -- why, the issue would have been different." Mattingly applies the same hinge in The Armada: the thriftbooks review notes the execution of Mary "freed Philip to launch the Armada" -- had she lived, the invasion could not have been launched on those terms; and "Scotland, ruled by Mary's son James VI, was the dog that did not bark in the night in 1587-88." What it IS: contingency as suspense -- the reader feels the weight of the hinge because the other road is kept visible. **Takeaway:** show the road not taken at every turn, and the road taken feels chosen, not fated.

## M13. Literary Craft Under the Constraint of Truth
Write polished, dramatic prose -- "purple prose but a royal purple" -- while insisting the history must be true; hate pedantry and theoretical fashion equally. Encyclopedia.com: "'History,' he once remarked, 'is the most difficult of the belles lettres, for it must be true.' His work further shows his concern for literary craftsmanship -- for carefully constructed and polished prose -- and a keen interest in individual personality and situational nuance." Hexter (via Encyclopedia.com): Mattingly's popularity rests on "his literary talents and... his hatred of pedantry and of both 'new wave' and 'old wave' theoretical type history." Wikipedia: he "once joked that he was an old-fashioned literary historian like 'Will Durant, Irving Fisher and William Hickling Prescott.'" What it IS: the credo that holds the whole practice together -- the prose must be crafted AND the facts must hold, and neither excuses the other. **Takeaway:** polish the prose, but never let the polish carry a fact the sources do not support.

## M14. The Plot as Tragedy
Let the narrative take the shape of a plot -- a rise, a pinnacle, a fall -- driven by passions, cross-purposes, mistakes and accidents rather than by inexorable fate or a single law. AHR tribute, pp. 40-41 (read end to end): Spain "stumbled upward to that abrupt pinnacle of power whence no way led except towards catastrophe"; "the plot even takes on something of the impact of great tragedy, though tragedy to be sure Shakespearean rather than Sophoclean, with a plot in which human passions and foibles, cross-purposes, mistakes, and accidents play a part more obviously decisive than any inexorable fate." The EBSCO starter confirms the tribute "describes the main principles and methods that shaped his own approach" -- so this is Mattingly's credo stated through Merriman. In The Armada the same shape recurs: the publisher's blurb: "Mattingly builds up, like the movements of a symphony, a broad picture... The feeling of tension mounts to a crescendo throughout Europe as the great drama of the Armada is approached." What it IS: history shaped as a plot without being falsified -- the rise and fall are in the sources, and the tragedy is the shape they take. **Takeaway:** find the plot in the sources and let it carry the book, but let the sources set the shape.

## M15. The Present-Tense Parallel as Genesis
Let a present crisis supply the book's animating image: the idea for The Armada came in June 1940, watching England stand alone in the Battle of Britain, and the book is built on the David-and-Goliath parallel. The technoworldinc critique: "Garrett Mattingly stated he first thought of writing an account of the defeat of the Spanish Armada in June 1940." EBSCO: "Mattingly dated the genesis of his study of the Spanish Armada to the inspiring example of the English people during the Battle of Britain, early in World War II... The image of an English David confronting the Spanish Goliath came readily to mind among a deeply apprehensive English people." What it IS: the present as the lens, not the subject -- the 1940 parallel tells Mattingly what the 1588 story is about (a small island standing alone against a great power), and the book is written to that image. **Takeaway:** let a living present image choose the story's shape, then prove it from the sixteenth century alone.

## M16. The Misquotation Correction
Begin an inquiry from a famous phrase, show it is misquoted, and let the correction reframe the whole subject. The Cambridge Core extract of "No Peace Beyond What Line?" (TRHS 1962): "'In peace or war in Europe', says Williamson, 'there was no peace beyond the line.' The phrase is often quoted by people who do not explain what line they mean... 'Line' is in fact a misquotation which should be 'lines'. The 'lines of amity' were verbally agreed upon by the French and Spanish negotiators of the Peace of Cateau-Cambresis in 1559. They were to be the Tropic of Cancer and the prime meridian passing through Ferro in the Canaries." What it IS: Mattingly's method in miniature -- a phrase everyone quotes, traced back to the treaty, corrected by the documents, and the correction (two lines, not one) changes what the phrase means. **Takeaway:** start from the famous phrase, check it against the document, and let the correction open the subject.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife so far

Phase one, the apprentice, 1900-1935. Training, not yet a subject: Washington, Kalamazoo public high school (graduated 1918), a year as an Army sergeant in the 43rd Infantry, then Harvard -- A.B. summa cum laude 1923, with study at Strasbourg, Paris and Florence on a Sheldon Travelling Fellowship (1922) and an undergraduate article on Shakespeare (title not found in any leg); two years in a New York publishing house; M.A. 1926; instructor in English at Northwestern 1926-1928, where he met Bernard DeVoto, the closest friend of his life and his narrative coach; instructor in English and history at Long Island University 1928-1942, where his first professional articles on sixteenth-century diplomacy appeared (titles not found); Ph.D. Harvard 1935 under Merriman, on a little-known Spanish diplomat in sixteenth-century England (exact dissertation title not found). The dissertation's plan -- a history of Anglo-Spanish diplomatic relations -- is the seed of everything that follows; the Renaissance Diplomacy foreword says the studies "were begun at Harvard University under the guidance of Roger Bigelow Merriman" more than thirty years before 1954.

Phase two, the archive years and the first book, 1935-1955. The long road to print: a Guggenheim fellowship in 1937-1938 spent in the European archives -- London, Brussels, Florence, Vienna, Paris, Simancas -- the Simancas work #474's C5 correctly assigns to Mattingly, not Merriman; the Further Supplement to the Letters, Dispatches, and State Papers of Henry VIII (1940); Catherine of Aragon (1941), an immediate critical and popular success and a 1942 Literary Guild selection, written novel-fashion without footnotes; war service 1942-1945 as a lieutenant commander instructing intelligence officers, the naval knowledge later fed directly into The Armada; Cooper Union from July 1946, where he "perfected his dramatic style of lecturing"; Columbia from 1947 (see C3); a Fulbright in Italy, 1953-1954; Renaissance Diplomacy (1955), the book that made his scholarly reputation -- cut by a third at his publisher's recommendation to avoid a university press, the original draft destroyed, and Hexter's verdict that the published book "is not as good as Mattingly could have made it... Even so, it remains one of the finest historical works of the past half century."

Phase three, the Armada and the coda, 1955-1962. The popular triumph and the early end: "Machiavelli's Prince: Political Science or Political Satire?" (American Scholar, Autumn 1958), his most famous single argument and his most contested; The Armada (1959), 443 pp., a bestseller and a Book-of-the-Month Club and History Book Club selection, winner of the special Pulitzer citation of 1960; William R. Shepherd Professor of European History from 1959; honorary D.Litt. from Wesleyan, 1961; George Eastman Visiting Professor at Oxford for 1962-1963, where he was to lecture on the Italian Renaissance -- a reinterpretation meant to "redo Burckhardt" that was never begun; "No Peace Beyond What Line?" read to the Royal Historical Society in November 1962 and published in the December 1963 volume, his last paper, set in type after his death on Tuesday 18 December 1962.

Afterlife so far. The "Invincible" Armada and Elizabethan England (Folger Booklets, 1963, posthumous) and Renaissance Profiles (1978, posthumous collection); the Festschrift From the Renaissance to the Counter-Reformation (1965), with Gershoy's "Personal Appreciation"; Hexter's "Garrett Mattingly, Historian" (Doing History, 1971), the standing critical assessment; Kingdon's American Scholar appraisal (1982); the Mattingly Award for diplomatic history, named for him. His simultaneity device has an acknowledged influence on Simon Schama. His resident-embassy model -- the resident ambassador as an Italian invention of the 1450s -- is now challenged by Daniela Frigo, Catherine Fletcher and Isabella Lazzarini, a live historiographical dispute, not a settled refutation. The Armada stays in print (Mariner Books reprint 2005) and is still, in the EBSCO starter's words, "quickly recognized as a classic of narrative history".

## Aesthetic logic

The book and the life share one aesthetic: the story is the job, and the truth is the constraint on the story, not its enemy. Mattingly's sentence holds the whole practice together: "History is the most difficult of the belles lettres, for it must be true." Everything else follows from it. The curtain-raiser opening, the simultaneity, the cliffhangers, the theatrical chapters, the portraits in a few lines -- all are literary devices, and all are justified only because the facts support them: the vivid details "came invariably from original sources, especially from diaries, memoirs, and state papers" (EBSCO). The concealed erudition is the same logic from the other side: the research is real (six languages, six archives) precisely so that it can be hidden, and the reader can have the novel without the footnotes. The even-handedness is the same logic applied to persons: Philip II is given his sadness and his industry because the sources give them to him, and the myth after the battle is studied because the myth is itself a historical fact. The pupil inverts the master without breaking from him: Merriman underplayed his hand, held dramatic distortion a cardinal sin, and stayed the historians' historian; Mattingly took Merriman's creed -- "a knowledge of the narrative is the indispensable foundation for everything else" -- and played every scene he got, because DeVoto had told him "When you get a scene, play it." The result is the paradox Hexter named: books painstakingly researched and widely popular, a paradox explained by literary talent plus a hatred of pedantry and of theoretical type-history, "new wave" and "old wave" alike. The aesthetic is a refusal: no cant, no bilge, no theory in place of the story, no footnote in place of the scene -- "honest historical workmanship with a minimum of bilge and cant."

## Load-bearing corrections

C0. The Pulitzer was a special citation, not the History prize. The Armada won a Pulitzer SPECIAL citation in 1960, citation "A first class history and a literary work of high order" (pulitzer.org, read in all three legs); the 1960 Pulitzer Prize for History went to Margaret Leech, In the Days of McKinley. Do not write "won the Pulitzer Prize for History" or "won the Pulitzer" without the "special citation" qualifier.
C1. "Epitaph" is figurative. "The Historian of the Spanish Empire" (AHR 54(1), October 1948, pp. 32-48) is a critical reassessment and statement of method published three years after Merriman's death, not an obituary or a tomb inscription. The doorway's word is accurate in spirit -- Mattingly calls Merriman his master in craft -- but it is not the piece's genre.
C2. "Outshone" is scoped to narrative reach. Mattingly outshone Merriman in public readership and narrative success (bestseller, Book-of-the-Month Club, special Pulitzer citation); he never claimed to surpass him as a scholar, and by his own account Merriman's Rise ranked with Prescott and Lea. Do not present the outshining as a scholarly verdict.
C3. Columbia appointment year unresolved. 1947 (Wikipedia, LibraryThing, Everand, NNDB) vs 1946 (Encyclopedia.com: "In 1946 he also became professor of history at Columbia") vs 1948 (Prabook Who's Who, SNAC, Penguin blurb, Google Books 2005 author bio).
