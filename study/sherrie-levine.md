# Sherrie Levine: Study Notes

**Site:** Sherrie Levine -- born 17 April 1947 in Hazleton, Pennsylvania; lives and works in New York and, since 1997, divides her time between New York City and Santa Fe, New Mexico -- American photographer, painter and conceptual artist of the Pictures Generation, known for rephotographing canonical photographs and remaking canonical artworks as her own, so that the copy, signed by her, becomes the work. ## The life: reproductions, then the copy as the work

Levine was born in 1947 in Hazleton, Pennsylvania, and spent most of her childhood and adolescence in the suburbs of St. Louis, Missouri, where her mother -- who loved to paint -- took her to the St. Louis Art Museum from the age of eight and to art house films; much of her art knowledge came from reproductions in books and magazines, a biographical fact that already contains her method: she learned art from copies before she ever made one. After high school in 1965 she spent eight years in Wisconsin, taking a B.A. (or B.F.A., gallery CVs disagree) from the University of Wisconsin, Madison, in 1969 and an M.F.A. there in 1973. In college she made Minimalist grid drawings that closely resembled Brice Marden's work; confronted with the similarity and the feeling of "reinventing the wheel", she turned to photography to break the impasse. She moved to Berkeley in 1973, taught art in the Bay Area, and moved to New York City in 1975. (C19708, leg 1 C19500-C19501, C19504-C19505; synthesis C19650)

Her first solo show was at 3 Mercer Street, New York, in 1977: "Shoe Sale", found children's dress shoes displayed on a checkered quilt and sold "2 shoes for $2" -- dead stock turned into fetish objects by a price tag and a signature. The count is unsettled (75 shoes per TheArtStory vs 35 pairs per a search-summary gallery source) and is not printed as settled; the Paula Cooper CV summary gives the show the alternate title "Two Shoes for Two Dollars", also not verified end to end. The same year Douglas Crimp included her in "Pictures" at Artists Space, the founding show of the Pictures Generation, selecting her "Sons and Lovers" (1976-77) presidential silhouettes. The breakthrough came in 1981 at Metro Pictures: "After Walker Evans", 22 photographs rephotographed from an Evans exhibition catalogue and presented as her own. The show was a scandal and a success; the Estate of Walker Evans treated it as copyright infringement and bought the works to stop their sale, and the series is now at the Metropolitan Museum of Art -- the exact mechanism (Levine donating vs the estate gifting in 1994) is unresolved, and the show year itself is 1981 in the majority and museum sources against 1982 (TheArtStory) and 1980 (artnet). (C19709, leg 1 C19500-C19501, C19504, C19511; leg 3 C19606, C19611; synthesis C19651)

From 1983 on she widened the appropriation from photography into drawing, painting and sculpture: watercolours after Mondrian, Matisse and Leger; drawings after de Kooning, Schiele and Malevich; the Knot paintings (from 1985/1987) and the Check paintings (1987); "The Bachelors (After Marcel Duchamp)" (1989, six frosted-glass sculptures after the malic molds -- Wikipedia only, not verified against an in-range source); "Fountain (After Marcel Duchamp)" in polished bronze (1991); the cast-glass "Newborn" copies after Brancusi (1993-1994); the gold skulls (2002 onward); the pixelated "After Cezanne" and "After Stieglitz" (2007); the "Gray and Blue Monochromes" after Stieglitz's Equivalents (2010); monochromes paired with refrigerators (2016). She divides her time between New York and Santa Fe (documented from 1997; the current Zwirner page names New York only). Major surveys: "Sherrie Levine: Mayhem" at the Whitney, 10 November 2011-29 January 2012, and "Sherrie Levine: 1977-1988" at the Aspen Art Museum, 6 June-28/29 September 2025 (closing date conflict, one day). She has been represented by David Zwirner since 2015 (first solo there 2016); Paula Cooper was her dealer for seventeen years before that (search-summary figure, not confirmed end to end). Her work is held in 36 listed public collections including the Met, MoMA, Whitney, Tate, Centre Pompidou and Philadelphia Museum of Art. No Dia holding exists -- do not claim one. (C19710, leg 1 C19500-C19503, C19506, C19508, C19511; synthesis C19652)


## The works: one operation, widening targets

The catalogue runs W01-W20, 1977 to 2026. Twelve grades A exist; the grades below are B where a good secondary description was read end to end or the description was thin, and split A/B where the two halves of one entry differ. The evolution is the argument: the operation never changes -- take a canonical thing and present it again as hers -- but its target widens from a photograph in a catalogue to a painting, a sculpture, a skull, a pixel grid, and finally back into painting itself. (C19711, leg 2 C19550-C19579; synthesis C19673)

**W01, "Shoe Sale", 1977** -- B. Dead-stock girls' dress shoes in a Soho storefront at 3 Mercer Street, displayed on a checkered quilt and sold "2 shoes for $2". The shoe motif returns in her 2025-2026 bronze boots and clogs. Mechanism: found readymade stock, displayed and sold as art, with almost no making involved; the earliest work in the Aspen 1977-1988 show establishes her relationship to the readymade, a Duchamp device in which prefabricated items are redefined as artworks through a contextual shift. (C19712, leg 2 C19565, C19572)

**W02, "President Collage: 1" (1979) and "Untitled (President: 4)" (1979)** -- A. Cut-and-pasted printed paper on paper; President Collage: 1 is 24 x 18 in. (61 x 45.7 cm). Levine ripped images of women from magazines and assembled them into silhouette portraits of three American presidents -- Washington, Lincoln, Kennedy; in President Collage: 1 a cut-out image of a fashion model forms the profile of Washington seen on the quarter. Mechanism: cut-out silhouette (outline of a famous man's head) filled with a photographic advertising image of a woman; two modes of figuration -- the photographic trace and the resemblance (outline) -- are forced into one image. The Hammer calls the 1978 Presidential Profile version a literal face-off in which gendered modes of representation stand in stark opposition while making a single strange image. (C19713, leg 2 C19553, C19574-C19576)

**W03, "After Andreas Feininger: 1-12" (1979)** -- A. Collage on paper; each sheet 61 x 45.7 cm, framed 72.4 x 57.2 cm. Levine's first works made entirely from images appropriated from other photographers, preceding by a year her rephotographed works after Eliot Porter and Edward Weston. Images were taken from several books by the American photographer Andreas Feininger, including his volume Trees; some were cut directly from the pages, others were made from tipped-in photographic plates that Levine removed and remounted. Mechanism: cut, remove, remount onto standard-sized sheets -- appropriation by literal excision from a book, not by rephotographing. (C19714, leg 2 C19572)

**W04, "Untitled (After Edward Weston, ca. 1925)" (1979)** -- B. Rephotograph; the source is Weston's 1925-ish portrait of his son Neil. Levine rephotographed Weston's photograph, presenting the copy as her own work; she does not crop the image, so it is very hard to tell her photograph from the original unless you are a connoisseur of prints. Mechanism: photograph a photograph, with no cropping and no visible manipulation; the work is the act of re-taking, not a new image. Weston's original itself double-makes the boy (he created his son, then used him as raw material for a photograph); Levine then copies that copy as a woman artist. (C19715, leg 2 C19552-C19553)

**W05, "After Walker Evans: 1-22" (1981), esp. "After Walker Evans: 4"** -- A. Gelatin silver print; After Walker Evans: 4 is 12.8 x 9.8 cm at the Met (the Whitney brochure lists 10 x 8 in. -- different print/presentation sizes circulate; do not merge the two figures). In 1981 Levine photographed reproductions of Depression-era photographs by Walker Evans, such as the portrait of Allie Mae Burroughs, wife of an Alabama sharecropper, from a reproduction in an exhibition catalogue, presenting the image as her own. Mechanism: rephotograph a catalogue reproduction (a copy of a copy), with no manipulation of the image. The series became a landmark of postmodernism, praised and attacked as a feminist hijacking of patriarchal authority, a critique of the commodification of art, and an elegy on the death of modernism. (C19716, leg 2 C19550-C19551, C19559, C19574)

**W06, "Untitled (After Malevich and Schiele)", from the 1917 exhibition, Nature Morte Gallery, New York (1984)** -- B. Pencil and watercolor on paper, 14 x 11 in. Levine remade Egon Schiele's nude self-portraits by replacing oil painting with watercolours; the series pairs Kazimir Malevich's abstraction with Schiele's figuration in a single sheet. Mechanism: medium swap plus pairing -- Schiele's expressive figure is redrawn in the thinner, cheaper medium of watercolor/pencil, and set against Malevich's geometry; she chose artists with radically different styles and rendered them in a uniform format, reducing the images to equivalent signs. (C19717, leg 2 C19553-C19554, C19563)

**W07, "Broad Stripe: 6" (1985)** -- B. Casein paint and wax on mahogany, 24 x 20 in. (61 x 50.8 cm). One of the Broad Stripes series (1985), large stripes of colour painted on a wood panel; the red and brown tones of the wood affected the colour of the paint. Mechanism: generic modernist stripe painting, but the ground is not neutral canvas -- the mahogany shows through and tints the casein, so the wood's colour and grain do part of the work. (C19718, leg 2 C19559-C19560)

**W08, "Large Gold Knot: 1" (1987), Knot Paintings (1985-1988)** -- A. Metallic paint on plywood; Large Gold Knot: 1 is 60 x 48 in. (152.4 x 121.9 cm). On untreated expanses of plywood, Levine paints only the randomly distributed knot patches, allowing them to dictate the final form of the artwork; in the earliest version she highlighted the knots by covering them in gold leaf. The knot paintings are also a pun on "not painting". Mechanism: chance as composition -- the lumber company's eye-shaped plugs used to repair knots and irregularities in the grain decide where the paint goes; Levine only picks out the knots, so the pattern is found, not invented. Curator Richard Flood calls them the Levine lexicon: a piece of plywood, and she fills in the knots; the pragmatism contains the poetry, and different plywoods give different rhythms in the wood. White Moonlight After Man Ray (begun 1985, revisited 2016) is a knot-painting variant in which the painted knots stand for clouds in a moonlit sky, after a Man Ray collage in which wood grain suggested clouds. (C19719, leg 2 C19565-C19566, C19568-C19569)

**W09, "Large Check: 1" (1987), and "Check #5"** -- A. Casein and wax on mahogany; Large Check: 1 is 24 x 20 in. (61 x 50.8 cm). Twelve grids that echo the geometric forms of Piet Mondrian but render them in pastel colours -- normally seen as feminine or decorative -- and drastically simplify the composition. Check #5 could be mistaken for a brightly-coloured hand-painted checkerboard in the 19th-century folk-art tradition, except it has eighty squares rather than the sixty-four of most game boards. Mechanism: take the modernist grid / chessboard, paint it in casein and wax on mahogany, and deliberately leave the preparatory pencil sketches visible, so the hand is on show -- something absent in the earlier appropriations. The stark patterns resemble a chessboard, suggesting art is a game whose rules Levine does not play. Check #5 does not directly copy or quote another artist; it is a conceptual appropriation of geometric abstraction, 1960s-1970s Minimalism, and game-playing. (C19720, leg 2 C19566-C19567)

**W10, "Meltdown" (1989)** -- A. Twelve-colour woodblock prints (woodcuts), in a portfolio of four prints. The woodcuts are derived from canonical paintings by four early modernists -- Marcel Duchamp, Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Piet Mondrian, and Claude Monet. To make the prints, Levine photographed a reproduction of each work, scanned the photos, and reduced each digital file to twelve pixels using a computer program. Mechanism: photograph a reproduction, scan it, crush it to 12 pixels by computer averaging, then rebuild those 12 colours as woodblock prints on Japanese rice paper. The simplified images eliminate all detail, brushwork, and distinguishing formal traits among the masterworks, turning them into abstract variations. The monochrome paintings of the 2020s are a development of Meltdown: instead of twelve coloured rectangles per work there is now just one single-colour average. (C19721, leg 2 C19570-C19571)

**W11, "La Fortune (After Man Ray)" (1990)** -- A. Mahogany, felt, and billiard balls; overall 33 x 110 x 60 in. (83.8 x 279.4 x 152.4 cm) per table; edition of 6. Levine transforms Man Ray's 1938 Surrealist painting La Fortune -- a billiard table under a mountain range and brightly coloured clouds -- into six billiard tables that give physical form to the one depicted in the painting. The pocketless tables are identical to Man Ray's, even in the unusual hourglass-shaped legs and the placement of the balls. Mechanism: extract one object from a 2-D painting and rebuild it life-size in 3-D, keeping the painted details (leg shape, ball positions) but fixing the surreal proportions to traditional ones. The balls are secured in place and the cue sticks are absent; there is no mechanism for the game to actually be played. First shown in 1991 at SFMOMA as six identical tables in precise serial alignment, in the manner of Donald Judd. (C19722, leg 2 C19577-C19578)

**W12, "Fountain (After Marcel Duchamp)" (1991)** -- B. Cast bronze; SFMOMA example 14 x 14 1/2 x 25 in. (35.6 x 36.8 x 63.5 cm), edition of 6 plus 1 artist's proof; shown at Mary Boone Gallery, New York, May-June 1991. Levine remakes Duchamp's 1917 readymade urinal as a polished cast-bronze sculpture. Mechanism: material inversion -- take the cheapest anti-art object (a porcelain urinal) and recast it in the most traditional precious sculptural material (bronze), turning Duchamp's gesture back into an "art object" by elevating its materiality and finish. The Broad makes the same point for the Buddha variant: Levine turns Duchamp's gesture back into an art object. Guard: dimensions differ across examples because some include the wood base and some do not; do not quote one figure as the work's single size. (C19723, leg 2 C19555-C19557)

**W13, "Fountain (Buddha)" (1996), with "Fountain (Madonna)" (1991)** -- A. Cast bronze; Fountain (Buddha) is 12 x 15 7/8 x 18 in. (30.48 x 40.32 x 45.72 cm). A second-generation Fountain -- the same bronze urinal, now given a second title (Madonna, then Buddha) that renames the object as a sacred figure. Mechanism: re-title plus re-finish. The urinal shape is unchanged; the new title (Madonna / Buddha) and the polished bronze turn a plumbing fixture into a devotional object, and the joke compounds: Duchamp's original Fountain is lost and lives on only in replicas he made during his life. (C19724, leg 2 C19557-C19558, C19560)

**W14, "Crystal Newborn" (1993) and "Black Newborn" (1994) -- "Newborn" (After Brancusi)** -- A. Cast and sandblasted glass (Black Newborn); cast crystal (Crystal Newborn); both 5 x 8 x 5 1/2 in. (12.7 x 20.3 x 14 cm). Levine remakes Constantin Brancusi's 1915 sculpture The Newborn -- an abstract ovoid evoking a child's head -- first in crystal (1993) and then in black glass (1994). She installs them on top of grand pianos, a display she began after seeing a photograph of a Brancusi exhibited that way in the home of a British collector. At Portikus (1994) she installed four identical sculptures: four black concert grand pianos, a white transparent egg-form on each. Mechanism: cast the ovoid in glass instead of carving it in marble, then multiply it and perch it on a readymade luxury object (the grand piano). The casting process resembles photography: once the mold is made, any number of identical copies can be made, and there is no original because the mold is a sort of negative. Levine made the sculptures with the cooperation of the Philadelphia Museum of Art, which owns a version of Brancusi's Newborn. Guard: the MOCA count (nine black-glass Newborns on pianos, 1995) is search-summary only and is not claimed here. (C19725, leg 2 C19561-C19562, C19564)

**W15, "Red and Gray Check: 7-12" (2000)** -- B. Oil on aluminum; each panel 18 x 10 in. (45.7 x 25.4 cm). A series of six check paintings that recall Minimalist Carl Andre, the grid of early modern painting (Malevich, Mondrian), and a chessboard -- an oblique reference to Marcel Duchamp. Mechanism: same check/grid system as the 1987 Large Checks, but now in oil on aluminum in a smaller, serial format of six panels -- the grid is repeated across panels rather than varied within one painting. (C19726, leg 2 C19559)

**W16, "Steer Skull, Horned" (2002); "False God" (2007); "Caribou Skull" (2006) -- gold skulls** -- A (Steer Skull) / B (False God, Caribou Skull). Cast bronze, polished to resemble gold; False God 25 1/2 x 26 x 12 in. (64.8 x 66 x 30.5 cm), edition of 12; Caribou Skull 63 x 35 x 23 1/2 in. (160 x 88.9 x 59.8 cm). Levine casts real animal skulls in bronze and polishes them to a gold shine, mounting Steer Skull like a hunting trophy on the wall. The skulls cite Georgia O'Keeffe's paintings of cow skulls from the New Mexico desert. False God is the skeleton of a two-headed calf, cast in bronze polished to resemble gold -- an explicit reference to the biblical golden calf, complicated by stripping the flesh (death instead of innocence) and by the deformity (two skulls from a single neck). Mechanism: take a real skull (found object / natural remain), cast it, gild it by polishing the bronze, and present it as a luxury object -- the first time Levine referenced a female artist (O'Keeffe) instead of a male one. The gold surface conjures excess, wealth, and opulence, and tangentially the golden calf as an idol to be worshipped. Guard: the Whitney works page dates False God 2008; Sotheby's and Christie's date it 2007 -- the 2007 date is used here, the 2008 listing flagged. (C19727, leg 2 C19574, C19579)

**W17, "After Edward Curtis: 1-5" (2005)** -- A. Five black-and-white photographs. Levine rephotographs works by the 19th-century ethnographic photographer Edward Sheriff Curtis (1868-1952), who documented Native American lives; Curtis took more than forty thousand photographs and visited eighty tribes. Mechanism: rephotograph a documentary archive whose maker claimed to record a vanishing population; by placing the portraits before us again, Levine asks how we know what we know, what the agendas and biases of documentarians may be, and whether photographs can tell the complete true story. Same rephotography operation as Evans/Weston, applied to an ethnographic rather than an art-photographic source. (C19728, leg 2 C19574)

**W18, "After Cezanne" (2007) and "After Stieglitz" (2007) -- pixelated photographs** -- A. Digitized and pixilated photographic prints, 18 prints in each series. After Stieglitz reworks Alfred Stieglitz's Equivalents series (1920s-1930s cloud photographs) by digitizing and pixilating them, abstracting them further. After Cezanne is a suite of 18 pixilated photographic prints that transforms Cezanne's painted, fractured 19th-century vision of trees and rocks into a colourful digitized matrix abstraction. Mechanism: take an analogue photograph (or a photograph of a painting), digitize it, and pixilate it until the image becomes a visible grid of colour blocks -- a picture on top of a picture, in Levine's words, where both sometimes disappear and sometimes are both visible, and "the vibration in the middle is the work". AWARE reads After Cezanne the same way: the pixelated photographs represent our fragmented vision of the world at the start of the 21st century, against Cezanne's vision at the end of the 19th. (C19729, leg 2 C19553, C19573)

**W19, "Crystal Skull: 1-12" (2010)** -- B. Cast crystal (Whitney caption) / cast glass (Whitney exhibition text) -- the two wordings describe the same series; each skull 5 1/2 x 7 x 4 1/2 in. (14 x 17.8 x 11.4 cm). Twelve identical cast-crystal human skulls, each encased in a separate vitrine, presented together in two rows on tall vitrines. Mechanism: cast a small model of a human skull twelve times identically, then isolate each copy in its own vitrine -- repetition plus display furniture does the work, not the form itself. The series recalls 17th-century vanitas paintings and Damien Hirst's contemporary platinum and diamond skulls. Guard: the Crystal Skull dating is 2010 in Wikipedia and the Whitney, but a Whitney image caption dates a "Crystal Skull" work to 2011 -- likely a 2010 series with a 2011 cast or photograph date; unresolved. (C19730, leg 2 C19554, C19560)

**W20, "After Piet Mondrian" (2023-ongoing), incl. "After Piet Mondrian 1: Yellow" (2026)** -- A. Oil on mahogany panel; After Piet Mondrian 1: Yellow is 50.8 x 40.6 cm. The paintings are based on a 1983 series of chromogenic photographs (c-prints) that Levine made from book illustrations of Mondrian's grid compositions. She is now painting the c-prints, rematerialising them and bringing the research full circle, with the aim of completing a coloured, an inverse, and a black-and-white painting of each photograph. Mechanism: invert the original appropriation -- move from photography back into painting. The paintings introduce chromatic distortions that mirror those of the older technique, in which colours shifted as the image was reproduced in a book and then rephotographed by Levine. The black-and-white paintings mimic the effects of black-and-white photography in the medium of painting; in the newest iteration the black-and-white paintings are translated back into colour, tinted in a single hue (Yellow). All of Mondrian's works are reformatted to fit identically sized mahogany panels. (C19731, leg 2 C19571-C19572)

The evolution falls into three moves. First, the operation stays fixed while its target widens: from a photograph in a catalogue in 1981, through a painting's billiard table rebuilt life-size in 1990 and a urinal recast in bronze in 1991, to a real animal skull gilded in 2002 and a masterwork crushed to twelve pixels in 1989 -- the move is always to take a canonical thing and present it again as hers. Second, the artist's hand retreats and returns: the early rephotographs and the Meltdown crush remove the hand entirely, the knot paintings let the wood decide, and then the check paintings deliberately leave the preparatory pencil sketches visible, so the hand is on show -- something absent in the earlier appropriations. Third, the late career turns the machine backwards onto itself: the 2023-2026 Mondrian paintings paint the photographs she once took of the paintings, colour becomes black-and-white becomes a single hue, and the copy of a copy becomes a painting again -- forty years later, the appropriation is appropriated. (C19732, leg 2 C19550-C19579; synthesis C19655-C19662)


## Mechanisms: eight ways to make the copy the work

**M1 -- Rephotography: photograph the photograph, change nothing.** Take a canonical photograph from a catalogue reproduction, rephotograph it with no cropping and no visible manipulation, and present the copy as the work. Watchable behavior: two prints side by side -- Evans's Allie Mae Burroughs, Weston's son Neil, Curtis's Native portraits -- that a connoisseur can barely tell apart; the work is the act of re-taking, not a new image. The copy is a copy of a copy (Evans via catalogue), and the scandal is the signature, not the picture. (W04, W05, W17) **Takeaway:** re-take the famous photograph and sign the copy. (C19733, synthesis C19655)

**M2 -- The copy as original: the almost-same.** Remake a canonical work so faithfully that the only difference is authorship -- then let that difference do all the work. Watchable behavior: an Evans print that is Evans's image and Levine's work at once; a Brancusi ovoid cast in glass instead of carved in marble; the viewer hunts for the difference and finds only the label. This is the mechanism behind her phrase "ghosts of ghosts": each copy is a ghost of the original, and her copy is a ghost of that ghost. (W05, W14) **Takeaway:** make it almost the same and let the signature be the difference. (C19734, synthesis C19656)

**M3 -- Medium swap: same form, wrong material.** Rebuild the source in a different medium so the form stays and the substance changes the meaning. Watchable behavior: Schiele's expressive nude redrawn in thin watercolour and pencil; Man Ray's painted billiard table rebuilt life-size in mahogany and felt, its balls bolted in place and its cues missing so the game can never be played; Duchamp's porcelain urinal recast in polished bronze; Brancusi's marble egg cast in sandblasted glass. The swap is the argument: watercolour cheapens Schiele, bronze ennobles the urinal, glass multiplies the egg. (W06, W11, W12, W14) **Takeaway:** keep the shape, change the stuff, and let the material argue. (C19735, synthesis C19657)

**M4 -- Let the ground decide: chance and the crush.** Hand the composition to something that is not the artist -- the wood, the computer, the game board. Watchable behavior: gold paint picks out only the knots the lumber company plugged into the plywood, so the pattern is found, not invented (and the series puns on "not painting"); mahogany tints the casein stripes from underneath; a masterwork is photographed, scanned and crushed by computer to twelve averaged pixels, then rebuilt as twelve-colour woodblock prints in which Duchamp, Kirchner, Mondrian and Monet become indistinguishable colour samples; a check painting turns out to have eighty squares, not a chessboard's sixty-four. (W07, W08, W09, W10) **Takeaway:** let the wood, the pixel count, or the board decide the picture. (C19736, synthesis C19658)

**M5 -- Multiply and display: the edition as the work.** Make the same thing many times identically and let repetition plus display furniture do the work. Watchable behavior: six identical billiard tables in precise serial alignment like Judd; twelve identical cast-crystal skulls, each alone in its own vitrine, in two rows; four (or nine) identical glass Newborns, each perched on a black grand piano; a two-headed calf cast in gold-polished bronze in an edition of twelve. The form is trivial; the multiplication is the meaning -- the mold is a negative, and once it exists there is no original. (W11, W13, W14, W16, W19) **Takeaway:** cast it twelve times and let the vitrines do the talking. (C19737, synthesis C19659)

**M6 -- Cut and fill: the silhouette as a trap.** Cut a famous outline and fill it with the wrong picture. Watchable behavior: the profile of Washington from the quarter, Lincoln, Kennedy -- each silhouette stuffed with cut-out magazine photographs of women, mothers and models, so the photographic trace and the outline fight inside one image; or images cut directly out of Feininger's books, tipped-in plates removed and remounted on standard sheets. Appropriation here is literal excision, not rephotography: the scissors do what the camera does in M1. (W02, W03) **Takeaway:** cut the president's head out of a magazine and fill it with the magazine. (C19738, synthesis C19660)

**M7 -- Gold as value-coating: polish the worthless into the precious.** Take a worthless or deathly thing and give it the surface of wealth. Watchable behavior: a urinal in polished bronze that reflects like Brancusi; plywood knots picked out in gold leaf; real animal skulls -- a steer skull hung like a hunting trophy, a two-headed calf -- cast in bronze and polished to a gold shine, the golden calf as false god, death instead of innocence. The gold surface conjures excess and opulence and at the same time accuses it: the idol is worshipped and exposed in one object. (W08, W12, W13, W16) **Takeaway:** gild the skull and let the shine accuse the worshipper. (C19739, synthesis C19661)

**M8 -- Return and invert: forty years later, run the machine backwards.** Go back to an old appropriation and reverse its direction. Watchable behavior: the 1983 Mondrian c-prints -- photographs of book illustrations of Mondrian grids -- are now painted in oil on mahogany, each source getting a colour version, a black-and-white version and an inverted version, with chromatic distortions that mirror the colour shifts of the old book reproduction; the 2007 pixelated Cezanne and Stieglitz prints push the same logic into the digital age, the image dissolved into a visible grid of colour blocks where both pictures sometimes disappear and sometimes are both visible, and "the vibration in the middle is the work". (W10, W18, W20) **Takeaway:** paint the photograph you once took of the painting, and invert it. (C19740, synthesis C19662)

Held out of the eight, both real: **M9, the readymade shop** -- found stock displayed and sold as art (Shoe Sale, 1977: dead-stock children's shoes sold "2 shoes for $2"), the earliest work and the purest Duchamp device, held out because the description is thin (grade B) and the mechanism is mostly covered by M1/M5; **M10, the grid emptied** -- take Mondrian's grid, paint it in pastel "feminine" colours on mahogany, leave the pencil sketches visible, and declare art a game whose rules she does not play (Large Check, 1987; Red and Gray Check, 2000), held out because it is a variant of M4's ground-decides logic rather than a distinct system. (C19741, synthesis C19663)


## The network: a hub with three spokes, plus a thin doorway tie

The peer spoke is the Pictures Generation: Crimp's "Pictures" (Artists Space, 24 September-29 October 1977, five artists: Brauntuch, Goldstein, Levine, Longo, Philip Smith) and the Met's "The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984" (21 April-2 August 2009, thirty artists) put Levine on one roster with Sherman, Prince, Kruger and Lawler. The only direct working partnership in the group is with Louise Lawler: "A Picture Is No Substitute for Anything" (1981-82), loft shows and performances run by the two artists with no gallery permission -- they exhibited their own photographs in Lawler's loft and a friend's empty loft, staged performances, and ran the venture themselves. A second, thinner collaboration: "What do we own? What is the Name?" with Barbara Kruger (1980, search summary only). No direct Levine-Sherman or Levine-Prince contact beyond shared shows was found. (C19742, leg 3 C19607-C19610, C19620; synthesis C19664)

The lineage spoke runs backward to Duchamp above all -- Fountain re-authored in bronze, The Bachelors after the Large Glass, Meltdown reducing Duchamp to twelve pixels -- then to Evans and Weston in photography, Rodchenko (After Rodchenko 1-12, 1987, MoMA), Brancusi (Newborn in glass, 1993), Man Ray (La Fortune, 1990), Mondrian (watercolours; Meltdown; the 2023-2026 paintings), and Curtis (2005). The dealer spoke is Paula Cooper (solos 2001-2014, long-term), with Metro Pictures at the start (1981 first solo), Mary Boone (1987-2015), Jablonka (from 1993), Simon Lee (from 2007), David Zwirner (from 2015/2016), Xavier Hufkens (2018). The Lavier tie sits outside all three spokes: a single 1990 Brussels pairing, the only evidenced Lavier-Levine contact. A 2023 group show puts Levine with three of the four named peers again: the Bourse de Commerce / Pinault Collection show of photographs 1970-1990 by Michel Journiac, Louise Lawler, Sherrie Levine, Richard Prince, Cindy Sherman and Martha Wilson -- Journiac is study #685's subject, so this show also ties Levine back to the current study chain. (C19743, leg 3 C19608, C19611-C19619, C19621, C19626; synthesis C19665)

The Pictures Generation label itself was coined by critic Douglas Crimp in 1977 for the "Pictures" show, then fixed as a movement name by the Met's 2009 survey; the Broad notes "Levine's work was central to Crimp's claims". Lavier's painted readymades and Levine's rephotographs are two answers to the same Duchampian question -- what makes a thing a work when the artist did not make it -- and the Brussels pairing of 1990 put the two answers in one room. (C19744, leg 3 C19626-C19627)


## Aesthetic logic: the almost-same as a machine

Levine's aesthetic is an aesthetic of the almost-same, and the almost-same is a machine with three settings. The first setting is the copy: rephotograph the photograph, recast the urinal, redraw the nude in watercolour, and the work is the signature on a thing someone else made -- authorship without making, originality without an original. The second setting is the material: bronze ennobles the urinal, glass multiplies the egg, gold leaf picks out the knot, mahogany tints the stripe -- and in each case the substance does the arguing the image refuses to do, cheapening what was grand and ennobling what was worthless. The third setting is repetition: six billiard tables, twelve skulls in vitrines, four glass eggs on four pianos, a masterwork crushed to twelve pixels -- and repetition is what kills the original, because once the mold exists there is no original left to copy, only copies of copies, ghosts of ghosts.

That is why nothing in Levine is ever finished in the usual sense. The series are open procedures: the rephotograph can take any canonical image, the medium swap can take any canonical form, the crush can take any masterwork, and the machine runs it through the same operation. The work is not the object; the work is the operation, and the operation is portable. A generative piece in her manner should therefore never render a copy and stop; it should run the operation -- re-take the famous image and sign it, swap the material and let it argue, let the wood or the pixel count decide, multiply the thing until the original disappears -- and let the operation take whatever the viewer brings to it. **Takeaway:** run the almost-same as a machine, and let the signature be the difference. (C19745, synthesis C19655-C19662)
