## The life: the architect who let living things finish the work

Luis Fernando Benedit was born in Buenos Aires in 1937. He knew painters Antonio Berni and Juan Carlos Castagnino at his aunt Cecilia Benedit's house as a child, drew for his Champagnat school magazine, and spent holidays in el campo -- riding, polo, the Pampas horizon, peons, horses, dogs -- the memory that returned as subject matter from 1978. He entered the UBA Faculty of Architecture in 1956 and graduated in 1963 with an award-winning project, a self-taught painter alongside. He specialised in popular architecture in Madrid 1963-65, where his painting turned Pop, flat tones and synthetic forms. In 1967 a landscape-architecture fellowship took him to Rome, where exposure to Jannis Kounellis was, in his own account, "a true epiphany", and his interests extended to biology and live organisms.

The career falls into three phases, and the break between the second and the third is the study's hinge. (1) Early painting, 1959-1968: Informalism and Art Brut, then CoBrA, then Pop; first show Nuevos rostros, Galeria Lirolay, 1961, with Marta Minujin, Dalila Puzzovio and Roger Alhua. The labyrinth motif first appears as a painted organ or egg form before it becomes a built space. (2) Living systems, 1968-1975: the first artificial habitat in 1968 (Tuttovetro y los pescados), Microzoo at Galeria Rubbers the same year -- anthills, lizards, fish, turtles, plants at different stages of germination, live bees, all at once, and the bees escaped down Florida street, by his own account in the 2009 interview. Biotron at the 35th Venice Biennale, 1970 (C9834 corrects the 1971 label found in two sources). Fitotron and the white-mice labyrinth at MoMA's Projects Gallery, 14 November 1972 - 2 January 1973: the first Latin American artist in that gallery, in the most specific sourced wording (C9850). By the early 1970s he felt "a suspicious being to artists and much more suspicious to scientists" and largely stepped back from live habitats after 1972, with two later returns, one of them Whitechapel 1975; the other is not identified in the sources read. (3) Analytical dismantling and Argentine history, 1976 onward: the embalmed ovenbird (W18, 1976), bird-as-robotic-mechanism drawings (W19, 1977), Campo (1978), the Palliere recreations (1984-85), the Beagle charcoals (1986-87), the Rancho houses (1991), the bone works and horse culture that fill Equinus Equestris at MALBA in 2009 -- 60 works of recent horse-themed production, NOT a career retrospective, a correction to the #645 pick line, which had the count right and the characterisation wrong (C9833).

Alongside all of it ran a parallel architecture practice: the Centro Cultural Recoleta recycling competition win in 1979, with Clorindo Testa and Jacques Bedel; the Ruth Benzacar Gallery building, 1983; the Munar Foundation, 1990. He was a regular CAYC participant from its founding in 1968 and a founding member of the Grupo de Arte y Cibernetica Buenos Aires in 1969, then of the Grupo de los Trece in November 1971. Awards: Konex Diploma al Merito 1982, 2002, 2012 (post mortem); Konex de Platino 1992; the Itamaraty Grand Prize at the Sao Paulo Biennial 1977, with the Grupo de los Trece. Late shows: the MNBA retrospective 1996 (Obras 1960-1996, curated by Marcelo Pacheco); Genealogias del campo argentino at Fortabat, 2013/2014, posthumous, 77 works (year unresolved, C9846); Invisible Labyrinths at ISLAA New York, 7 September 2024 - 5 April 2025, 40+ works, the first large-scale New York show since MoMA 1972 (C9847 on the closing date). His son Tomas Benedit's childhood drawings became the "third degree" works of about 1977 to the early 1980s -- the child's drawing, its reworking as a design, its realisation as a volumetric object; Glusberg's phrase. Whether Tomas was five or seven when the series began is unresolved (C9845). A pending challenge he named in 2009 was to design a set for the Teatro Colon; whether he ever did is not recorded, and it is not claimed here.

## M1 -- The habitat as a working system: container + rules + living agent

Benedit builds a transparent container -- Plexiglas, aluminium, glass -- and sets the rules inside it: food, light, water, paths, shelter. Then he lets a living organism complete the work by behaving. The artist is the system designer, not the image-maker; the organism is the protagonist. Glusberg's phrase for the 1968-1972 works is "biological physical-chemical experiences"; Benedit's own word for the containers is "habitaculos", artificial habitats. W04 Habitat para hormigas (1968, Plexiglas, soil, ants, enamel and sugar, 65 by 46 by 16 cm); W05 Tuttovetro y los pescados (1968, water, objects and live fish, the first animal habitat work); W06 Habitat para tortugas acuaticas (1968); W07 Habitat para caracoles (1970, Plexiglass, snails, polyethylene, wood and vegetal matter, 80 by 40 by 40 cm); W08 Pecera para peces tropicales (1970, thermostat, aerator, 40-watt lamp, live fish; dated 1971 by one source, C9841); W09 Biotron (1970); W13 Fitotron (1972); W11 and W14, the white-mice labyrinths; W16 Evaporador de Sachs (1972). The Microzoo solo show at Galeria Rubbers in 1968 is the mechanism's first full statement. The watchable behaviour is unscripted: ants carrying loads through soil and sugar; snails moving across vegetal matter; tropical fish circling a thermostat-lit tank; bees choosing between artificial flowers and real ones outside; mice solving a maze that lengthens every 48 hours; plants growing toward a lamp. The work is never finished at the opening -- it keeps happening, and what happens is not scripted.

**Takeaway:** the habitat is not a diorama. A diorama shows a frozen scene; a habitat runs. The difference is the agent's freedom to behave, and the artist's willingness to let the behaviour be the work.

## M2 -- Computer-directed feeding: timed release, animal choice

A machine releases food on a schedule the animal cannot see, and the animal's preference between the machine's offering and the natural alternative becomes the visible result. The computer is not decoration; it is the feeding hand. In Biotron the 25 artificial flowers exuded drops of sugar solution at computer-directed intervals, and the bees were reported to prefer the artificial flowers to the real ones outside -- the system out-competed nature at its own game, on nature's terms. W09 Biotron (1970, Plexiglass, aluminium, PVC, wood, fifty 100-watt bulbs, electric generator, 25 automatic flowers, 4,000 live bees, 300 by 500 by 200 cm, Argentine Pavilion, 35th Venice Biennale) is the full statement; the physical work no longer exists and is known from photographs and drawings. Its dimensions carry one unresolved variant, 4 metres against 5 (C9843). W13 Fitotron (1972) is the plant-side version: plants "automatically supplied with light and a chemical growth formula" -- 70 tomato and 56 lettuce plants in the MoMA press release, pepper plants in Burlington and the MoMA checklist, cabbages and peppers in a 1972 photograph caption; the plant list conflicts and no single definitive list is stated here (C9838), but the mechanism, automatic supply on a schedule, does not conflict. The watchable behaviour is a slow visible vote, counted in bodies: rhythmic dripping at intervals, bee traffic thickening around the artificial flowers and thinning toward the garden opening; in Fitotron, plants growing without soil, fed by formula, under bulbs -- growth as a scheduled delivery.

**Takeaway:** the mechanism to steal is the preference test. Do not build a flower that drips; build a choice, and let the animal's choice be the image. A piece on M2 needs two food sources and a visible count of who eats where.

## M3 -- The labyrinth as a behaviour test: animal, plant, human

A maze is not a puzzle to look at; it is an apparatus that measures how a living thing solves, chooses, or fails. Benedit builds the same apparatus three times with three different agents. The path is the instrument; the solving is the content. W10 Labyrinth for Ants, drawing, 1970 (felt-tip pen, pencil, transfer type, crayon on board, 56.5 by 75.9 cm, MoMA); a distinct 1971 drawing of the same title is a separate work and must not be merged with it. W11 Laberinto para ratones blancos I (1971, Plexiglass, acetate, glass, seven white mice, 27 by 44 by 22 cm) and W14 Laberinto para ratones blancos II (1972, the MoMA version): mice given water, food only by solving the labyrinth, whose path lengthened and became more complicated every 48 hours. The I/II numbering must not be treated as chronological -- the working diagram for II is dated 1970 (C9840). W15 Laberinto vegetal (1972, black Plexi box, germinating seeds, 40-watt lamp; dated 1973 by one source, C9842): seeds forced to choose left or right paths to follow the light. W12 Laberinto invisible (1971, electronic alarm, 150-watt lamp, mirrors, stainless steel, Mexican axolotl, dimensions variable): the only labyrinth designed for a human to perform -- the visitor walks an invisible maze, interrupting a light beam sounds an alarm, and the reward at the end is an axolotl in a transparent box. The mirror count is unresolved: seven flat plus one concave, eight, or seven concave, depending on the source (C9839). Laberinto invisible was NOT shown at MoMA, whatever one ICAA synopsis says; MoMA's own post essay states there are no records confirming it, and the 1972 press release lists only two environments plus drawings (C9836). The 1972 Buchholz trio (cockroaches, ants, vegetal) is recorded, with only the cockroach prototype drawing confirmed. The watchable behaviour: a mouse hesitating at a fork, then committing; a seedling bending left at a junction, then left again; a visitor freezing mid-step as the alarm sounds, then finding the axolotl. The 48-hour lengthening is the mechanism's signature: the maze gets harder on a schedule, so the solving never settles.

**Takeaway:** the labyrinth migrates across agents -- ant, mouse, plant, human -- and the migration is the point. A piece on M3 should pick one agent and one rule (path lengthens; light forces a choice; alarm punishes a wrong step) and let the solving be watchable in real time.

## M4 -- Hydroponics: growth without soil, under artificial light

Plants grown in water and chemical formula, lit by bulbs, in a transparent chamber. No soil, no weather, no season: growth becomes a fully controlled variable, and the chamber becomes a greenhouse that is also a laboratory and also a sculpture. The work is a climate, not an object. W13 Fitotron (1972, hydroponic system, aluminium, polyethylene, light bulbs, plants, 300 by 200 by 500 cm; MoMA Projects, 14 November 1972 - 2 January 1973; now in the MALBA collection) is the five-metre climatic chamber. W16 Evaporador de Sachs (1972, prototipo multiple, Plexiglass, water, living plant, 32 by 15 by 11 cm) follows Gericke's 1930s hydroponics research. Germinaciones (1968) and Gota de agua (1971, or about 1970, C9848) sit at the same wet edge, unnumbered. W05 Tuttovetro y los pescados (1968) is the wet edge of the same idea from the animal side. Burlington groups these as the hydroponic environments, against the labyrinths. The watchable behaviour is slow and legible: roots visible in water instead of hidden in soil; leaves turning toward bulbs on a schedule; water evaporating and condensing in a closed loop, the Sachs evaporator; a plant growing where no plant should grow, because the formula says so. The transparency is functional: you watch the growth because the chamber is built to be watched through.

**Takeaway:** hydroponics is the habitat mechanism with the agent slowed to plant speed. A piece on M4 should be slow, legible, and transparent -- growth you can see happening, in a container you can see through, with the light and water doing visible work.

## M5 -- The visitor as test subject: the audience inside the apparatus

Benedit turns the apparatus on the viewer. In Laberinto invisible the human being is the mouse: the visitor walks the maze, the beams and alarm judge the walk, the axolotl is the reward. In Plant Experiment the public's attitude is the variable: be friendly to one plant, neutral to a second, hostile to a third -- and the loved plant dried out first, against expectation. The viewer is not looking at an experiment; the viewer IS the experiment. W12 Laberinto invisible (1971; reconstructed at ISLAA 2024-2025 and at the Faria Henrique Gallery, New York, 2-29 February 2011, the last exhibition date found in his lifetime). W17 Plant Experiment (Experimento con plantas, 1973, three living plants plus written instructions; Whitechapel, 29 May - 6 July 1975). The Microzoo bees escaping down Florida street in 1968 is the unruly version: the living agent refuses the container and the street becomes part of the work. The watchable behaviour is a visitor's body in the maze -- hesitation, wrong step, alarm, recovery; three plants under three attitudes, one of them dying from kindness; bees leaving the gallery for the street. The interaction here is earned: the system only works if a real person steps into it, and the person's behaviour is the content.

**Takeaway:** M5 is the only mechanism in this study where human interaction belongs in a piece prompt as the primary input -- because in the source work the human IS the agent. Every other mechanism's interaction should be watching, not clicking. A piece on M5 should make the viewer's choice visible (which path, which attitude) and show a consequence (alarm, reward, a plant's fate).

## M6 -- Despiece: the dead organism as analytical object

After 1972 Benedit stops keeping organisms alive and starts taking them apart -- "despiece", dismantling. The living agent is replaced by its remains, its drawing, or its museum display. The control stays; the behaviour goes. What was once a habitat becomes an autopsy, a diagram, a specimen. W18 Furnarius Rufus (vulgar "Hornero", 1976, wooden frame with an ovenbird's nest containing an embalmed bird) is the turn itself: the bird is dead and framed like evidence. W19 Proyecto Pirincho I (1977, pencil and watercolor) draws the bird twice, once in nineteenth-century naturalist technique and once as a machine -- technical drawing "reconstructs" the flying organism through complex robotic mechanics. Campo (1978, unnumbered) is the rural show as a museum of Pampean biopolitics: boleadoras, batons, stirrups, bridle and muzzle, castrating scissors, and a photograph of a dead pedigree bull next to artificial-insemination pills holding its frozen semen. Tijera de castrar (1978, unnumbered) stands alone or as a component; the sources do not settle which. The watchable behaviour is not movement but inspection: a dead bird held in a frame; a bird drawn twice, once as nature drew it and once as a machine; tools laid out like surgical instruments; a house labelled like a specimen. The watchable thing is the viewer's own close looking, and the dread that comes with it: the system is still running, but its agent is dead.

**Takeaway:** M6 is the negative image of M1. A piece on M6 should not animate the animal; it should animate the looking -- slow pans across a dissected thing, labels appearing, the mechanism drawn over the body. The contrast with M1 (alive) and M6 (dead) is the study's sharpest pairing for two prompts that talk to each other.

## M7 -- The museum fiction: simulated natural-history display

Benedit builds fake museum exhibits -- drawings and objects that simulate a natural-science museum's displays, using the museum's own language (labels, vitrines, scale representations, period engravings, photographs) to present a history that is partly invented. The display is the work; the authority of the museum is the material. W20 Del viaje del Beagle - Tahiti Tia Oru (1987, charcoal on canvas, one of eight works six feet square): drawings and objects simulating natural-science museum exhibits, built from Darwin's voyage of 1831-1838, texts, period engravings and photographs, as a reflection on Argentina's historic construction. The Palliere recreations (1984-85, unnumbered) re-create works by the nineteenth-century artist Leon Palliere -- Un nido en la pampa, Indios del Gran Chaco, El payador. The Rancho series (1991, unnumbered) is duplicate acrylic houses filled with horsehair, wool and sugar cubes, the rural Argentine house partly deconstructed and labelled like an anthropological specimen. Peron, Peron (unnumbered) is an enormous pencil drawing of Peron on horseback, the head replaced by a hole illuminated by light from an adjacent wooden sculpture. The watchable behaviour is a label that says too much; a specimen that is slightly wrong; a six-foot charcoal landscape that is really a museum wall; a house that is also a jar of horsehair. The viewer reads the display as true, then notices the fiction -- and the noticing is the work.

**Takeaway:** M7 is about the frame, not the thing. A piece on M7 should build the display first (vitrine, label, scale, lighting) and let the specimen inside it be the surprise. The Tomas-drawings "third degree" works belong adjacent to M7: the same move -- one thing re-made through three frames -- applied to a child's drawing instead of a museum's.

## M8 -- Bone and horse: national identity as material

From the 1990s Benedit works in polished cow and horse bone, because bones "guard memory, endure in time and are the last thing to disappear" (his words, 2009 interview). The material IS the argument: Argentine identity is not painted, it is assembled from what the campo leaves behind. The horse is the last large theme, and the bone is its medium. The H series (20+ abstract compositions of polished cow- and horse-bone fragments assembled on wooden boards, titled simply H plus a number) is the centrepiece of Equinus Equestris (MALBA, 15 May - 27 July 2009); individual H-series numbers and dates are not confirmed, and whether Fitotron was in that show is not asserted -- it is in MALBA's collection and was photographed there about 2008-09, but no source places it in Equinus Equestris itself. Bone chairs including "Silla armada de huesos" (the first bone chair was taken by Ruth Benzacar to ARCO in the 1990s); "San Hubertus"; three dogs in different materials; mirrors of various sizes in wooden frames engraved with horse anatomy, front and rear views; sixteen portraits in four groups of descendants of Pampa chiefs, in charcoal, sanguine and graphite on canvas; the closing triptych, the churches of Baradero and San Antonio de Areco flanking a large equestrian self-portrait; a 2006 watercolour, "Rauhwacke-oahu". The watchable behaviour is slow and material: bone catching light on a wooden board; a chair that is also a skeleton; a mirror that shows your face over an engraved horse's anatomy; sixteen faces of chiefs' descendants looking back. The movement here is the light across polished bone, and the viewer's reflection inside the horse's body.

**Takeaway:** M8 is the only mechanism in this study where the material carries the meaning alone, with no living agent and no apparatus. A piece on M8 should be still, lit, and close -- the opposite of the habitat prompts' busy life -- and let the bone do the talking. Do not animate the bone; animate the light on it.

## The aesthetic logic: the container, the rule, and the answer back

Benedit's aesthetic is the logic of the container and the rule. Everything he makes between 1968 and 1972 is a box with a law inside it: a habitat with food and light, a labyrinth with a lengthening path, a greenhouse with a formula. The beauty is not in the box -- Plexiglas and aluminium are deliberately plain -- it is in what the law does to the living thing inside the box, and in the fact that the artist does not control the outcome. The bees prefer the artificial flowers; the loved plant dies first; the visitor trips the alarm. The system answers back, and the answer is the work.

After 1976 the same logic runs with the agent removed: the box stays, the law stays, but the living thing is replaced by its remains, its drawing, or its museum label. The work becomes an autopsy of the earlier system. The bone works complete the arc: the container is gone, the law is gone, and only the material that outlasts both is left -- bone, which "guards memory".

His training explains the form and his biography explains the turn. The architect builds containers and sets rules; the self-taught painter fills them with animals; the child of the campo, returned to the campo in 1978, replaces the laboratory with the ranch, the tool, and the bone. The through line is control: first control of a living system, then control of its representation, then control of its remains. A study that misses the control misses Benedit; a piece prompt that adds decoration to the container instead of a law inside it is not a Benedit prompt. Against Gamarra, his predecessor in this chain, the contrast is exact: Gamarra's works endure, quietly, under pressure; Benedit's works answer back, and the answer is the event.
