## M1. Scoring wet paint (the wound as the mark)

Tiznit and Volubilus, both 1953, are made by scratching and scoring wet paint to create a scarred, bristling surface, the Museum of Modern Art, and the paint skin is wounded as it is made, gouges and scratches in the surface, and the mark is subtraction, not addition, and the light catches the incisions, and the surface reads as map, graffiti, or eroded architecture, and the place name does the figurative work, Tiznit for a town in Morocco he visited, Volubilus for the Roman site Volubilis, while the picture itself stays abstract. Evidence: Tiznit, summer 1953, white enamel house paint, pencil and crayon, the earliest Twombly in the Museum of Modern Art collection, shown with Rauschenberg's monochromes at the Stable Gallery joint show of September 1953, and Volubilus, its companion, and the scoring is the first Twombly mechanism and it never leaves, the incised line returns in the blackboard paintings and in the late work as the pencil under the wash. **Takeaway:** cut the field instead of drawing on it, so the line is a wound in the surface and the light does the drawing, and let a place name carry the figuration the picture refuses. Against the chain: Mitchell (#393) also built a surface, but hers is built up, layered white and loaded stroke, and his is cut open, and the difference is that her paint is added and his is removed, and de Kooning (#387) also scraped, but his scraping reveals a body underneath and Twombly's reveals only the ground.

## M2. White line on dark ground (the inversion)

The grey-ground paintings begun in August 1954, six or eight of them, put thin white lines on a dark canvas that appear to be scratched onto the surface, with bitumen applied in a quick and coarse fashion, and the line glows against the dark the way chalk glows on a board at night, and the eye reads the line first and the ground second, and the dark is the picture and the light is the mark, an inversion of the usual figure and ground, and the marks are scattered, almost legible, defying interpretation, the first appearance of the run-on handwriting that becomes his signature. Evidence: Panorama, begun 1954, dated 1955, oil-based house paint, wax crayon and chalk, 254 by 340.4 centimetres, private collection, the largest and only surviving work of the group, shown at the third Stable solo show in January 1957, see C8, and the group is the bridge between the scarred surfaces of 1953 and the Roman pale grounds of 1957, the dark ground held over from New York while the line learned to run. **Takeaway:** make the ground dark and the mark light, so the picture is read backwards, the light carrying the meaning and the dark carrying the weight, and let the marks scatter until they almost spell something. Against the chain: Kline (#388) is the nearest technical kin, black and white calligraphy, but his black is the mark and his white is the ground, and Twombly inverts it, white mark on dark ground, and the inversion is the difference between a stroke that asserts and a stroke that glows, and Mitchell (#393) also worked a white ground, but hers is layered and lit, and his is dark and encrusted, and the white behaves differently on each.

## M3. Blind drawing in the dark (habit switched off)

In the army in 1953 to 1954, a cryptographer at Camp Gordon in Georgia and the Pentagon, he modified the Surrealist technique of automatic drawing by creating compositions in the dark, after lights out, in a rented hotel room in Augusta, and the blind drawings resulted in elongated, distorted forms and curves, and the Foundation chronology says the point was to obliterate any graphically expert routine, and the hand forgets what it knows when the eye cannot check it, and the forms stretch and wobble, and the wobble is the mechanism, not the failure of it. Evidence: the technique is named in The Art Story and the Foundation chronology, and it is the only Twombly method with a stated purpose in his own terms, the obliteration of routine, and it explains the looseness of the early line, which is not carelessness but a deliberate removal of visual feedback, and the blind drawing is the ancestor of the blackboard loops, which also refuse the eye's correction. **Takeaway:** remove the feedback and let the hand draw without the eye, so habit cannot steer the line, and the distortion is the proof that the routine is gone, and the drawing is honest precisely because it cannot be corrected. Against the chain: no one in the chain draws blind, Mitchell looks for hours between strokes and Guston drew for eighteen months to find his break, and Twombly is the one who switches the lights off, and the difference is that their discipline is looking and his discipline is not looking.

## M4. Asemic looping, handwriting with no words

The blackboard paintings of 1966 to the early 1970s are terse, colourless scrawls on grey grounds, white wax crayon, loops and cursive that form no actual words, examples of asemic writing, and rows of rhythmic, script-like arcs that speed up into dense knots or slow into hesitant repetitions, inspired in part by handwriting drills but creating no specific words, and the eye tries to read, fails, and starts to watch rhythm instead, and tempo becomes the content, and the hand practises meaning and produces only motion. Evidence: Untitled (New York City), 1968, Museum Brandhorst, the blackboard painting at full scale, which made 70.5 million dollars at Sotheby's New York on 11 November 2015, the highest price for any Twombly, and the execution anecdote, that he sat on the shoulders of a friend who shuttled back and forth along the length of the canvas, is reported with it is said hedging in The Art Story and flatly in Wikipedia, with no primary source cited by either, so the study presents it as anecdote, as the story goes, not as documented method, see C9, and the largest blackboards are monumental, Untitled 1970 at the Museum of Modern Art is 159 and a half by 252 and an eighth inches, and the frenzy of the 1960s is disciplined here into repetition, and the picture becomes a practice field. **Takeaway:** write loops that never resolve into letters, and vary only their speed and their density, so the viewer stops reading and starts listening to the tempo, and the rhythm is the picture. Against the chain: Mitchell (#393) also built with a stroke, but hers is a loaded brushstroke that delivers colour and feeling, and his is a thin wax loop that withholds meaning, and the difference is that her stroke says and his stroke rehearses saying, and Kline (#388) also made calligraphy, but his is a single decisive gesture and Twombly's is a drill repeated until the gesture forgets itself.

## M5. Inscription as material, the name IS the picture

Starting out from purely graphic marks, he developed a kind of meta-script in which abbreviated signs, hatchings, loops, numbers and the simplest of pictographs spread throughout the picture plane in a process of incessant movement, repeatedly subverted by erasures, and eventually this metamorphosed into script itself, Katharina Schmidt, quoted in Wikipedia, and he often inscribed on paintings the names of mythological figures during the 1960s, and the Virgil series is eight drawings consisting solely of inscriptions of the word Virgil, and about 40 percent of his pictorial works from 1953 onward carry textual notations, 901 notations from 113 literary sources, the Greub catalogue, and a word appears, is crossed out, reappears, and reading and looking keep interrupting each other. Evidence: Poems to the Sea, 1959, a 24-part work on paper drawn in Sperlonga in the summer of 1959 under the influence of Mallarme's poetry, the sea present only as words and white, which made 21.7 million dollars at Sotheby's in November 2013, the record for a Twombly work on paper, and Say Goodbye, Catullus, to the Shores of Asia Minor, begun in Rome in 1972 as Anatomy of Melancholy after Robert Burton's 1621 book and finished twenty-two years later in 1994 in Lexington, Virginia, under the new Catullus title, the centrepiece of the Menil Twombly Gallery, and the long painting is the proof that the inscription is not a label on the picture but the picture's material. **Takeaway:** put a word on the canvas and then cross it out and write it again, until the word stops being a word and becomes a shape, then a rhythm, and let the reading and the looking interrupt each other, because the interruption is the picture. Against the chain: Guston (#392) also made poem-pictures, but his words sit inside the picture with the objects, and Mitchell (#393) keeps the words outside it, in the title only, and Twombly puts the word on the surface and then erases it, and the difference is that his poem is written and unwritten in the same place.

## M6. The erotic pictograph

In the 1960s erotic and corporeal symbols became more prominent, and Leda and the Swan, 1962, stages the myth as violent collision, forceful swirls, scratches and zig-zags flying in all directions for Jupiter as swan, fleshy pinks and ovoid forms for Leda and the eggs, recognizable hearts, a phallus and swan neck, and a window-like rectangle that stabilizes the explosion, and The Italians, Rome, January 1961, hides enigmatic scribbles referring to body parts, breasts, phalli, blood, semen, under multiple layers of colour, and the body is spelled out in marks that refuse to settle into illustration, and the picture is at once carnal and illegible, and the viewer recognises the parts a beat after seeing the marks. ## M7. The cycle as narrative engine

No single panel tells the story, meaning accrues across the sequence the way chapters accrue in a book, and Nine Discourses on Commodus is the first of his series conceived as a unified whole, and Lepanto is to be absorbed as a single image, a panoramic portrayal of war on a heroic scale, and the cycle is the unit of meaning, five Ferragosto, nine Commodus, ten Iliam, four plus four Seasons, twelve Lepanto, eight Bacchus, and the viewer must walk the sequence, and the walking is the reading. Evidence: Nine Discourses on Commodus, painted December 1963 in Rome, nine canvases, 204 by 134 centimetres each, Guggenheim Museum Bilbao, acquired 2007, two whorls of matter per panel, from serene cloud-like structures to bleeding wounds to a fiery apotheosis in the final panel, first shown at Leo Castelli in March 1964, installed in a jumbled order that made the trajectory undecipherable, second showing only at the 1979 Whitney retrospective, and Fifty Days at Iliam, 1977 to 1978, ten paintings, Philadelphia Museum of Art, Trojans on one side of the room, Achaeans on the other, Shield of Achilles hung outside the gallery as a preface, see C12, and Quattro Stagioni, two sets of four, the first for the Museum of Modern Art, the second for the Tate, see C11, and Lepanto, twelve paintings, Museum Brandhorst, see M12. **Takeaway:** build in parts that only make sense together, and let the sequence do the storytelling the single picture refuses, so the viewer walks the story instead of seeing it. Against the chain: Mitchell (#393) also used the polyptych, panels as stanzas, but hers is one painting cut into movements, and his is a sequence of separate paintings that add up to a narrative, and the difference is that her seam is a stanza break and his sequence is a book, and Guston (#392) also told stories across canvases, but his are objects in a room and Twombly's are chapters with no objects in them.

## M8. The grid and armature under chaos

The Commodus paintings look wild and are secretly measured, a tightly controlled armature governs their composition, numerical sequences articulating the grids, graphs, and geometric axes that form the paintings' skeleton, the Guggenheim Bilbao page, and each is based around a grid form, perhaps summoning ideas of the imposed order of the Roman Empire, The Art Story, and a mid-line guiding mark subdivides the composition, and the grey ground is negative space, and the mess is held inside a frame, and the painting looks wild and is secretly measured, and the grid wins in some panels and loses in others. Evidence: Nine Discourses on Commodus, 1963, the grid visible in the numerical sequences and the mid-line mark, and the armature is the reason the nine panels read as a reign collapsing left to right even though the paint in each panel is a whorl of matter, and the same armature logic governs the Lepanto sequence, alternating single motifs and series, tense calm, explosions of colour in the centre, red panels at the end. **Takeaway:** draw a visible grid first, then let the gesture fight it, and let the grid win in some panels and lose in others, so the wildness is legible because the measure is visible. Against the chain: Mitchell (#393) also built a lattice, but hers is the stroke itself woven into a web, and his is a grid underneath the stroke, and the difference is that her order is in the weave and his is under it, and Kline (#388) also used a structure, but his is the black beam of a single gesture, and Twombly's is a graph the gesture is plotted on.

Against the chain: Guston (#392) also painted characters, but his are objects, a hood, a shoe, a light bulb, and Twombly's are clouds, and the difference is that Guston's character is a thing and Twombly's is a condition, and Mitchell (#393) also painted feeling as weather, but hers is a valley and his is a man, and the difference is that her weather is a place and his weather is a person.

## M10. The pole-brush loop

Bacchus is made with a brush attached to the end of a pole, enabling Twombly to capture the energy of an unbroken movement across a large canvas, the brush soaked in paint, which dripped down the canvas, its red colour evoking both wine and blood, Tate, and the brushes are between two and four inches thick, laden with heavy liquid paint, and the largest canvas is 325 by 494 centimetres, and the loop climbs and falls in endless spirals, and the whole body paints, not the hand, and the loop is a lasso that never catches anything, and the gesture is amplified by the long lever arm and cannot be corrected mid-stroke. ## M11. The floral burst
