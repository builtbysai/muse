# Sergio Martino: Study Notes

**Site:** Sergio Martino (b. Tuesday 19 July 1938, Rome, Italy; living), Italian film and television director, screenwriter and producer, born into the business -- grandson of director Gennaro Righelli, younger brother of producer Luciano Martino -- the giallo stylist who became the Italian genre cinema's most reliable journeyman: five gialli in three years, then the poliziottesco, the sex comedy, the twilight western, the cannibal film, the post-apocalyptic ruin, the football comedy, and finally thirty years of television. His practice is the formula, taken seriously: take the commercial brief of the year, shoot it with more craft than the budget warrants, hang it on one strong visual device and one strong score, and move on to the next brief before the last one cools. [C30083]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Enzo G. Castellari study: Castellari ends his study with the remix -- one visual grammar run through whatever hit somebody else had last year, until the remix becomes the style -- and the other director who hired the De Angelis brothers nine times is the man who ran the same grammar through the giallo first. Where Castellari brings the slow motion and the specialist team, Martino brings the black gloves and the dream: the killer's POV that makes the viewer a voyeur, the nightmare insert that makes the heroine's psyche the location, the mud western that answers *Keoma* with a hatchet instead of a gun. The study starts where Castellari's leaves off -- with the other half of the nine-film partnership, the director whose *Torso* is the door the chain has not yet opened, the giallo that American slashers are built on, and who is still travelling to festivals to present it, in person, in 2026. ## Corrections to the brief

Four errors in the material handed to this study, all corrected before any claim was built on them. First, "The Secret of the Black Village" does NOT exist as a Martino film: searches of his filmography (Wikipedia, IMDb, Archivio del cinema italiano) return nothing under that title or any close Italian equivalent, and the closest real titles are *Il paese delle piccole piogge* (2012) and the 1961 pirate film *Il segreto dello sparviero nero* (not Martino's); the late-film slot uses his real final credit, *Il paese delle piccole piogge*, instead. Second, *Torso* was NOT produced by Luciano Martino: it was produced by Carlo Ponti, a departure from the first four gialli the brothers made together, and *Giovannona Long-Thigh* is absent from Luciano's producer filmography, so neither production credit is asserted here. Third, DVDBeaver's Bruno Nicolai credit list wrongly includes *Torso* among Nicolai's scores: *Torso*'s score is the De Angelis brothers' (Soundohm/Digitmovies OST copy, Movie Nerd credits), and Nicolai's Martino count is four, not five. Fourth, Riz Ortolani scored NO Martino-directed film: the only overlap is Lenzi's *So Sweet... So Perverse* (1969), where Sergio was executive producer and story author and Ortolani scored -- the tie is executive-producer plus story, not director-composer, and Ortolani must not be listed as a Martino composer. A fifth correction reaches back to #756 and is restated because it governs the doorway: *Mannaja* stars Maurizio Merli, not Franco Nero, and the Keoma-derivative reading stands on the shared composer and the shared actor Donald O'Brien, not on Nero. [C30087]

## Works

**W01 -- Mille peccati... nessuna virtu (*Mondo Sex*), 1969.** Mondo documentary. Grade B. The debut: a sensational survey of sex around the world, voiceover plus staged "documentary" vignettes. The mechanism is already visible in embryo -- a film built as a string of set pieces rather than a plot, a habit that will recur in every phase after it. Evidence: Wikipedia "Sergio Martino"; leg 2 table.

**W02 -- Arizona Colt Returns, 1970.** Spaghetti western. Grade A. The first feature, a sequel to Michele Lupo's *Arizona Colt* (1966) with Anthony Steffen replacing Giuliano Gemma: the gunman is framed for a stagecoach robbery, survives his own hanging by a wire trick, and is hired to rescue a rancher's kidnapped daughter. Brightly shot, conventional, competent but flat -- the baseline his later western *Mannaja* reacts against -- with a wacky sung theme ("I think I'm gonna get my gun / Bang bang") that undercuts the grit. The sung theme is the tell: even here, the score is doing the film's character work. Evidence: Spaghetti Western Database; Wikipedia "Sergio Martino".

**W03 -- The Strange Vice of Mrs Wardh (*Lo strano vizio della signora Wardh*), 1971.** Giallo. Grade A. The first of the five gialli, and the template: diplomat's wife Julie (Edwige Fenech) is tormented by flashbacks of a sadomasochistic ex-lover (Ivan Rassimov) while a razor killer stalks Vienna. Nora Orlandi's score -- a deranged "Dies Irae" that fires every time Julie flashes back to sex with Jean -- is the film's trigger mechanism, cuing the nightmare insert (M2). Glossy Vienna locations against psychosexual dread; George Hilton as the unreliable lover; produced by brother Luciano. Evidence: Spectacle Theater; feminaridens; Wikipedia "Sergio Martino".

**W04 -- The Case of the Scorpion's Tail (*La coda dello scorpione*), 1971.** Giallo. Grade A. Martino's most nimble, classical whodunit: widow Lisa (Anita Strindberg) inherits a fortune after her husband's plane explodes, and insurance investigator Peter Lynch (George Hilton) follows a trail of murders to Greece. Sunlit Athens locations, insurance-scam machinery, the giallo reduced to a clean puzzle of suspects and alibis -- the machine at its cleanest (M1). Bruno Nicolai scores; Emilio Foriscot shoots, with Giancarlo Ferrando as camera operator before his promotion to DP. Evidence: Spectacle Theater; feminaridens / Sight & Sound.

**W05 -- All the Colors of the Dark (*Tutti i colori del buio*), 1972.** Giallo / occult psychodrama. Grade A. Martino's most surreal film: Jane (Fenech), traumatised by a miscarriage after a car crash, is stalked by a blue-eyed stranger (Rassimov) and drawn into a black-mass cult. It opens with a grotesque nightmare -- quad-fragmented imagery, a mother dying in childbirth, a stiletto murder -- and the black-mass sequence (dog slaughter, blood-drinking, Bruno Nicolai's driving rock-chant) is shot for maximum delirium. Martino said he was inspired by Polanski's *Rosemary's Baby*, using Polanski-style POV shots for the heroine's delusions; the film ends on an abrupt freeze-frame. The nightmare insert (M2) is no longer an interlude here -- it is the film. Evidence: Nerdist; Daily Dead; Film Walrus; feminaridens.

**W06 -- Your Vice Is a Locked Room and Only I Have the Key (*Il tuo vizio e una stanza chiusa e solo io ne ho la chiave*), 1972.** Giallo (Poe adaptation). Grade A. From Poe's "The Black Cat": decadent writer Oliviero (Luigi Pistilli) and his wife Irina (Fenech) play psychosexual games in a decaying villa -- a black cat, a murdered mistress, a commune freak-out. Subdued, brooding, misanthropic, the opposite of the earlier films' gloss; gratuitous killer POV that points straight at *Torso*; the title itself is a line lifted from *Mrs Wardh*. Nicolai scores again; Ferrando shoots. Evidence: Spectacle Theater; Alchetron.

**W07 -- Torso (*I corpi presentano tracce di violenza carnale*), 1973.** Giallo / proto-slasher. Grade A. The hinge film, and the study's centre of gravity: a masked killer strangles and dismembers female students in Perugia, and survivor Jane (Suzy Kendall) retreats to a hilltop villa -- and the killer follows. It opens on a lecture about Perugino's arrow-pierced St Sebastian, art as murder thesis, and later projects murder-scene evidence before students the same way. Carnival mask plus black gloves plus red-scarf motif; obsessive killer POV and voyeuristic camera (upskirt shots, peeping at lesbian lovers) that make the viewer "both voyeur and accomplice"; the final twenty minutes are a near-silent cat-and-mouse sequence in the villa, including the key-and-newspaper trick; a childhood-trauma flashback explains the killer. Widely credited as a direct blueprint for *Halloween* and *Friday the 13th*. Produced by Carlo Ponti, not brother Luciano -- the one giallo outside the family machine -- and the first De Angelis score in the nine-film run. Evidence: Slant; Dyerbolical; Letterboxd/Hexagore; Cinema Sentries; feminaridens.

**W08 -- Giovannona Long-Thigh (*Giovannona coscialunga disonorata con onore*), 1973.** Commedia sexy all'italiana. Grade A. The farce machine (M6) in full: a cheese-factory owner bribes a monsignor, then hires prostitute Coco (Fenech) to pose as his wife and compromise a judge who likes other men's wives. A train ride Rome to Sicily of escalating mix-ups, then all characters -- owner, wife, whore, pimp, judge, judge's wife, jealous secretary -- converge in one house; everyone talks at 100 mph; political-corruption satire under the smut; "more sizzle than steak", the poster promising more nudity than the film delivers. A major commercial success; De Angelis score; Martino himself called it "the same story as *Pretty Woman*" and said it contains no vulgarity, while distributors demanded at least one shower or undressing scene per film. Evidence: Wikipedia "Giovannona Long-Thigh"; DVD Talk; Cine-Excess (Dania interviews).

**W09 -- The Violent Professionals (*Milano trema: la polizia vuole giustizia*), 1973.** Poliziottesco. Grade A. Milan cop Giorgio (Luc Merenda) goes undercover as a getaway driver to avenge his murdered boss and expose a crime syndicate reaching into high politics. Two kinetic car chases, one ending with a car sideways into a tree; a train-escape opening that murders a child -- the genre's signature transgression; the undercover infiltration as the film's engine (brothels to pimp to driver to bank heist); a groovy, melancholic De Angelis score that "perfectly sets the tone of metropolitan violence"; shot in a genuinely violent Milan of the Years of Lead. Released ten days after Castellari's *High Crime* -- the two poliziottesco foundation films as a dated pair, Merenda leading Martino's, Nero leading Castellari's. Evidence: Mondo-Digital; Letterboxd; Genre Grinder; leg 3 section 6.

**W10 -- The Suspicious Death of a Minor (*Morte sospetta di una minorenne*), 1975.** Giallo / poliziottesco hybrid. Grade A. Detective Paolo Germi (Claudio Cassinelli) works kidnapping cases undercover while a killer targets young women, with comedy touches sitting incongruously beside the murders. The bridge film: giallo plotting fused with poliziottesco procedure, plus deliberate comic relief -- Martino testing how far the two machines could be bolted together. Cassinelli takes over the cop role from Merenda here and carries it through the adventure films into the 1980s. Evidence: feminaridens; Cinema Sentries; Genre Grinder.

**W11 -- Sex with a Smile (*40 gradi all'ombra del lenzuolo*), 1976.** Sex-comedy anthology. Grade A. Five unconnected sketches satirising Italian sexual mores -- a bodyguard (Marty Feldman) who watches too closely, a woman offered 20 million lire for sex, a man who phones Fenech to recount erotic dreams about her (she ends up bedding a stranger under a bridge in the dark). The anthology structure is the farce machine in miniature: each sketch is one erotic premise plus a punchline twist, and the erotic premise is the joke-delivery system, not the point. De Angelis score. Evidence: Wikipedia "Sex with a Smile"; leg 2 table.

**W12 -- Mannaja (*A Man Called Blade*), 1977.** Spaghetti western (twilight). Grade A. The mud western (M4): bounty hunter Blade (Maurizio Merli) hacks off an outlaw's hand with his hatchet, then takes revenge on the silver-mine owner who destroyed his family. Shot in the sulphurous swamps of Manziana south of Rome; everything caked in mud, shrouded in fog -- fog also masked the decaying Elios Studios sets; slow motion, soft focus, an eerie heartbeat on the soundtrack; the finale shot through the villain's eyes in a deserted, fog-bound town; the hatchet as signature weapon (mannaja is Italian for axe); a moody De Angelis theme. One of the last spaghetti westerns ever made, explicitly compared to *Keoma* but more gothic-horror in feel -- the same composer, the shared actor Donald O'Brien (Caldwell in *Keoma*, Burt Craven here), and reviewers calling it Keoma-derivative. Evidence: Spaghetti Western Database; Letterboxd; Vocal Media; leg 3 section 6.

**W13 -- Slave of the Cannibal God (*La montagna del dio cannibale*), 1978.** Jungle adventure / cannibal film. Grade A. Susan (Ursula Andress) hires Professor Foster (Stacy Keach) to find her missing anthropologist husband in the New Guinea jungle; the tribe worships the couple as god and goddess. The most "reputable" cannibal film: a polished jungle adventure for the first seventy minutes -- long shots of real jungle, Giancarlo Ferrando cinematography, De Angelis adventure score -- before the cannibals arrive in the final half-hour; real animal violence and the notorious bestiality scene sit inside an otherwise conventional expedition plot. The mechanism is the delayed monster (M5): the wonder is held back until the budget's last act. Evidence: Mondo-Digital via Letterboxd; DVD Talk.

**W14 -- Island of the Fishmen (*L'isola degli uomini pesce*), 1979.** Adventure / fantasy horror. Grade A. Shipwrecked prisoners wash up on a Caribbean island ruled by Rackham (Richard Johnson), whose mad scientist breeds fishmen to loot a sunken city (Atlantis). The most ambitious of the adventure trilogy: Moreau plus voodoo plus Atlantis plus volcano; rubber fishmen suits in underwater sequences; a plantation-mansion set with a mad-science lab; gentler and more fantasy-oriented than the cannibal film -- spectacle over gore. Martino calls it the second of his adventure trilogy, after *Slave* and before *The Great Alligator*. Evidence: Films From Beyond; GarethMyles.com; leg 2 table.

**W15 -- The Great Alligator (*Il fiume del grande caimano*), 1979.** Creature feature ("Jaws in the jungle"). Grade A. Photographer Daniel (Cassinelli) is hired to publicise Paradise House, a jungle resort; the river god Kroona incarnates as a giant crocodile and eats the tourists. Shot in Sri Lanka; the creature is a stiff rubber prop shown in quick cuts (its tail is the only moving part) plus obvious miniature bridges and trucks; the set-piece climax is a massacre of tourists on a giant cruise raft; ecological messaging (resort versus tribe versus river god) wrapped in a *Jaws* rip-off; Stelvio Cipriani score with tribal rhythms. The accumulation mechanism (M5) at its cheapest and most honest: the prop cannot move, so the film cuts faster. Evidence: Horror Review; Letterboxd/Hexagore; leg 2 table.

**W16 -- The Scorpion with Two Tails (*Assassinio al cimitero etrusco*), 1982.** Giallo / supernatural (TV-origin). Grade A. Joan (Elvire Audray) dreams of ritual murders in an Etruscan tomb; her archaeologist husband (John Saxon) is then killed the same way, and she investigates a tomb, a drug-smuggling ring, and her own reincarnation. Originally shot as an eight-part TV series (about 400 minutes), hacked down to a 97-minute feature; the truncation shows -- harsh cuts, a plot both hard to follow and over-simplified; victims' heads twisted 180 degrees; maggot visions; the Etruscan tomb (shadows, sulphurous fog from a pit) is genuinely creepy. Martino abandons 2.35:1 widescreen here, and the flat TV look kills his signature angles and fast cutting -- the visible break into Phase 4. Fabio Frizzi scores, largely recycling cues from *City of the Living Dead* (1980) and *The Beyond* (1981): the Frizzi bridge between #751 and #757, and the film that ties Martino to the Fulci circle. Martino used the pseudonym Christian Plummer. Evidence: Genre Grinder; Rotten Tomatoes reviews; DVDBeaver; leg 3 section 4.

**W17 -- 2019, After the Fall of New York (*2019 dopo la caduta di New York*), 1983.** Post-apocalyptic sci-fi. Grade A. Twenty years after nuclear war, mercenary Parsifal (Michael Sopkiw) must rescue the last fertile woman on Earth from ruined New York. Built from miniatures and studio sets at De Paolis Studios, Rome, plus Arizona desert exteriors: a lovingly built miniature of the blasted city (beautiful, unconvincing); a cyborg clown with a glowing eyeball presiding over a demolition derby; ape-men, rat-eaters, knights on horseback, flamethrowers; a pounding De Angelis synth score (never released on soundtrack) repeatedly cited as the film's best element; Picasso's *Guernica* as a backdrop; credited as "Martin Dolman". Heavily influenced by *Escape from New York* and *Mad Max 2*; its premise anticipates *Children of Men*. The ruin mechanism (M5) at full stretch: keep introducing new wonders until the budget runs out. Evidence: Wikipedia "2019, After the Fall of New York"; Mondo-Digital; Soundohm.

**W18 -- L'allenatore nel pallone (*Trainer on the Beach*), 1984.** Football comedy. Grade A. Hapless coach Oronzo Cana (Lino Banfi) is hired to keep tiny Longobarda in the First Division while the owner plots relegation and the Brazilian star pines with saudade. Pure Italian popular comedy: cameos by real players, coaches and journalists carry the film; the sport is the farce stage, not the subject; a huge cult following in Italy; sequel in 2008. The last De Angelis score in the nine-film run -- eleven years from *Torso* to this, giallo to football comedy, the partnership outlasting every genre it scored. Evidence: Wikipedia "Sergio Martino"; leg 1 table.

**W19 -- Hands of Steel (*Vendetta dal futuro*), 1986.** Sci-fi action (Terminator riff). Grade A. Cyborg Paco Queruak (Daniel Greene), 70-80 percent machine, is sent to kill an environmental leader, regains his memory, and flees to Arizona -- arm-wrestling, truckers, and a laser cannon follow. Shot in the US; the cyborg forearm-maintenance scene lifts *The Terminator*; a computer readout lists his "Negative Characteristics: None"; Claudio Simonetti synth score. The film is split by tragedy: Claudio Cassinelli died in a helicopter stunt accident (clipping the Navajo Bridge) during production, and his remaining scenes use a double. The last big-screen flourish before the TV turn. Evidence: Missile Test; Reel Reviews; B&S About Movies; Wikipedia "Claudio Cassinelli".

**W20 -- Late TV work: Carabinieri (2006-07, 48 episodes); L'allenatore nel pallone 2 (2008); Il paese delle piccole piogge (2012, TV movie).** TV series / TV movie. Grade B. Long-running cop series; football-comedy sequel; his last credited directing work, a small-town TV movie. The look flattens entirely: TV pacing, TV framing, no widescreen, no set-piece bravura. The genre-hopping engine stops -- he becomes a reliable TV craftsman. *Il paese delle piccole piogge* is the final credit (IMDb lists it as his last directorial work). Graded B: the work is documented but thinly, and no detailed account of its staging was found. Evidence: Cine-Excess interview; IMDb. [C30088]

## Evolution: early vs late

**1963-1970 -- Apprentice hand.** Assistant to Brunello Rondi on *The Demon* and to Mario Bava on *The Whip and the Body* (1963, 2nd assistant director, uncredited); assistant on *Hercules Against Rome* (1964); screen story author and cinematographer on 1960s westerns; executive producer and story author on Lenzi's *So Sweet... So Perverse* (1969) -- the Lenzi tie predating the Castellari tie by four years. Mondo debut (W01), then a by-the-numbers western sequel (W02). Competent, anonymous; the only signature is the sung theme song, a habit that will recur.

**1971-1973 -- The giallo quintet.** Five gialli in under three years, all written with Ernesto Gastaldi, most starring Edwige Fenech and George Hilton, produced by brother Luciano (except *Torso*, Carlo Ponti). The arc inside the quintet is the story: *Mrs Wardh* (glossy, psychosexual, flashback-driven) to *Scorpion's Tail* (the clean classical whodunit) to *All the Colors of the Dark* (the surreal, occult, Polanski-inflected outlier) to *Your Vice* (brooding, Poe, subdued) to *Torso* (the machine stripped to its essentials -- mystery abandoned for body count and set pieces). Each film narrows the giallo until only the killer, the victims and the camera remain. This is his peak as a stylist: 2.35:1 widescreen, slow zooms, odd camera positions, fast cutting.

**1973-1979 -- Genre-hopping journeyman.** As the giallo fades, Martino follows the market cycle by cycle, at roughly three films a year: poliziottesco (*Violent Professionals*, *Silent Action*, *Suspicious Death of a Minor*), sex comedies (*Giovannona*, *Sex with a Smile* and its sequel), then the late-70s adventure trilogy (cannibal film to fishmen fantasy to giant crocodile). The through-line is not a look but a method: take the current commercial formula, shoot it with more craft than the budget warrants, and let one strong visual idea carry it -- the mud in *Mannaja*, the real jungle in *Slave*, the rubber croc in *Alligator*. Kat Ellinger's verdict, quoted in Starburst: he is "ready to embrace any new popular fad" yet "much more than a hack".

**1982-1986 -- Exploitation sci-fi and the TV turn.** *Scorpion with Two Tails* is the visible break: a TV miniseries cut down to a feature, widescreen abandoned, the signature angles gone. *2019* and *Hands of Steel* are the last big-screen flourishes -- both rip-off exercises (*Escape from New York*, *Terminator*) saved by set design, miniatures and synth scores. *L'allenatore nel pallone* (1984) shows the other exit: popular Italian comedy, which becomes his home.

**1987-2012 -- Television craftsman.** From the late 1980s on, Martino works mostly in Italian television -- TV movies (*Mozart e un assassino*, 1999; *L'ultimo rigore*, 2002), series (*L'ispettore Giusti*, 1999; *Carabinieri*, 2006-07, 48 episodes), and the 2008 football sequel. The visual language flattens to TV norms; the set-piece bravura of 1971-83 does not return. His last credit is the 2012 TV movie *Il paese delle piccole piogge*.

**What changed:** widescreen to TV frame; slow zooms and bravura set pieces to functional coverage; psychosexual dread to farce, then adventure, then TV naturalism. **What did not change:** the formula-first method, the reliance on a strong score to carry tone, and the habit of building a film around one memorable visual device. [C30089]

## M1 -- The giallo machine: gloves, suspects, trauma reveal

A whodunit engine: a black-gloved killer, a chain of elaborately staged murders, a field of red-herring suspects (each with a motive and a secret), and a final reveal that reframes everything through the killer's childhood trauma. In *Mrs Wardh* the ex-lover is the prime suspect and the razor killer works Vienna; in *Scorpion's Tail* the insurance-scam puzzle interviews every suspect in turn; in *Torso* the red scarf is recognised by the victim and the killer's trauma flashback closes the case. The machine is at its cleanest in *Scorpion's Tail* and at its most distilled in *Torso*, where the mystery half is discarded and only the murder set pieces remain. The device that makes it Martino's and not Argento's: the camera is not a witness, it is a participant -- the killer POV shots in *Torso* make the viewer "both voyeur and accomplice", and the lecture on Perugino's arrow-pierced St Sebastian at the film's opening states the thesis outright, art as murder, murder as art. Evidence: W03, W04, W07; Spectacle Theater; Slant.

**Takeaway:** a whodunit is a machine with four moving parts -- gloves, suspects, murders, trauma -- and you can strip it down to the murders alone and it still runs, because the camera is doing the suspecting for you. [C30090]

## M2 -- The dream / nightmare insert

A self-contained, visually distinct dream sequence -- fragmented, saturated, illogical -- that opens the film or recurs as a refrain, establishing the heroine's interior state as the film's real subject. In *Mrs Wardh*, Julie's flashbacks to sado-masochistic sex with Jean are cued every time by Orlandi's "Dies Irae"; in *All the Colors of the Dark*, the opening nightmare (quad-fragmentation, a mother dying in childbirth, a stiletto murder) and the black-mass sequence that follows are the film, not an interruption of it; in *Torso*, the killer's childhood-trauma flashback is the same device turned inside out. The device makes the woman's psyche the location of the film, not the city -- Vienna and London are glossy backdrops, and the real set is Julie's or Jane's head. The score is the trigger: the "Dies Irae" fires, and the film leaves reality. Evidence: W03, W05, W07; Spectacle Theater; Nerdist.

**Takeaway:** a dream insert is not a break from the story, it is the story's address -- put the psyche on screen and the city becomes scenery. [C30091]

## M3 -- The poliziottesco chase and the undercover infiltration

Two linked devices: a kinetic, destructive car chase through a real Italian city, and a hero cop who stops investigating from outside and infiltrates the criminal world from inside (brothel to pimp to driver to heist). In *The Violent Professionals* the train escape and child murder open the film, two major chases punctuate it (one ends with a car sideways into a tree), and Giorgio posing as a getaway driver for Richard Conte's syndicate is the plot's engine; *Silent Action* (1975) and *Suspicious Death of a Minor* (1975) repeat the undercover structure. The chase is the genre's punctuation; the infiltration is its plot engine. The mechanism's honesty is in the setting: shot in a genuinely violent Milan of the Years of Lead, the chase is not a stunt reel, it is the film's argument that the law can only move at speed -- the same argument Castellari makes in *High Crime*, released ten days earlier. Evidence: W09, W10; Mondo-Digital; Genre Grinder.

**Takeaway:** a chase is punctuation and an infiltration is a plot -- put the cop inside the crime and the action stops being decoration and starts being evidence. [C30092]

## M4 -- The mud-and-fog western

A spaghetti western rebuilt as a horror film: a sulphurous, swampy location; every surface and face caked in mud; fog (partly practical, partly to hide decaying studio sets); slow motion, soft focus, and a heartbeat on the soundtrack; violence delivered by an unusual weapon rather than a gun. In *Mannaja* -- the Manziana swamps south of Rome, the fog-bound opening, the hatchet (mannaja is Italian for axe) that gives the hero his name, the finale shot through the villain's eyes in a deserted town -- the mud is the film's whole identity, and reviewers note it more than the plot. Contrast with *Arizona Colt Returns* (1970), bright, conventional and forgettable: the same director, the same genre, and the difference is entirely the mud. The fog does double work, atmosphere and economy at once -- it hides the sets that the budget could not rebuild. Evidence: W02, W12; Spaghetti Western Database; Letterboxd.

**Takeaway:** one location, honestly filthy, is worth more than a townful of sets -- let the mud be the genre, and the fog hide what the money could not build. [C30093]

## M5 -- The post-apocalyptic ruin (and its jungle precursor)

A world built from miniatures, studio sets and found locations, populated by a parade of factions -- each with its own costume, creature and gimmick -- encountered in sequence like levels. In *2019* it is the miniature blasted New York, the cyborg clown's demolition derby, ape-men, rat-eaters, knights on horseback, the *Guernica* backdrop; in *Hands of Steel* the dystopian future city, acid-rain signs, the laser cannon finale; the jungle trilogy is the precursor -- *Slave of the Cannibal God* (real jungle, late monster payoff), *Island of the Fishmen* (Atlantis, voodoo, volcano), *The Great Alligator* (the resort, the rubber croc, the tourist-massacre raft). The mechanism is accumulation: keep introducing new wonders until the budget runs out. The counter-device is the delay: *Slave* holds its cannibals back for seventy minutes, and the wait is the film's discipline; *Alligator* cannot move its prop, so it cuts faster, and the cutting is the film's honesty. Evidence: W13, W14, W15, W17, W19; Mondo-Digital; Horror Review.

**Takeaway:** a ruin is a parade, not a place -- introduce a new wonder every few minutes and the audience will forgive the miniature, because the next faction is already arriving. [C30094]

## M6 -- The sex-comedy farce machine

A mistaken-identity engine: a desirable woman is hired to impersonate someone else, and an ensemble of interested parties (husband, wife, pimp, judge, secretary) converges on one location while the complications multiply; or, in anthology form, a series of self-contained sketches each built around one erotic premise and a punchline twist. In *Giovannona Long-Thigh* the Rome-to-Sicily train ride of mix-ups ends with everyone in one Sicilian house; in *Sex with a Smile* the five sketches include the man who phones Fenech to narrate his erotic dreams about her (she ends up bedding a stranger under a bridge in the dark). Fast talk (everyone at 100 mph), political-corruption satire under the smut, and a poster that promises more than the film shows. Martino himself called *Giovannona* "the same story as *Pretty Woman*" -- the farce is a love story wearing a dirty joke, and the machine runs on convergence: get everyone into the same room and let the lies collide. Evidence: W08, W11; DVD Talk; Cine-Excess.

**Takeaway:** farce is convergence -- one woman, one lie, one house, and everybody in it at once, talking too fast to notice the satire underneath. [C30095]

## M7 -- The De Angelis title-song / score machine

The score as the film's identity: a hummable, often sung theme that announces the film's tone before any image does, and a score that shifts genre with the film (groovy-melancholic for crime, pounding synth for sci-fi, moody ballad for western). Guido & Maurizio De Angelis scored nine Martino films across eleven years and seven genres -- *Torso*, *Giovannona Long-Thigh*, *The Violent Professionals*, *Sex with a Smile*, *Mannaja*, *Slave of the Cannibal God*, *2019*, *Occhio, malocchio, prezzemolo e finocchio*, *L'allenatore nel pallone* -- the through-line of the whole career, from the first giallo-adjacent score to the last commercial peak. *Mannaja*'s theme reveals the hero's face as the song begins; *2019*'s synth score is repeatedly cited as the film's best element and was never released on soundtrack; *Violent Professionals*' "groovy, melancholic" score "perfectly sets the tone of metropolitan violence". The exceptions prove the rule: *Mrs Wardh* uses Nora Orlandi's "Dies Irae" as a trigger cue, and *All the Colors of the Dark* uses Bruno Nicolai's rock-chant for the black mass -- in Martino, the score is a mechanism, not wallpaper, and when the composer changes, the film's identity changes with it. Evidence: W07, W08, W09, W12, W13, W17, W18; Soundohm; leg 1 table; leg 3 section 4.

**Takeaway:** a score that changes genre with the film is a second director -- give the music the identity and the images can follow the market wherever it goes. [C30096]

## M8 -- The Dania family machine: same faces, new genre

The company is itself a mechanism. Four of the five gialli and the key sex comedies run on the same repertory: Edwige Fenech (the fragile, eroticised heroine, six-plus films over twenty-two years), George Hilton (the unreliable lover), Ivan Rassimov (the angular menace), screenwriter Ernesto Gastaldi (eight films), producer Luciano Martino (ten films), cinematographer Giancarlo Ferrando (seven films), editor Eugenio Alabiso (five films). The family triangle is literal: Sergio directed, Luciano produced, Fenech starred -- and Fenech was Luciano's wife 1971-1979, so the core giallo run is a family business, brother produces, brother directs, sister-in-law stars, and Hilton was married to Sergio's cousin. Then the company re-forms by phase: Merenda bridges giallo to crime in the same year (co-lead of *Torso*, lead of *The Violent Professionals*, 1973); Cassinelli takes over the cop role in 1975 and carries it through the adventure films until he dies on the set of *Hands of Steel* in 1985. The mechanism's value is continuity across discontinuity: the same faces and the same crew, a new genre each time, so the audience recognises the people even when the film changes its skin -- and the producer node, Luciano's Dania Film, is the machine behind half the chain's films, including Lenzi's *Ironmaster* and Bava's *A Blade in the Dark*. Evidence: W03-W07, W09, W10, W13-W16, W19; leg 3 sections 1, 2, 5.

**Takeaway:** a repertory company is a genre machine -- keep the faces and the crew, change the film around them, and the audience follows the people through every fad the market invents. [C30097]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Martino is not a director who has a genre; he is a director who has a company and a method, and the method goes looking for genres. Early he is the giallo man, learning the whodunit from Gastaldi and the nightmare from Polanski, building the machine out of black gloves and red herrings and then stripping it down until only the killer, the victims and the camera remain in *Torso*; then the journeyman, who follows the market cycle by cycle -- crime, sex comedy, western, cannibal film, creature feature, post-apocalyptic ruin, football comedy -- and lets one strong visual idea carry each film (the mud in *Mannaja*, the real jungle in *Slave*, the rubber croc in *Alligator*, the miniature city in *2019*); then the television craftsman, whose widescreen angles flatten into functional coverage and whose set-piece bravura does not return. The score is the constant underneath the change: Nicolai's rock-chant, Orlandi's "Dies Irae", the De Angelis brothers' nine-film run from giallo to football comedy, Frizzi's recycled Fulci cues, Simonetti's synth -- the music carries the tone so the images can follow the market. And the company is the constant beside it: the same faces, the same crew, the same producer brother, a new genre each time, so the audience recognises the people even when the film changes its skin. His own programme, insofar as any source gives one, is the one he stated at Tenerife Noir in 2025: to experiment and do everything -- "experimentar y hacer de todo". The films are the proof: take the brief of the year, shoot it with more craft than the budget warrants, hang it on one device and one score, and move on before it cools. [C30098]
