Shelf I, Print & the Etched Line

Jose Guadalupe Posada

studied 2026-11-03/32 min read/6,377 words

Study date: 2026-11-03. ig-artist-study #306.

Site: Jose Guadalupe Posada (born 1852, Aguascalientes, Mexico; died 1913, Mexico City; the 1851 birth year is carried as an unsettled variant) has no Instagram presence: verified absence, not a gap. He was the engraver who built the Mexican broadside: several thousand zinc etchings across four decades (the 15,000-20,000 estimates are carried as high; Miliotes says several thousands), roughly 2% of it calaveras, cut fast on zinc, printed by the thousand, sold for centavos on cheap colored stock by street vendors. The famous works: "La Calavera Catrina" (design c.1910, first printed posthumously in the 1913 Day of the Dead broadside "Remate de Calaveras Alegres y Sandungueras"); the "Calavera Oaxaquena" broadside (1903); "Don Quijote" calavera; "La Gran Calavera Electrica"; "Calavera Maderista"; "Calavera de la Adelita"; "Calavera huertista"; the "Juego de la Oca" game board (c.1900); "Gaceta Callejera" no. 1 (May 1892); the Emiliano Zapata hero-cut portrait (the block his; the circulating impression a later Jose Sanchez printing); the Jesus Negrete crime broadside; "The American Mosquito"; the mock calavera of his own publisher Antonio Vanegas Arroyo. Identity anchors: the skull as a reusable armature; the hat as the social type; the sheet as an assembled machine of wood type, image block, verse columns, border, imprint, and price; the plate outliving the author by decades; the publisher's name on the imprint where the artist's name should be; the omnidirectional satire that refused to pick a side; the unmarked grave and the shop that kept printing him into the 1930s.

Doorway: Hop from the Frida Kahlo study (#305): Kahlo borrowed his broadside format outright for "A Few Small Nips" and modeled the skeleton on his calavera; Rivera called him "my master Posada," named the Catrina, stood her in the Alameda mural, and invented having known him in person. The chain now reaches the printmaker who supplied the imagery the painters stole: he drew on zinc for a publisher who owned the plates, signed inside the image while the publisher's name went on the imprint, sold the sheets as merchandise and not as art, and died in an unmarked grave while the shop kept printing him for seventeen years. Against the chain so far, Rivera painted the wall because the wall could not be unhung, at the scale of the building, and refused the patron on the wall. Kahlo worked beside him at the scale of the votive panel and refused a different thing: the idea that the small format is the minor one. He suggested the tin panels himself, after the Detroit miscarriage: paint the years of her life if she cannot find anything to paint. Then she painted the smallness into the counter-argument of the century. M1. The composite broadside

The sheet is not a picture; it is an assembled machine. Wood-type display header, central zinc-etch image block, letterpress verse or prose columns, small filler cuts, ornamental border, publisher imprint, price. The image block and the text are decoupled by design: Posada made images "without fixed identifications" (Cordova), and the publisher chose titles, texts, and prices, then recycled the same block under new titles on multiple occasions. The Catrina block walked across at least four surviving broadsides (the 1913 Remate, the 1920s Cucaracha, the 1924 Panteon, Han Salido por Fin) with different texts and different prices. f04 is the machine at full power: giant header "LA CALAVERA OAXAQUENA," hero etching, verse caption, three letterpress columns, small drinking-skeleton filler vignettes, border. f15 runs the same machine on a crime: ornamented headline, staged climax tableau, two dense columns of police-report prose. f10 is the newspaper variant: masthead, one large riot scene, three columns, dateline, the standing subhead promising publication "when sensational events require it." The generative content is a system, not a style: a fixed image-bank plus variable text, title, and price slots plus one assembly pass per occasion, and the occasion (Day of the Dead, a crime, an election) is what chooses the parts.

Against the chain: Rosskam (#300) never needed the shutter: he made the cut the authorship and demanded image and text refuse to repeat each other; Posada's machine is the inverted version of the same demand, the image and the text are separable parts and their recombination IS the work. Shahn (#303) lettered the image by hand because only the eye and the hand can measure; Posada's shop lettered the sheet in wood type because the type could be swapped while the block stayed. Weegee (#281) typed the headline in the car and fixed the rig at f/16; Posada fixed the rig at the zinc plate and let the publisher type the headline. Nobody else in the chain built the page as a kit of interchangeable parts where the image outlives its caption.

Takeaway: The sheet is assembled, not composed; the image is one part among many, and the parts are meant to be recombined. Watch for the decoupling: same block, new text, new price, new occasion.

M2. The image-bank economy

The zinc plate is the asset; the print is a withdrawal. Blocks stay in the shop's stock and get reprinted for decades after the author dies: Manilla's tapatia design printed in 1910, fifteen years after his death (f03); Posada's huertista printed in 1930, seventeen years after his (f20); the Zapata portrait pulled as a "later impression" by Jose Sanchez and still entering museum collections in 1960 (f11); the Catrina plate exists in two "authentic" states, the original and a family-made working copy that "likely accounts for many of Catrina images circulating after Posada's death" (Foundation). The Vanegas Arroyo sons ran the press into the 1930s. The imprint line is the version control: f10's "Imp. Santa Teresa num. 1" (1892) vs f03's "2a. Sta. Teresa No. 43" (1910) vs the 1920s Cucaracha broadside with its price raised from 5 to 10 centavos between printings (Cordova). Read the imprint and the price and you read the reprint history. The afterlife is not the exception; in this set it is the norm (f03, f08, f11, f20 are all posthumous pulls).

Against the chain: Maier (#290) never put the practice down and let the world develop her frames after she was gone; Posada's shop developed his frames for him, on a press schedule, for seventeen years, and put a price on each one. Kahlo (#305) made the panel unmissable by being small; Posada made the image unkillable by being a plate: the work survives because the asset was never the impression, it was the zinc. Nobody else in the chain separated authorship from production this completely: the artist's signature lives inside the image while the publisher's name owns the imprint and the reprint.

Takeaway: The plate is the work; the print is a receipt. Watch the imprint line: publisher, address, and price are the ledger of the block's afterlife.

M3. The calavera type-cast

The skull is a mask you hang class, profession, and allegiance on. Posada casts the identical skeletal armature as the French-hatted garbancera dandy (f01), the Don Quijote knight (f05), the Adelita on horseback (f07), the mustached charro revolutionary with his aguardiente (f09), the top-hatted publisher (f14), the giant electric hypnotist (f06), the Huerta insect (f20). The calavera literaria, the mock-epitaph verse that runs under the image, does the same work in words: the form addresses the LIVING as if already dead (Clark). Line up f01, f05, f07, f09: the skulls are near-identical armatures; the hat, the prop, and the verse do 90% of the typing. The mechanism is casting, not portraiture: pick a social type, hang the skull on it, let the props indict it. Death is the leveler and the type-cast is the instrument: everybody gets the same armature, and the wardrobe does the accusing.

Against the chain: Kahlo (#305) held one face fixed for twenty-eight years and changed the wreckage; Posada holds one skull fixed for a career and changes the wardrobe. Arbus (#280) asked permission and induced the gaze; Posada asked nothing and dressed the skeleton in the subject's own clothes. DeCarava (#297) refused the credential machine; Posada issued credentials, one hat at a time, all of them revoked by death. Nobody else in the chain ran portraiture as a casting call.

Takeaway: One rig, many costumes; the satire is in the casting, not the likeness. Watch the props: they carry the indictment the skull cannot.

M4. The hat as identity shorthand

Inside the type-cast, the headwear carries the entire social read by itself. Cordova: "The hat alone is enough to crown a Catrina." The French ostrich-plume hat is the Europeanized elite (and, per the 1913 verses that rode with the Catrina, the race-and-class traitor); the sombrero is the dandy, the charro, the revolutionary; the top hat is the publisher and the bourgeois; the china's sombrero is the soldadera; the Madero curl of mustache is the second shorthand (f09). Cover the hats on f01, f02, f14 and the three skulls are interchangeable; uncover them and you know exactly who is being mocked. Few systems compress social meaning this hard into one prop. This is also the mechanism of the Catrina's afterlife: the design travels because the read travels, and the read is all hat.

Against the chain: Parr (#282) fired the flash at noon and made the number the work: the compression of meaning into a datum; Posada compresses it into an accessory. Kahlo (#305) displaced feeling into the hardware around a fixed face; Posada displaces identity into the hardware above a fixed skull. Nobody else in the chain made a single prop the whole social sentence.

Takeaway: One accessory slot retypes the whole figure; the rest of the drawing can stay nearly constant. Watch the hat first: it names the victim before the verse does.

M5. The crowd-density engine

The hero skeleton is ringed by a dense pack of small skulls and figures that fills the frame to its edges. The mob is drawn as TEXTURE: individual skulls read as units, the mass reads as weather. The small skeletons dance, drink, cook, and jeer behind the hero; skulls heap at the base like rubble. f04 runs it at full power: the hero charro-skeleton centered, a continuous frieze of small skeletons behind, skulls piled at his feet. f06 stacks forty-plus skulls into a hill that fills the lower two-thirds. Compare the single-figure portrait mode of f09 and f11: the crowd engine is the opposite compositional choice, and it is the one the festival and panic broadsides use. The mechanism turns the individual into an occasion: one hero agent with props, N small agents with looping micro-gestures, and the density is the meaning.

Against the chain: Shahn (#303) painted history as a walkable band and kept the worker's face visible inside the machine; Posada packs the band into a single sheet and lets the worker dissolve into the texture. Rivera (#304) walked history as a parade and split time into stone and color; Posada's parade fits on orange-tan stock and everybody in it is already dead, which is what makes the parade honest. Nobody else in the chain made density itself the argument.

Takeaway: The crowd is a texture, not a census; the hero is the occasion's excuse. Watch the edges: when the frame fills to the border, the mechanism is on.

M6. The hypnosis-beam diagram

In f06 the giant skeleton projects straight radiating lines from its face toward the skull-drift: hypnosis beams, while the electric streetcar waits at right with its driver and passengers. The lines DRAW the social mechanism: the crowd is not gathered, it is mesmerized; the streetcar is the machine of the spell. The beams are the only straight ruled lines in an otherwise hand-drawn image; they do not belong to the drawing's world, they belong to its argument. This is the calavera turned into a media-theory diagram, and it is why the "Gran Calavera Electrica" reads as modern as any 20th-century cartoon: modernity-as-mesmerism, drawn as geometry laid over hand line. The mismatch of line languages is the point.

Against the chain: Rivera (#304) let the telescope and microscope be the wall's geometry, his "elongated ellipses" as architecture; Posada rules hypnosis beams across a skull-drift and makes the invisible force the geometry instead. Man Ray (#288) made the picture after the shutter, on paper; Posada draws the force that no camera could catch, straight from the skull's face. Nobody else in the chain gave the argument its own line language, separate from the drawing's.

Takeaway: Render the invisible force as geometry over the hand-drawn crowd; keep the diagram layer and the figure layer in different line languages. Watch for the ruled line in the loose drawing: it is the thesis.

M7. The crime-report assembly

The sensational broadside has a fixed assembly order: (1) ornamented display-type headline naming the criminal and the sensation; (2) a staged action tableau that freezes the climax; (3) dense justified prose columns that narrate the trial, the gang, the 1,200-page case file. The image's job is the money shot; the text's job is the police report. f15's tableau is blocked like a stage scene, three figures around the fallen lantern, and the headline's ornamented type is half the image's visual weight. The artist's name appears NOWHERE on the sheet: only the publisher's. A 21-piece Swann lot notes the sheets credit the publisher, not the artist; the Library of Congress credits Posada by attribution, not by byline. The same machine runs the Gaceta Callejera (f10): masthead, one large riot scene, three columns of reportage, dateline. Feed it a current event and it prints the sensation.

Against the chain: Weegee (#281) fixed the rig at f/16 and ten feet and typed the headline in the car; Posada's rig is the zinc plate and the publisher types the headline. Bubley (#295) photographed the assignment as a census and named every worker; the Gaceta names the bandit in wood type and buries the artist's name entirely. Nobody else in the chain ran the news machine with the illustrator deleted from the credits by design.

Takeaway: Headline type, climax tableau, file copy: three slots, one event in, one sensation out. Watch the byline: it belongs to the publisher, never the artist.

M8. The game-board spiral

f18 is the print as a playable system: 63 numbered vignette cells spiral inward to a central "EXPLICACION" panel carrying the dice rules; every cell is a tiny Posada illustration; the imprint and signature are embedded in the board; "REGISTRADA CONFORME A LA LEY." The broadside is not read, it is PLAYED: the image is the track, the text is the rulebook, the numbers are the state machine. This is the commercial mode, the 98% of the output that is not calaveras (Clark): chapbooks, songbooks, embroidery patterns, games. Interaction is native to the form: the player's token moves through the artist's image-bank, and no click-to-anything needs inventing because the board IS the interaction.

Against the chain: nobody else in the chain made the print playable. Lartigue (#289) wrote the day in the album and the album is read; Posada's board is entered. Irene (#302) left blanks in the poster for the village to finish; Posada leaves 63 numbered cells for the dice to finish. The closest the chain comes to a state machine is HCB's (#286) trapped instant; Posada traps sixty-three instants and hands the viewer the token.

Takeaway: Numbered path, rule hub, per-cell micro-illustrations: the print is a system you enter, not a picture you view. Watch the center panel: it governs the whole board.

M9. The repurposed craft lineage

Manilla's metal engraving (f02/f03): fine, dense, European-academic line, the tavern-dandy scene rendered with careful crosshatch. Posada's zinc etching (f01/f04/f09): bolder, looser, faster, more caricatural; the SAME subjects re-cut in a cheaper, quicker medium that could be drawn almost as fast as a cartoon. The scholarship: Manilla "first adapted the calavera for broadside use" (Clark); Posada "adapted Manilla's early calavera imagery and developed it significantly" (AAMG); their overlapping shop years produced "confusion among scholars regarding the authorship of many of the prints" (AAMG), and the 1910 posthumous reprint of Manilla's block (f03) shows the shop treating both men's plates as one continuous stock. The lineage is visible in the subject; the break is visible in the line: the line weight doubles, the drawing speed triples, the subject stays. Also carried, with the correction: Manilla was NOT Posada's teacher; the sources say colleague and predecessor met at the Vanegas Arroyo shop.

Against the chain: Shahn (#303) carried the camera's frames back into tempera and fresco, changing the medium but keeping the seeing; Posada carried Manilla's engraving into fast zinc, changing the medium and doubling the speed. Rosskam (#300) made the cut the authorship; Posada's cut is so close to Manilla's subjects that the authorship itself is the scholarly confusion, and the shop's stock treated both men's plates as one asset. Nobody else in the chain inherited a predecessor's whole image-bank and re-cut it faster.

Takeaway: Take the predecessor's composition, re-cut it in the faster cheaper medium; the subject is the lineage, the line is the break. Watch the line weight: it tells you which century the hand belongs to.

M10. The price tag on the sheet

The broadside carries its price and its publisher as part of the design: imprint lines, centavo prices (5 centavos, raised to 10 on a 1920s reprint, Cordova), cheap colored stock (the orange of f04, the tan of f15). The sheets were merchandise for a "downscale mestizo male audience" (Cordova), sold by strolling vendors; Posada was paid $2.00-$2.50 per image (Foundation, via Diaz Frene and Cedeno Vanegas; Blas Vanegas Arroyo told Brenner $3.00 in 1929). The Foundation's revisionist line: the prints "were 'merchandise'... bought for their use value... they were not 'art' when they were produced." The imprint is the shop's biography: f10's "Imp. Santa Teresa num. 1" (1892) to f03's "2a. Sta. Teresa No. 43" (1910) is the address history of the press. The cheapness is not a constraint on the aesthetic; it is the aesthetic.

Against the chain: Collins (#298) assembled the hyphenated Americans and filed herself among the merchandise; Posada never filed himself anywhere else: the merchandise was the whole practice. Kahlo (#305) moved to large canvases in 1939 because they were easier to exhibit and sell; Posada never moved off the centavo sheet because the sheet was the market. Nobody else in the chain put the price on the work as a first-class design element.

Takeaway: Price, publisher, and address are design elements, not metadata; the sheet announces what it costs and who sells it. Watch the imprint: it is the ledger and the signature at once.

M11. The hero-cut portrait

f11: the Zapata portrait in full poster language. Frontal, three-quarter, enormous sombrero as one continuous black ellipse, charro jacket as a hatched black mass, cartridge belt as a repeated-unit band, stern mustached face, NO background. The same bold relief vocabulary as the calaveras, applied to a living hero: black shapes do the modeling, and the figure is readable at street distance. Compare f09's skeleton in the same language: the technique does not care whether the subject is alive or dead. The mechanism is the icon portrait: one figure, three black masses (hat, torso, ground-shadow), one repeated accessory band, no scenery, built to be recognized from across the street. And the circulation story is the image-bank economy again: this impression is the later Jose Sanchez printing, the posthumous channel, but the cut is Posada's.

Against the chain: Parks (#296) restaged the icon with the cleaning woman in its armature; Posada stages the hero in the calavera's armature, and the armature does not flinch. Rivera (#304) made the personification the container, god-scale holding life-scale; Posada makes the black mass the container and the hero fills it exactly. Kahlo (#305) held the mask-face for twenty-eight years; Posada's hero wears the sombrero the way Kahlo wore the face, as the fixed read.

Takeaway: Three black masses, one repeated band, no scenery: the icon is built for street distance. Watch the background: when it is empty, the figure is the whole argument.

M12. The omnidirectional blade

"Posada made prints that satirized everyone and did not single out any one group" (encyclopedia.com); the Clark: "Seldom overtly political." Across the frames the blade swings at the Europeanized elite (f01), the charro (f04), the revolutionary (f09, f20), the bandit (f15), his OWN publisher (f14), the Americans (f19), and the living hero (f11, by elevation rather than mockery). Death is the leveler; no class is exempt. Which is precisely why the later proto-revolutionary reading had to be INVENTED: Rivera "advanced the false belief that Posada was a proto-revolutionary artist" (en.wiki), while the Foundation's research shows Posada pro-Diaz (he drew flattering Diaz portraits for the accommodationist Vanegas Arroyo), then pro-Madero, then explicitly ANTI-Zapata and Villa (he illustrated "La Jeringa de Zapata": "death to the Revolution"). Mendez's 1953 "Homenaje" is "largely a fantasy" (Foundation). The politics is in the occasion, not the artist: the same skeletal rig indicts whoever is in the news, and the audience is whoever buys the sheet.

Against the chain: DeCarava (#297) refused the credential machine entirely and kept his seeing clean; Posada issued credentials to everyone and revoked them all by the same skull. Weegee (#281) typed the headline but the camera kept the street's side; Posada's press mocked the subject and the reader in the same sheet. Nobody else in the chain kept the blade allegiance-free for a whole career.

Takeaway: The satire has no fixed allegiance; the occasion chooses the target and the rig does the rest. Watch the set, not the sheet: a single print picks a side, the archive refuses to.

Evolution

Aguascalientes 1852 (1851 carried as variant) -> the Academia Municipal de Dibujo -> 1868, apprenticed to the lithographer Trinidad Pedroza -> 1871, El Jicote in Leon, 11 issues, killed by one offending cartoon -> 1872-88 Leon, his own workshop, teaching lithography, the 1888 flood lithographs -> end of 1888, Mexico City: his own shop on Calle Cerrada de Santa Teresa (f13) -> La Patria Ilustrada under Ireneo Paz -> 1888-90 into Antonio Vanegas Arroyo's publishing house, where he meets Manuel Manilla (1830-1895) and they share engraving duties -> the broadside decades of the 1890s-1900s: Gaceta Callejera 1892 (f10), crime sheets, corridos, chapbooks, game boards, zinc-etch calaveras (~2% of the surviving output) -> 1910-13, the Revolution: a mocking "Calavera de Madero" in 1910, then pro-Madero, then explicitly anti-Zapata/Villa -> 20 January 1913, death (gastroenteritis vs alcoholism vs "acute alcoholic enteritis" carried unsettled), unmarked grave -> the afterlife: the shop reprints into the 1930s; 1921, Dr. Atl reproduces him uncredited; 1924, the shop destroyed by an offended politician, plates lost; 1925, Charlot's "Posadas" article in a Mexico City weekly (misspelled, not the European debut the myth claims); 1929-30, the Brenner/Toor/Rivera monograph; 1944, the Art Institute's "Printmaker to the Mexican People"; 1953, Mendez's fantasy "Homenaje"; 1963, Mendez's book; 1972, the Aguascalientes museum; 2013, the centenary; the Catrina becomes a global icon, named by Rivera, first printed after Posada was already dead.

The aesthetic logic: the sheet as the people's press

Everything in the work is distributed. The image is one part of the sheet, the sheet is one withdrawal from the plate, the plate is one asset in the shop's stock, and the stock keeps printing after the author is buried. Where Rivera (#304) made the wall the people's newspaper because the wall could not be unhung, Posada made the sheet the people's newspaper because the sheet could be carried home for centavos: the broadside is the mural's portable, disposable, reprintable shadow. Death is the leveler and the press is the leveler's instrument: everybody gets the skull, everybody gets mocked, and the mockery arrives on orange stock with the price printed on it. The famous image, the 21st-century Catrina logo, tends to obscure the historically retrievable Posada: what the study retrieves is not a folk saint of the Revolution (he was not one; the Foundation's research buries that reading) but a systems builder who built his system out of separable parts, the hat and the block and the imprint and the price, and then let the shop run it for seventeen years after his death. The mechanism IS the career: draw fast, cut bold, sign inside the image, let the publisher own the rest.

Network

Pedroza (teacher and employer; the lithography apprenticeship, one-way-in). Manilla (senior contemporary and colleague at the Vanegas Arroyo shop, NOT his teacher; Posada adapted his calavera imagery and developed it, and their overlapping years left the authorship confusion scholars still sort; lateral, with the 1910 posthumous reprint of Manilla's block as the shop treating both men's plates as one stock). Vanegas Arroyo (publisher; the two-decade working relationship; the shop, the imprint, the price; the Diaz accommodationism that Posada's flattering Diaz portraits served; lateral and binding). Ireneo Paz (first Mexico City employer; one-way-in). Constancio Suarez (the verse-writer; the calavera literaria channel; lateral). The Danse Macabre tradition (iconographic ancestor; no direct Goya contact evidenced, carried as tradition, not influence; one-way-in). One-way-out: Charlot (the 1925 article, the block-collecting, the 1928 Met channel); Dr. Atl (1921 uncredited reproductions); Anita Brenner ("prophet," 1929/30); Frances Toor (1930, the first monograph); Rivera (the 1930 foreword on 406 prints, NAMED the Catrina, the 1947 Alameda mural, and the invented personal acquaintance, the devotion real and the friendship fabricated); Kahlo (reverent; the Nips broadside format); Orozco (studied the broadsides); Leopoldo Mendez (900+ reproductions, the 1963 book, the TGP bridge, and the 1953 "Homenaje" fantasy); the Taller de Grafica Popular (the broadside tradition adopted); Jose Sanchez (the later printer); Roberto Berdecio (the collector channel); Eisenstein (1929, the Que Viva Mexico tribute).

Study method

Written from 20 viewed frames and 8 end-to-end source reads, with honest gaps logged: the 1913 Remate sheet itself unviewed; the 1902 "Gran Mole" and c.1905 "Regalo de Calaveras" early-Catrina sheets unviewed; the other three known Catrina broadsides seen only in Cordova's reproductions; no La Llorona broadside located; Charlot's 1925 article read via its ICAA record only; the LOC blog 403-blocked; the Barajas, Frank, Tyler and Miliotes books unread firsthand; f12 illegible beyond "workshop interior"; f14, f16, f17 behind glass. Load-bearing corrections the research forced on the brief: the Catrina was NOT named by Posada (Rivera's name, 1930, with Cordova's caveat that Rivera lied about much); Rivera INVENTED having known Posada personally; the proto-revolutionary myth is false on two independent grounds (en.wiki + Foundation: pro-Diaz, then pro-Madero, then explicitly anti-Zapata/Villa; Mendez's 1953 "Homenaje" largely fantasy); Manilla was NOT his teacher (colleague/predecessor, met at the shop); calaveras are ~2% of surviving output (Clark), the fame is posthumous curation of a sliver; the "father of Mexican printmaking" is myth-layer (the Met says "often described as" then credits Manilla first; Mexico printed since the 1500s; he "likely used photomechanical processes and deliberately made distressed-looking images," en.wiki); Charlot's 1925 article did NOT make him known in Europe (a Mexico City weekly, name misspelled "Posadas"; the channel was Mexico City to New York to Chicago); the 20,000 output figure is the high estimate (Miliotes: "several thousands"; NGA 15-20k; Met 15k+); birth 1852 vs 1851 unsettled; death cause unsettled; "died penniless" contested by the Foundation ($2-3 per image, own workshop). No Instagram: verified absence, not a gap. No mechanism invented; every engine above is footed to a viewed frame or an end-to-end source read.
