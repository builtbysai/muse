Study date: 2026-11-04. ig-artist-study #319.

Site: Katalin Deutsch (Szilasbalhas, Hungary, 19 May 1912 -- Mexico City, 2000), known as Kati Horna, Hungarian-born Mexican photographer. Photojournalist of the Spanish Civil War for the anarchist press, commercial photographer and photo editor in Mexico from 1939 (Nosotros, Mujeres), teacher, and, in the 1962 S.nob burst, a staged surrealist working with Leonora Carrington, Remedios Varo, and Beatriz Sheridan as her models. She called herself an "invisibilist" and an "obrera del arte" (art worker), refused to exhibit, and her surrealist cache was found by her daughter after her death. Identity anchors: the mask as second face; the photo-story sequence; the found object as cast member; superimposition; the gendered witness of war; the unmade bed as a stage; the one candle.

Doorway: Hop from the Leonora Carrington study: the veiled mourner. In Horna's Oda a la Necrofilia (1962) Carrington herself wears the black mantilla, veiled beside an unmade bed with a white death mask on the pillow and a single lit candle: the ritual chamber Carrington spent twenty years painting, photographed as a morning's work. Carrington is the sitter here, and the rite is no longer painted or housed in bronze, it is directed. Horna also photographed Carrington for her own 1957 collage (chromo-lithograph lady with a real metal chain) and shot Varo in 1957 wearing a mask Carrington made. Three authors, one negative: Horna shoots, Carrington makes, Varo wears. Where Carrington's study ended with the menagerie in the plaza, Horna's begins in the bedroom with one candle and no audience.

M1. The mask as second face

Horna photographs people through masks, and masks where people should be, until the mask becomes the portrait's second sitter. In the Varo portrait (1957) Varo's face is seen through the heart-shaped cutout of a white mask Carrington made for her, and the black lace edging does the work a frame would do: the mask is not worn, it is presented, and Varo looks out from inside someone else's face. In the Oda frames (1962) the white death mask lies on the pillow exactly where the lover's head would be, while Carrington herself is veiled or turned away: the mask is the absent body, and the living woman is the one in disguise. In Mujer con mascara (1961) the painted folk mask with its beard hangs beside the sitter like a double that has more face than she does; she poses next to it the way a twin would. The mechanism: the portrait has two subjects, and the mask is always the more articulate one. The human face withdraws (veiled, turned, cropped) so the mask can speak. This is triple authorship made visible: Horna shoots, Carrington makes, Varo wears, and none of them is the sole author of the face you see.

Against the chain: Carrington's (#318 M1) animal surrogate parked identity in a beast beside the sitter. Horna parks identity in a made thing the sitter holds up or lies down beside. Varo's (#317) faces were countable objects in chests; Horna's masks are faces you could wear, which is stranger because the portrait admits its face is removable.

Takeaway: Give every portrait a second face, a mask that is more articulate than the sitter, and let the human withdraw. Watch the pairing: when the mask reads as the sitter and the person reads as its holder, the mechanism is on.

M2. Series-as-sequence: the photo-story

She builds narrative chains of staged frames with a fixed cast of props, and the frames read as successive moments of one action. The two Oda frames (1962) share the room, the unmade bed, the black mantilla, the candle, the death mask: one shows the veiled mourner with her hand raised against the wall, the other the nude figure with a candle beside the bed. They are not two pictures; they are two instants, and the mind supplies the grieving action between them. The vampire series (Sucedio en Coyoacan, 1962) runs the engine at full length: 24 frames, storyboarded after the fact, Sheridan moving as a vampire through rooftop, garden, and stone sculptures. The series was largely improvised and then structured, which is the reverse of normal film direction: she shot the story into existence and found its order afterward. The verso inscription on the NGA Oda print ("Oda Necrofilia 1960 / Leonora Carrington / SNOB Mexico") treats the print as a story title page, not a standalone work. The mechanism: props are a cast (the candle, the mask, the bed return in every frame like actors), the set never changes, and narrative is what the fixed set plus a changing gesture produces.

Against the chain: Carrington (#318 M3) ran four simultaneous worlds in one frame. Horna runs one world across many frames: the cosmology is replaced by the sequence, and time does the work that Carrington's quadrants did. Varo's panels were machines with inputs and outputs; Horna's frames are instants with a before and an after you are not shown.

Takeaway: Stage one unchanging set with a fixed cast of props and shoot the story as successive instants, letting the viewer's mind build the action between frames. Watch the cast: when the candle and the mask return in every frame like actors, the mechanism is on.

M3. Director and witness are the same eye

The staged surrealist and the war documentarist are not two artists. The aerial shot of the CNT meeting (1937) is as formally arranged as any tableau: a rainy Barcelona street seen from the fifth floor of CNT headquarters on Via Durruti, hundreds of dark umbrellas composed into a single dark mass, 1930s cars parked at the edges. She found the composition, she did not make it, but the eye that found it is the same eye that staged the Oda. The breastfeeding mother in Velez Rubio (1937) is witnessed, not posed, yet carries exactly the same gravity as the La Castaneda inmates (1944), where she stages nothing and the screaming boy's face does all the work. Her own words for the ethics: "invisibilist" and "obrera del arte" (art worker), not artist. She refused exhibitions and publicity while she lived. The mechanism: the camera's contract is presence without intrusion. Whether the scene is found (the umbrella sea, the nursing mother, the asylum) or built (the mourning room, the vampire's rooftop), the camera stands at the same distance and refuses to perform. Direction, for Horna, is what you do before the shutter, and invisibility is what you do during it.

Against the chain: Varo (#317) directed by building machines that ran without her. Carrington (#318) directed by painting every inch. Horna directs by arrangement and then disappears: the only member of the chain whose authorship is an act of withdrawal. Lazo staged too, but as theatre; Horna stages as housekeeping.

Takeaway: Direct the scene, then disappear from it: arrange everything, witness it once, never perform. Watch the distance: when the staged and the found read as the same calm eye, the mechanism is on.

M4. Found objects as cast, not props

Dolls, eggs, bottles, and masks are performers with faces and roles, not things on a table. In the Hitler series (1937, made with Wolfgang Burger) the egg IS Hitler: a drawn scowling face with mustache and angry eyes, wire arms flailing, a finger pressing down to crush it, a mass of plain eggs below as the crowd. The satire works because the egg is not a symbol of Hitler; it is Hitler, small and crushable, and the plain eggs are the people watching. In A Marriage for Eggs (c. 1936) two eggs court, one presenting a rose to the other, wire limbs making them actors rather than still life. The cracked doll heads of Munecas del miedo (1939) are shot like portraits of the wounded, signed "Paris 39" like a war dispatch. In El botellon (1962) the demijohn's glass holds a face that is neither reflection nor print but something between: the bottle is performing containment. The mechanism: give the object a face (drawn, painted, cracked into) and limbs (wire, string, nothing at all), and the object stops being a thing and starts being a person with a smaller budget. The humor is macabre and the pathos is real, at the same time, because the egg cannot help being both funny and fragile.

Against the chain: Carrington's (#318 M6) hybrids were stable citizens of the canvas, painted. Horna's are literal objects with drawn faces, photographed: the hybrid is cheaper, quicker, and crueler. Varo's machines were built from household objects but stayed machines; Horna's objects quit being objects.

Takeaway: Cast the object: give it a face and limbs, a role and an audience, and let it perform the scene a person would. Watch the face: when the drawn scowl makes the egg a person and the person in the frame irrelevant, the mechanism is on.

M5. Superimposition as photomontage

She layers one reality over another in-camera or in print rather than juxtaposing them side by side. In Subida a la Catedral (1938/1960) a woman's face is ghosted across a Barcelona stone wall with barred windows and a staircase; the image was made to carry text-like meaning (published as "La mujer espanola antes de la revolucion"), and the superimposition reads as a sentence: the woman IS the wall, the wall is her confinement. In the 1957 Carrington collage, Carrington's photographic head (beret, a real metal chain around her neck) is pasted onto a chromolithograph Renaissance body and architecture, with applied color: the collage is worn as jewelry, and the chain is the one true object in a field of printed ones. The mechanism: two complete images occupy the same space, and neither wins. The ghost is not a double exposure trick; it is a statement that two realities are true at once. The real metal chain against the printed gown is the tell: she lets one honest material touch the collage so the viewer feels the seam and believes the rest.

Against the chain: Carrington's (#318 M3) worlds were stitched side by side, mutually indifferent. Horna's are stacked, mutually haunted: the face does not sit next to the wall, it is in it. Varo's doubles were countable; Horna's are uncountable, because the ghost has no edge.

Takeaway: Stack two complete realities in one frame and let neither win; ground the ghost with one true material so the seam is felt. Watch the layer: when the face is in the wall rather than on it, the mechanism is on.

M6. The domestic interior as stage

Bedrooms and rooftops become theatres with almost no set dressing. The Oda's whole set is an unmade bed, one candle, one pillow mask: that is everything, and it is enough for a mourning ritual because the ritual is in the gesture, not the furniture. The vampire's rooftop (1962) is a bare Coyoacan roof with vent pipes; the set is the city itself, undecorated, and Sheridan's bat-wing pose against the sky does the rest. The mechanism: the poorer the set, the stronger the performance. One candle is lighting design; the unmade bed is costume. This is the economy of someone who learned photography in agencies and war zones, where you shoot what is there. The surrealism is not in the props (there are barely any) but in the permission: that a bedroom can be a chapel if the camera treats it like one.

Against the chain: Carrington's (#318 M5) ritual chambers were fully furnished and painted into ceremony. Horna's are half-empty rooms the camera consecrates by pointing. Varo built the room into a machine; Horna leaves the room a room and changes what happens in it.

Takeaway: Stage the rite in a half-empty room: one candle, one bed, one mask, and let the camera's attention do the consecrating. Watch the set: when removing one more object would break the spell, the set is right.

M7. Portraits that perform the sitter

Her circle does not sit for likenesses; they perform themselves in costume, and the portrait is a collaboration with as many authors as participants. The Varo mask portrait is triple-authored: Horna shoots, Carrington makes the mask, Varo wears it. The 1957 Carrington collage re-costumes Carrington as a Renaissance lady, her own photographic face pasted into art history, the real chain around her neck signing the theft as hers. Jose Horna at the Edward James maquette (1960) is doubled by his own reflection in the model's glass: the architect-model and the man merge into one image, and the portrait is of the work as much as the worker. The mechanism: the sitting is a performance the sitter co-directs, and the photograph records the performance, not the person. Nobody is caught off guard in Horna's portraits; everybody is in on it. That is why the images feel intimate rather than invasive: the camera was invited.

Against the chain: Carrington (#318 M1) parked identity in an animal that did the same work as the sitter. Horna parks identity in a role the sitter chose: the mask, the Renaissance lady, the architect in his glass. Varo's figures were technicians in her machines; Horna's sitters are co-directors of theirs.

Takeaway: Let the sitter co-direct: costume, mask, or role first, likeness second, and credit the performance in the frame. Watch the invitation: when the sitter is clearly in on it, the portrait is earned.

M8. Gendered witnessing

The war eye deliberately chooses the subjects male war photography ignores. In Velez Rubio (1937) the war is shown through feeding, not fighting: a woman breastfeeding, surrounded by women with babies, outdoors in daylight. The CNT meeting (1937) is not leaders on a stage but a sea of umbrellas: a collective body, photographed from above as one organism. In La Castaneda (1944) the inmates are photographed as subjects with interior life; the screaming boy is centered, not the bars, and the attendant stands with them rather than over them. The literature later named this "gendered witnessing," but Horna was already practicing it in 1937 while Capa shot the battlefront: she shot the rearguard, the women, the children, the mad, the people war happens to rather than the people who make it. The mechanism: point the camera at what the official record leaves out, and shoot it with the same formal gravity as a state portrait. The breastfeeding mother gets the composition a general would get.

Against the chain: nobody else in the chain was a witness; they were all directors (Varo, Carrington) or poets (Lazo). Horna is the chain's only photojournalist, and the only one whose mature surrealism grew out of documentary ethics rather than painting technique. Her staged work inherits the witness's gravity: that is why the Oda feels like reportage from a grief.

Takeaway: Photograph what the official record leaves out, with the formal gravity of a state portrait. Watch the subject: when the rearguard gets the composition the battlefront would get, the mechanism is on.

M9. Found surrealism: the documentary that reads as staged

Some of her most surreal images are straight records. The Carnaval de Huejotzingo (1941) shows men in horned black masks and dark cloaks advancing through smoke, one pointing at the camera: it looks directed, and it is documented ritual. Nobody staged it for her; she was there. The flea-market cardboard cutouts (1933) are a painted suited man with a tray and a woman in a shawl, standing among junk and scattered shoes: she found the mannequin-couple, she did not arrange them, and the joke is in the finding. The mechanism: the world stages itself if you go where the costumes are (carnival, flea market, asylum). The camera's job is to be present at the rite, not to invent it. This is the documentary twin of M6: there, she builds the poor set; here, she finds the rich one. Both run on the same fuel, which is showing up.

Against the chain: Carrington (#318) painted every rite she showed. Varo (#317) built every machine. Horna is the only one who sometimes just went and stood in front of the marvelous, and the marvelous, obligingly, performed. The chain's painters had to invent the impossible; Horna's camera kept finding it wearing a mask.

Takeaway: Go where the world already wears costumes: carnival, market, asylum, and let the found rite perform. Watch the smoke: when the image reads as staged but the caption says documented, the mechanism is on.

M10. Object still life of the everyday-uncanny

Small, quiet photographs where one displaced object carries the whole charge. In Invierno en el patio (1939) a plaster head and a mask lie in snow among bare branches: winter does the staging, and the mask's painted face against the white is the entire statement. The flea-market cutouts (1933) are the same engine in daylight: the couple abandoned among shoes and leaves, the joke in the framing rather than the subject. The mechanism: displacement plus framing, nothing else. The object is ordinary (a plaster cast, a cardboard figure), the place is wrong (snow, junk), and the camera refuses to explain. The charge comes from the refusal: no caption, no narrative, just the thing where it should not be, photographed straight. This is the quietest engine in her work and the most portable: it needs no models, no sets, no history, only an eye for the thing out of place.

Against the chain: Carrington's (#318) impossible needed tempera and cosmology. Varo's needed machines. Horna's needs a mask in snow. The chain's painters earned the uncanny with labor; Horna earns it with placement, which is the photographer's native advantage and the reason her smallest works travel best.

Takeaway: Place one ordinary object wrong (a mask in snow, a couple in junk) and photograph it straight, refusing to explain. Watch the displacement: when the framing is the only joke, the mechanism is on.

Evolution

1931-33 Berlin and Budapest: agency training. At 19 she moves to Berlin (1931), joins Brecht's circle, absorbs Bauhaus and radical politics; assists at Dephot; her mother funds a course with Jozsef Pecsi in Budapest (1932), where she befriends the teenage Endre Friedmann, the future Robert Capa. No viewed works survive from this phase; she later said the early work was lost in the war, but the crates survived in Amsterdam and were rediscovered 2016-2022. 1933-34 Paris: street and agency reportage with a Linhof for Agence Photo. The flea markets (1933) already show the signature move: pointing the lens at discarded objects and giving them personality. She meets the surrealists at the Cafe des fleurs. 1935-37 Paris: satirical egg-comics with Wolfgang Burger, Max Ernst's disciple, starring painted eggs and vegetables as Hitler and his crowd. She keeps her distance from militancy (FAI sympathizer, not militant) and settles into her default register: sharp, subtle, somewhat macabre humor. 1937-38 Spain: war as gendered witnessing. Commissioned by the CNT to photograph collectivized Aragon pueblos for propaganda abroad; works in the CNT-FAI propaganda department; edits the anarchist weekly Umbral, where she meets Jose Horna. While Capa shoots the battlefront, she shoots the rearguard: nursing mothers, umbrella crowds, and photomontages like Subida a la Catedral, a ghosted face captioned "La mujer espanola antes de la revolucion". 1939 Paris: dolls of fear and winter still lifes (Munecas del miedo, Invierno en el patio), signed "Paris 39" like dispatches; "Lo que va al cesto" made in Paris, July 1939, becomes her first Mexican publication in Todo. She and Jose flee via the Mexican embassy; she travels as Catalina Fernandez / Catalina Partos; Veracruz, 31 October 1939. 1940s-50s Mexico City: the art worker years. The house at 198 Tabasco Street, Colonia Roma, becomes the exiled surrealists' hub. Full-time photographer for Nosotros (from 1944), chief photo editor of Mujeres (1958); architecture commissions with Ramirez Vazquez, Legorreta, Lazo; teaching from 1958. Portraits of the avant-garde, studies of confinement (La Castaneda 1944, Titeres en la Penitenciaria 1945), staged sessions with Varo and Carrington, all three in Colonia Roma by 1943. She refuses exhibitions and publicity; the daughter finds the surrealist cache after her death in 2000. 1962: the S.nob burst, "el episodio mas fructifero de su carrera." For the Fetiche section she produces the Oda, Impromptu con Arpa, and Paraisos artificiales: Carrington as grieving widow, folk masks, a face in a demijohn, Sheridan's 24-frame vampire fable. Her husband is gravely ill; he and Varo both die in 1963. After 1963 she and Carrington grow closer and collaborate on photographs of Jodorowsky's staged spectacles. She teaches until 1999. The arc: from agency apprentice to war witness to art worker to, at fifty, the director of her own photo-stories. The eggs of 1936 and the eggs of the Oda's candlelit room are the same cast, twenty-six years apart: she never changed her actors, only their stage.

Aesthetic logic: the invisibilist

Every Horna image is staged or found, and none of them is performed for the camera. That is the whole logic, and she named it: the invisibilist. The war photographer and the surrealist director are the same person because both do their work before the shutter and then get out of the way. The mask gets the portrait because the face withdraws. The egg gets the satire because the dictator is small. The breastfeeding mother gets the state-portrait composition because the rearguard is the war. The bedroom gets to be a chapel because the camera treats it like one. Nothing is explained, nothing is captioned in the frame, nothing winks. The macabre humor (eggs courting, Hitler crushed by a finger) and the gravity (the screaming boy, the veiled mourner) share one register, which is attention without intrusion. She called herself an art worker, not an artist, and the humility is technical, not moral: the worker's job is to set the scene and disappear. That is why the 1962 burst is the summit: at fifty, with her husband dying, she finally staged the work with no client, no magazine, no propaganda office, only her friends as cast and one candle as lighting. The invisibilist, for once, directed everything, and still refused to be seen.
