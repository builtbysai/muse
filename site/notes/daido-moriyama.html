M1. The no-viewfinder throw (the doorway mechanism)

The whole study hinges on one refusal. Where Noguchi (#273) declares himself with the viewfinder and lets the moment decide, Moriyama deletes the viewfinder and lets the body decide. His own words, from the quote page: "I prefer taking photographs without looking through the viewfinder." And the body's version: "most of my snapshots I take from a moving car, or while running, without the finder, and in those instances one might say that I'm taking the pictures more with my body than with my eyes." Tomasz in FRAMES gives the technical reading: knowing the camera in and out, noticing something within a millisecond, pointing, pressing. The mechanism is not carelessness. It is a transfer of authority from the eye to the body, from aim to reflex. f13 is the doctrine embodied by the 85-year-old master himself: photographed from behind at Enoshima, long hair, compact digital camera held at arm's length at chest height, shooting two pedestrians against old buildings, no finder, no LCD check. f15: the same man in a long coat and scarf, night plaza under an elevated highway, camera at arm's length, rain-wet tiles mirroring the street lights. He has been shooting this way for sixty years and the posture never changed, only the camera got smaller.

Takeaway: the camera is thrown, never aimed; the body is the shutter; never compose, never check, never reframe.

M2. Are Bure Boke as method (rough, blurred, out-of-focus)

The Provoke style, foremost associated with Moriyama (Ocula), and it is a method, not a look. The manifesto line: "My photos are often out of focus, rough, streaky, warped, etc. But if you think about it, a normal human being will in one day receive an infinite number of images, and some of them are focused upon, others are barely seen out of the corner of one's eye." The refusal of the craft standard: "For me, capturing what I feel with my body is more important than the technicalities of photography. If the image is shaking, it's OK, if it's out of focus, it's OK. Clarity isn't what photography is about." The process is documented: Kodak Tri-X pushed to 1600, underexpose and overdevelop in hot D-76, rough agitation, extreme contrast, blown highlights, exaggerated grain. f02 is the method as a single frame: two policemen at a diner counter, the left officer's face blown to pure white by the flash, heavy grain, slight tilt. The flash does not illuminate the subject; it erases him. That is the Are-Bure-Boke contract: the roughness is not applied to the picture, it is the picture's argument about how a day of images actually arrives at a human being.

Takeaway: roughness is the honest rendering of vision; never sharp, never graded smooth, never a clean digital grade; the error is the composition.

M3. The volume machine (make more than not)

The doctrine Dante (#268) made his religion has its ancestor here, but Moriyama's version is wilder and more physical. The numbers: "often getting through 36 exposures within a couple of hundred meters"; "close to 300 books" (Hasselblad); over 150 photo books and more than 100 solo exhibitions since 1968 (Ocula); Record, the self-published journal, 50 issues and counting. The operating instruction: "forget everything you've learned... just shoot. Take photographs ... of anything and everything, whatever catches your eye. Don't pause to think." The theory of it: "Photography is not the endeavor to create a two-dimensional work of art, but by taking photo after photo, I come closer to truth and reality." Watchable in the frames: f10, the color-era walk, a man in a pinstripe jacket walking away down a Shinjuku back alley, the back instead of the face, because the walk is the subject; f15, the wet plaza, the hunter still out after dark. The unit of the work was never the frame. It was the roll, the day, the book, the pile. The canon hung flat in f06, the 2026 "The Hunter" gallery hang, is sixty years of hunting compressed to one wall sequence, and the sequence never pretends any single print is the point.

Takeaway: volume is the editing philosophy; the archive, not the frame, is the unit; never curate as you go, never stop for the one perfect frame.

M4. The stray-dog gaze

Misawa, Aomori, 1971, on a camera-magazine assignment: "I stepped out of the hotel in the morning... the dog was just there. So, I immediately took several pictures." Then the darkroom discovery: "I realized later in the darkroom when I printed the image how amazing the dog's expression is. Snapshots are all about an instant moment and this dog instantly became a part of me. I am actually honored to be compared with that dog." The walk is the dog's walk: "I basically walk quite fast. I like taking snapshots in the movement of both myself and the outside world. When I walk around, I probably look like a street dog because after walking around the main roads, I keep wandering around the back streets." And the doctrine of no plan: "When I go out into the city, I have no plan... I am like a dog: I decide where to go by the smell of things, and when I am tired, I stop." The dog is not a mascot. It is the practice's self-portrait: move by smell, stop by tiredness, wander the back streets after the main roads. The frames show the token's circulation: f06 (the dog hung in the gallery canon), f09 (the dog at storefront scale on a Uniqlo glass panel, the icon as streetwear graphic), f16 (two dog prints, one normal, one mirrored, in the Daido Slide bookstore display). One dog, fifty years, every scale.

Takeaway: the gaze is canine, not curatorial; move by smell, stop by tiredness; no plan, no target list; the dog is the recurring token, never a mascot.

M5. Farewell Photography (dismantle the medium)

1972, the rupture book: 写真よさようなら. The mechanism is stated by the maker: "I tried to dismantle photography but ended up being dismantled myself." And: "I wanted to go to the end of photography." Ocula's reading: technological mistakes as finished products, pushing at the definition of a successful photographic image. Photogpedia: a revolutionary approach and contempt for conformity. The point was never provocation for its own sake. The point was that the medium's failure modes, overexposure, grain, halftone, the print falling apart, are the medium telling the truth about itself. f04 is the aftermath made literal: the Farewell iconography flattened onto a skateboard deck, an extreme halftone close-up of lips and teeth, "TEAM FAREWELL PHOTOGRAPHY" as a brand. The dismantling, completed by the market. And it does not diminish the book; it proves the mechanism. Once you declare that the mistake is the finished product, the culture is free to keep copying the mistake forever, which is M6.

Takeaway: the failure modes are the medium; overexposure, halftone, and error are finished products; never correct, never rescue, never apologize.

M6. Copy of a copy (photographs of photographs)

The metaphysics of the whole practice: "What else is a photograph but a copy to begin with?" And the ethics to match: "I've even considered doing away with the copyright symbol from my own photo books... I think everyone should be free to copy anything they want to." The practice: he photographs posters, billboards, TV screens; his silkscreens from the 1970s onward are reproductions of reproductions (Hasselblad; the 2020 Silkscreen show at Taka Ishii; Warhol credited as the influence). Ocula on Another Country in New York: images that "insist on the 'photographed-ness' of photography," two film cells per image. The frames are the copy chain made visible: f04 (the halftone deck), f09 (the dog at storefront scale), f11 (his night-city imagery functioning as graphic design on the STREET: NEW YORK poster, type-on-type, the designer's roots showing). There is no original anywhere in the chain, and the work never pretends otherwise. That is why the Hasselblad citation calls it a radical approach to the medium itself, not just to the subject.

Takeaway: every image is a reproduction of a reproduction; photograph the poster, print the photograph; never pretend the first copy exists.

M7. The book-as-camera (publication as the primary medium)

Tate's framing: "Moriyama is perhaps most celebrated for his photobooks," and the 2012 Tate show took the medium's dissemination, the central role of the photo-book in avant-garde photography, as its theme. The mechanism is speed: "I wanted to print out fragments of my true daily life for people to see... I would print them out as quickly as possible and have people look at them." Record is not a book, it is "a lifeline, which is as essential as electricity or gas." The torrent: Japan: A Photo Theatre (1968), Farewell Photography and A Hunter (1972), Light and Shadow (1982), Tights (1986/87), Shinjuku (2002), Daido Slide (2024, essays by Phillips, Chandes, Baker, and Nogueira, design by Satoshi Machiguchi). f16 shows the current state of the machine: the Daido Slide bookstore display, the book as object, the dog as repeatable print icon beside it. The book is the camera because the book is the shutter's real destination. He does not take photographs and later make books. He walks in order to print, and the printing is the walk's continuation.

Takeaway: the book is the camera; print fast, sequence loosely, publish as a lifeline; never the single definitive print, always the stream.

M8. Memory and time as material (the late doctrine)

The doctrine that folds all the others into one photography. TOKION: "A photograph is a 'record.'... a photograph is both a 'commemoration' and a 'memory'... These are for sure photographs I have taken, but I feel as if I am encountering them for the first time." And the temporal collapse: "Whether they were taken in the past or will be taken in the future, they all come together to form a single photography." And the entropy: "People steadily lose the landscapes they have accumulated... I think people continue to live in the present because we forget most every little thing." The material metaphor: "Photographs are pieces of the everlasting world ... daily life ... and fossils of light and time." The works of this doctrine: Memories of a Dog (1984/1997), A Journey to Nakaji (1987, a pilgrimage to Nakahira), and the 2022 exhibition's slide-randomized projection of all 50 Record issues, the archive shuffling itself. The essay anthology says it in the title: The Past is Always New, the Future is Always Nostalgic (2000). After the throw (M1), the rough grain (M2), the volume (M3), the dog (M4), the dismantling (M5), the copies (M6), and the books (M7), this is the resting position: one photography, past and future collapsed, the print as fossil.

Takeaway: past and future frames belong to one photography; time is the material, the print is the fossil; never nostalgia, never a timeline, never dates.

The aesthetic logic

Five clauses, and they are all forms of letting go. First, THE THROW IS THE PRODUCT: the body aims, the eye does not, and the frame that lands is the one that was true. Second, ROUGHNESS IS HONESTY: a day of images arrives blurred, streaked, and blown out, and the photograph that admits this is the accurate one. Third, VOLUME IS THE METHOD: the roll, the day, the book, the pile; no single frame carries the work. Fourth, THE COPY IS THE ORIGINAL: every image is a reproduction, so photograph the poster and print the photograph without apology. Fifth, TIME IS THE MATERIAL: fifty years of walks are one walk, and the print is its fossil. The essay this logic writes is one sentence long: walk fast, throw the camera, let the rough ones stand, print everything, and let 1971 and 2026 share the frame.
