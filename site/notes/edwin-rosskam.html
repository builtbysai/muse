Site: Edwin Rosskam (March 15, 1903, Munich, Germany, to American parents; died February 25, 1985, Roosevelt, New Jersey) has no personal Instagram presence: verified absence, not a gap. He learned photography from Man Ray in Paris in 1927-28, took part in the surrealist ferment, and spent three and a half years in French Polynesia making what Louise Rosskam later called "very arty photography... it wasn't documentary in any way" (via Saretzky). Identity anchors: born to candy-manufacturing parents (Good 'n' Plenty); enemy-alien child in Germany during WWI, father dead there; arrived Philadelphia in 1919; one year at Haverford College, about four at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts; a painter who showed at the Carnegie International and held a one-man show in Paris; married Marguerite Lipp in 1926 (apartment designed by Le Corbusier; photographed there by Andre Kertesz in 1927-28); met Louise Rosenbaum in Atlantic City in the mid-1930s and married her in 1936; the weekly Philadelphia Record picture page "Camera Tales of the Town" in 1936, his first photo-story laboratory, captioned by a coworker and credited to no one; the 1938 Puerto Rico assignment for Life with Louise (the Ponce massacre aftermath, the story killed by the governor); the Alliance Book Corporation's "Face of America" city books from 1937 to 1940, all edited by him, much of the photography and darkroom work by her; one year as Stryker's FSA editor (1939-40, hired at a salary "second only to his own"); the photo-direction of "12 Million Black Voices" (Viking, October 1941); the 11,000-square-foot Grand Central Defense Bond photomural installed December 1941, then the world's largest photo mural; the Standard Oil years (1943-46) as a photographer, not an editor, with Louise, covering "oil from the geologist to the sharecropper"; the Puerto Rico years (1945-53), building a Tugwell photo file and then the Division of Community Education (DIVEDCO), the million-dollar-order education program that came in around $700,000; the 1953 move to Roosevelt, New Jersey, back to painting and writing; and the novel "The Alien" (1964), the Roosevelt book (1972), and the grave beside Ben Shahn's. He quit painting because, in his own telling, he was not a painter; the rest of his life was the search for the place where his eye belonged, which turned out to be the desk where other people's pictures were kept or killed.

Doorway: Hop from the Delano study (#299): Delano took Stryker's assignment and directed the essence in front of the lens, the pose made openly in service of the sitter. Delano was the sitter's godfather on his first assignment (Rosskam drove southern Virginia with him "to brief me, and be my godfather," Delano said in 1965: "my first mentor really, the guy who looked after me, was Ed Rosskam"). Rosskam was the payroll's godfather in general: he got Delano hired (the memory splits on the details, see the study-method note), wrote the twenty-page researched scripts that told photographers what the file lacked, and then spent the war years proving he could also hold the camera himself, from the Mississippi towboats to the Puerto Rican basement that became DIVEDCO. M1. The cut is the authorship: selection as creation

What it is: Rosskam's doctrine, stated in the 1965 oral history, and the load-bearing sentence of the whole run: "Photography is a matter of selection... you point your camera at a particular frame of amorphous reality, select that frame out and click the shutter and say, 'This is it.'" As an editor he performed that selection a second time, on other people's frames: placing FSA file photos in publications, choosing images for the Alliance books ("Home Town" cut 60,000 words to 20,000: "You cut it"), photo-directing "12 Million Black Voices," answering picture requests by mood ("I have to illustrate something that has a gray mood... So you went and got it"). He watched Stryker's picture-killing from the inside and named it for what it was: "the method by which this was done, which was a punching of holes through negatives, was barbaric to me because I'm sure that some very significant pictures have in that way been killed off, because there is no way of telling, no way, what photograph would come alive when." A hole punched through a negative is a frame murdered before its trial, and he knew it. The one concrete cut this run could verify: in three weeks of Bronzeville with Wright, they photographed a gangster who "didn't seem to like it. It didn't happen to appear in the book. It's in the File, I guess." The File kept what the book killed; the archive outlives the editor, but only because the editor put it there.

What it IS, watchably: the Flashbak Chicago set against the book. f17 through f20 - the steady-gazed boy, the milk-crate readers, the box-office queue, the jump-rope laugh - are the Chicago the book's edit kept out: ordinary, joyful, unremarkable. The book's edit kept the downbeat. Rosskam's own frustration is the receipt: the photographs "illustrate Wright's downbeat history instead of providing contrast." The same city, the same assignment, two Chicanos of evidence, and the cut decides which one history reads. f11 is the cut at the other end of the scale: the mural's giant children's faces were chosen, composited via glass negatives, printed in 48-inch strips, and mounted through station doors. Selection at 11,000 square feet.

Against the chain: Winogrand (#278) sprayed the block and let the mountain sort it out; Rosskam was the mountain, and he knew sorting was authorship. Evans (#284) deleted the photographer; Rosskam deleted the photograph, on purpose, in daylight, with reasons. Weegee (#281) typed the headline in the car; Rosskam wrote the headline by choosing which picture the headline got. Collins (#298) let the caption carry the seamy negative against the clean one; Rosskam decided which negative the caption would ever meet. Nobody else in the chain treated the wastebasket as a medium.

Takeaway: make the selection the picture: the frame that lives and the frame that dies are both made by the cut, and the File is the editor's autobiography.

M2. Text and image must not repeat: the contrapuntal photo-book

What it is: Rosskam's thesis on the photo-book, stated as a rule and violated, in his own judgment, by his most famous book: "My thesis was then, and still would be... that the photographs should never be interfered with by saying the same thing in the text that you are in the text and vice versa. There are things you can only say with words, there are things you can say better with pictures than any other way... you read them jointly." The page is contrapuntal: words carry what only words can carry, pictures carry what only pictures can carry, and the reader hears both voices at once. He held up the Sherwood Anderson "Home Town" book as the better-built object (60,000 words cut to 20,000, "You cut it": the cutting itself the craft), and judged "12 Million Black Voices" a partial failure on his own terms: the photos that made the cut illustrated Wright's downbeat history instead of providing the contrast he had wanted. The mechanism has a direction: agreement is easy and dead; the friction between word and image is where the book lives. The Philadelphia Record picture page in 1936 was the laboratory: weekly, fast, words and pictures set beside each other, the coworker's captions doing the carrying the pictures could not.

What it IS, watchably: f12 against its caption. The image is tender: a mother leaning over a baby asleep in a hammock, another child watching, a wooden shack. The caption editorializes poverty: "The hammock saves space in a shack already overcrowded." Word and image say the same thing - poverty - and the picture dies a little: it becomes an illustration of its own caption instead of a picture. f10 is the honest version's ghost: the caption says needlework industry, the image says family portrait, and the friction between them is more interesting than either alone. Rosskam's thesis is a complaint about f12 and a defense of f10.

Against the chain: Lartigue (#289) wrote the day in the album, image and handwriting in the same hand; Rosskam split the voices and made them argue. Wright's text and Rosskam's pictures in the actual book are the failure case he admitted: both saying the same downbeat thing, the book reading once instead of jointly. DeCarava (#297) refused the credential machine and printed the dark as pigment; Rosskam refused the redundant page and demanded the friction. Nobody else in the chain made the layout a moral question.

Takeaway: build the page so words and pictures never say the same thing: read them jointly, and let the friction between them be the book.

M3. The shooting script: directing without a camera

What it is: the researched written brief - sometimes twenty pages - that tells a photographer what holes exist in the file, what economic pattern to read, and what to look for, without dictating a single shot. Rosskam wrote them for Marion Post Wolcott (the winter script: shoot the season, shoot the pretty towns, shoot what the file lacked), for Vachon, for others. The mechanism is direction at one remove: the editor never lifts the camera, but the coverage that comes back is his composition, argued in prose before it was shot in film. The other half is the godfathering: Delano's first assignment, southern Virginia, Rosskam driving along "to brief me, and be my godfather," the script delivered in person, the beginner walked into the field. Stryker's shooting scripts were famous; Rosskam's were his, researched harder, and the research was the direction: he read the file's gaps the way a photographer reads light, and told the shooter where the shadows were.

What it IS, watchably: the coverage the script produced. Wolcott's winter, which appeared in "Home Town," exists because a script asked for winter; the file's "pretty towns" exist because a script asked for what the file called the gigantic poor house. f14 - the Connecticut Avenue service station, the "AUTO WASH 75c" sign, the tire change - is the urban coverage the scripts pushed for: the file's rural bias corrected by a written order. The mechanism is invisible in the frame, which is the point: the script never appears in the picture, but the picture would not exist without it. It is direction that leaves no fingerprint on the negative.

Against the chain: Delano (#299) declared the camera and moved the people; Rosskam never touched the camera and moved the assignment. HCB (#286) drew the geometry and trapped the instant; Rosskam drew the assignment and trapped the season. Strand (via #299) urged Stryker to hire Delano; Rosskam wrote the twenty pages that told the hired man where to point the lens. Lange (#293) kept the seeing for herself; Rosskam wrote the seeing down and handed it to Wolcott. Nobody else in the chain authored pictures by mail.

Takeaway: direct without a camera: write the researched brief that names what the file lacks, and let the coverage that returns be your composition.

M4. The good makes the bad count: contrast as evidence

What it is: the editorial ethics, stated as a demand on photographers: "If I am to illustrate the good and the bad, I got to have the good to make the bad count. If you don't give it to me, how am I going to get it?" A documentary argument needs its positive pole; poverty without its counterweight reads as caricature, and he refused the caricature: "picturesque is somebody else's poverty." The mechanism is contrast as evidence: the joyful frame does not soften the grim one, it certifies it - the file that contains both can be trusted about either. He fought the file's "gigantic poor house" bias by pushing lyrical coverage (Wolcott's winter, the pretty towns), and he judged his own landmark by this rule and found it short: the book's edit, in his telling, failed the contrast test, the downbeat photos illustrating the downbeat text, the positive pole missing. His companion line is the craft version of the ethics: "It's much easier to take an interesting picture of the dead cat on the ash can, than a picture of a live cat asleep." The hard picture is the good one.

What it IS, watchably: f02 against the book's edit. The Easter-parade young man in the suit, the fedora, the boutonniere, meeting the lens: dignified, ordinary, unremarkable - the exact frame the book's cut kept out. f20, the jump-rope laugh, is the same pole. Rosskam's own Chicago frames are quiet and direct, people where they are, frontal, eye-level, no tilted angles, "not taken to be dramatic as such." The Flashbak set is the positive pole exhibited as the prosecution's missing evidence: this is what the book could have been, and its absence is the argument about the edit. The good was shot; the good was not used.

Against the chain: Parks (#296) shot Jim Crow in Kodachrome as evidence, the indictment in color; Rosskam wanted the defense exhibit in the same file, the joy as the certificate of the indictment's honesty. Lange (#293) kept the seeing for herself; Rosskam demanded the seeing include the live cat asleep. DeCarava (#297) photographed tenderness from inside the home; Rosskam demanded tenderness be admitted as evidence, not just felt. Collins (#298) named the worker and let her pose in dignity; Rosskam made dignity a filing requirement. Nobody else in the chain treated the happy frame as the load-bearing one.

Takeaway: shoot the good so the bad counts: the joyful frame is not the softening, it is the certificate, and the file that lacks it cannot be trusted about the rest.

M5. The coverage is the object: the thousand, not the one

What it is: Rosskam's theory of the file, stated flatly: "The meaning of [a coverage] was in the total thousand. Not in any one, or two, or six, that was selected. And the only way you'll ever see that is to go there and go through them." A photographer's FSA output ran to thousands of frames with about a thousand kept, and the thousand was the work: "this is the only way photography can be kept honest." The mechanism inverts the whole pipeline's instincts: the great picture is the lie the file tells about itself (six selected frames make the FSA look like the gigantic poor house), and the truth is the accumulation. The panorama effect is the proof: flipping through a full coverage set, Vermont farmhouse texture against West Virginia farmhouse texture, the region emerges from the pile, not the print. The file was shot "for the File," not for the five pictures an editor could pull for a story. The editor who selected sixes for a living is the one who said the sixes were not the work.

What it IS, watchably: the f17-f20 run as a sequence, not four pictures. The newsboy gaze, the milk-crate paper, the box-office queue, the jump rope: singly they are nothing, four pleasant street frames; together, flipped through, they are a neighborhood at a Saturday, the absorption and the ordinariness accumulating into a place. That is the thousand at miniature scale. The File is the object; the book is the excerpt; the study's own 20-frame inspection is the method, honestly scaled down. f08, the Truro bedroom inventory - the mantelpiece of kitsch, the whiskey glass, the wallpaper - is the same mechanism inside one frame: the meaning is in the total of the objects, not any one.

Against the chain: Ray-Jones (#283) mined the contact sheet for the single telling frame; Rosskam said the contact sheet was the work and the single frame was the lie. Winogrand (#278) let the mountain sort the spray; Rosskam kept the whole mountain and said the sorting was not the point. Maier (#290) never developed and let the world do it; Rosskam developed everything and said the developing was the argument. Evans (#284) deleted the photographer; Rosskam kept the thousand and deleted the six. Nobody else in the chain told the editor's trade that its product was not the product.

Takeaway: build the coverage as the object: the thousand kept frames are the work, the selected six are the excerpt, and the great picture is the lie the file tells about itself.

M6. The scale of display: editing at building size

What it is: editorial composition applied to an 11,000-square-foot wall. The Grand Central Defense Bond photomural, planned and assembled late 1941 under Stryker's rehire, then the world's largest photo mural, installed December 1941 (completed December 14, one week after Pearl Harbor, the word "WAR" pasted over "DEFENSE"): FSA frames plus a battleship copy-negative from Time, in three sections - Agriculture, Industry, Family - composited via glass negatives in a special-built lab, printed in 48-inch-wide strips, mounted through the station's doors, re-tensioned daily with wing screws against the humidity. The mechanism is the book's layout logic at the scale of architecture: selection, sequence, contrast, sectioning, the reader walking through the page instead of turning it. The three sections are M4 at building size: the good and the bad, the field and the factory and the family, the positive pole holding the argument together across a train station wall.

What it IS, watchably: f11. Two installers in bosun's chairs, small as insects, adjusting a giant panel of children's faces. The human body against the print is the scale made visible: the edit is no longer a decision, it is a construction site. The children's faces at that size stop being photographs and become citizens, the station's commuters walking under and between them. This is what "photo-direction" means when the page is a wall: the sequence is walked, the sections are entered, the contrast is stood inside.

Against the chain: Evans (#284) squared the camera; Rosskam squared the station. The mural is the photo-book with the binding removed and the pages hung on a wall, which makes it the ancestor of every gallery hang in the chain's afterlife. Delano (#299) composed the engine as the monument; Rosskam composed the monument out of other people's engines, fields, and faces. Steichen's Family of Man (1955) is the famous version; Rosskam proposed the same idea for the whole FSA file earlier, and built it, once, in a train station, in wartime, with wing screws. Nobody else in the chain edited at the scale of a building.

Takeaway: edit at building size: let the selection become a wall, the sequence a walk, and the section a room, and the reader will stand inside the argument.

M7. The team byline: authorship dissolved into partnership

What it is: two photographers working as one unit - assignments shared, darkroom shared, prints stamped only "Rosskam" - so that decades later even the Library of Congress could not attribute individual frames, and Louise had to correct catalogers by email. Edwin: formal compositions, art training, the frontal inventory. Louise: quick people-moments, the Vermont series, the Puerto Rico campaign work. The mechanism is credit flowing to the unit: the archive misremembers the unit as one man, and the misremembering is the work's condition, not its accident. Stryker's verdict: "they worked beautifully together." The cost is measurable: Louise's zoot-suit young man made the first printing of "12 Million Black Voices" and was dropped from later reprints when her negative was lost - her frame, his credit line, the archive's loss. About thirty of "Towboat River's" photographs were only identified as hers fifty years later. The study's own f16 - "Oldest woman in Lincoln, Vermont," the handwritten "19426-E" at the frame edge - is the attribution frame: proof of her independent documentary eye, surviving inside his stamped file.

What it IS, watchably: f01. Edwin in round glasses holding the big press camera, Louise with the Rolleiflex on a strap, seated together on a Puerto Rican seawall, photographed by Charles E. Rotkin. Two cameras, one wall, one unit. The portrait is the byline made visible: the team as the author, the instruments as the identity. f16 is the same unit's other half: her frame, her eye, his stamp. Read together they are the whole mechanism: the partnership produces the work, and the signature hides half the partnership.

Against the chain: Irene Delano (#299) held the flash, wrote the captions, and got the credit line "editing and criticizing my work"; Louise Rosskam held the Rolleiflex and got the stamp "Rosskam." Maier (#290) never showed the practice; the Rosskams showed it as one practice with one name. Mark (#292) stayed thirty-two years and the byline stayed hers; the Rosskams stayed a lifetime and the byline stayed his. Arbus (#280) induced the gaze alone; the Rosskams divided the labor and merged the name. Nobody else in the chain made the signature itself the mechanism.

Takeaway: sign the team, not the hand: let the credit flow to the unit, and let the archive's misremembering be the record of how the work was actually made.

Evolution

The painter's eye, 1903-1936: Munich to Philadelphia, Haverford to PAFA, the Carnegie International, the Paris one-man show, the surrealist ferment, Man Ray's teaching, the Polynesian "arty photography" that Louise said "wasn't documentary in any way." The picture-page laboratory, 1936-38: the Philadelphia Record's "Camera Tales of the Town," weekly and fast, words beside pictures; the Puerto Rico Life assignment, killed by the governor. The editor years, 1938-43: the Alliance city books (San Francisco with Saroyan, Washington with Eleanor Roosevelt, the Anderson "Home Town," the LaFarge "As Long as the Grass Shall Grow"), the FSA editorship at Stryker's right hand, the shooting scripts, the "75 Years of Negro Progress" exhibit, the Viking pitch and the Wright book, the Grand Central mural at wartime. The photographer years, 1943-53: Standard Oil as a shooter, not an editor, by his own insistence ("I had no ambition whatever to be anybody's editor"); the towboats, the 90 hours of wire-recorded river voices, "Towboat River"; Puerto Rico, the Tugwell file, the anthropologists he insisted on bringing in, DIVEDCO's million-dollar-order education machine, the novel. The return, 1953-85: Roosevelt, New Jersey, painting again, the local documentary, the books written not photographed. The loop closed: the man who quit painting because he was not a painter went back to it, having spent thirty years proving that his real medium was other people's pictures - selected, sequenced, directed, and, at the end, simply kept.

The aesthetic logic: the file as the autobiography

Rosskam's whole life is one long answer to the question his own doctrine asks: if photography is a matter of selection, what does the selector select for? The painter who quit painting found that his eye belonged not to the canvas but to the contact sheet - not the single decisive frame but the thousand kept ones. "We had this crazy idea that we could change the course of history," he said of the FSA years, and the craziness was literal: the belief that choosing which pictures lived was a form of making history. Every mechanism serves it: the cut (M1) is the authorship, the contrapuntal page (M2) is the reading contract, the script (M3) is the authorship before the shutter, the positive pole (M4) is the honesty condition, the thousand (M5) is the work itself, the mural (M6) is the edit at the scale of the public, and the team byline (M7) is the admission that the author was never one person. The signature move is the refusal: he refused the punched negative, the redundant page, the poverty-only file, the single great picture, the solo credit. Each refusal is a selection, and the selections add up to the man. That is why there is no English Wikipedia page and why it does not matter: the File is his entry, and the File is enormous.

Network (evidenced, with direction labels)

  • Roy Stryker: one-way, boss, patron, "father." Hired Rosskam as FSA editor in 1939 at a salary "second only to his own"; rehired him late 1941 for the mural; brought him to Standard Oil in 1943. Rosskam's verdict: "this whole project is unexplainable except in terms of Roy... Roy was a magnificent sponge for ideas... I don't think anybody except Roy could have done it." He added, humbly, that Stryker's taste was formed by the people he fell in with: "chiefly Ben Shahn... And then there was a woman... Ernestine Evans... an editor, and she helped form his approach and his taste... humbly, I think I did" too.
  • Richard Wright: two-way, the book. The first Black person the Rosskams "got to know well." Three weeks in Bronzeville, spring 1941; the undertakers, the gangsters, the cut gangster in the File. "I was the pupil, he the teacher" (to Leonard Lyons, September 26, 1941). The book's edit disappointed Rosskam on his own contrapuntal terms.
  • Jack Delano (#299): two-way, mentee. The Philadelphia friend (Delano's memory) he had never met (his own memory); both agree he lobbied Stryker for the hire. The godfather of the first assignment, the recruiter to Puerto Rico, the successor on the Tugwell file. "My first mentor really, the guy who looked after me, was Ed Rosskam."
  • Irene Delano: lateral, the other recruiter's recruit. Painter; designed publications for Stryker at Standard Oil; followed Jack to Puerto Rico at Rosskam's recruitment; stayed fifty years.
  • Louise Rosskam (nee Rosenbaum, 1910-2003): two-way, wife, partner, the unit. The Record page, the Alliance darkroom, Standard Oil, Puerto Rico, "Towboat River" co-author; prints stamped "Rosskam," attribution corrected by email decades later. Stryker: "they worked beautifully together." Her Vermont series and the f16 frame stand as her independent work inside his stamped file.
  • Marion Post Wolcott (#294): lateral, the script's recipient. The twenty-page winter script; her winter coverage in "Home Town"; her seconding of Delano's hire; Rosskam rated her best at making "positive" subjects interesting.
  • Ben Shahn: lateral, the painter-photographer. Shared Stryker's ear; Philadelphia friend; Roosevelt, New Jersey neighbor; invited the Rosskams to Roosevelt. Louise's rule: you can "always pick out the painters-photographers and the non-painters-photographers."
  • Paul Vanderbilt and Romana Javitz: lateral, the file-system people. Vanderbilt brought the filing system to the FSA file; Javitz ran the NYPL 42nd Street picture collection. Rosskam's admiration: "Her notion of what belongs together and why is fabulous" - and his filing koan: "a picture can be at the same time an evening, a landscape, a child in the grass, and how are you going to classify it?"
  • Rexford Tugwell: one-way, the originator. "It was Tugwell that brought this about" - first the Resettlement Administration's photographic project, later the governor of Puerto Rico who asked Stryker "Who have you got who can do this same job for us here?" and got Rosskam.
  • Luis Munoz Marin: two-way, the governor. Friend ("we both liked brandy"); commissioned the DIVEDCO expansion; Rosskam ghost-wrote his unpublished autobiography; Louise was his 1948 campaign photographer.
  • Ernestine Evans (1889-1967): one-way, the editor's editor. Rosskam's own tip to Doud: "She was an editor, and she helped form [Stryker's] approach and his taste." Almost invisible in the canon; a future study's subject.
  • Edward Steichen: one-way, the admired model. Rosskam proposed a Family-of-Man-style publication for the whole FSA file, years before 1955; the Grand Central mural was his built version.
  • Ansel Adams: lateral, the expositor. Directed the 1940 Pageant of Photography in San Francisco, for which Rosskam organized the small-town exhibit.
  • Dorothea Lange, Walker Evans, Russell Lee, John Vachon, Gordon Parks: the photographers whose work he selected and used; he recognized Lange's Migrant Mother "by the number" from pulling it for requesters so often; he borrowed Lee from FSA for the Bronzeville weeks.
  • The Atlantic's Amarillo exhibit: the objector. A Texas official complained about Rosskam's Negro farmer on panel 4 of the small-town exhibit - the edit as a political act, on the record.

Study-method note: this study was built against the run's own brief where the brief was wrong, per the existence-checks rule, and the corrections are load-bearing. The brief's "Wolcott and Rosskam put in the word" is confirmed but reframed: both sides agree Rosskam lobbied Stryker, but Delano remembers old Philadelphia friends and parallel advocacy (Wolcott "began working on Roy" too), while Rosskam's own 1965 account says he had never laid eyes on Delano and hired him on the pictures; the study carries both. The brief's implied Viking initiation is reversed by Rosskam's own testimony: he conceived and pitched the Negro photo-book to Viking after the Sherwood Anderson book, "although I had at the time no knowledge that I would get a hold of Richard Wright"; the Wikipedia page's "Viking approached Wright" framing is flagged as contested. The brief's 1942 date is corrected: "12 Million Black Voices" was published October 1941. The brief's WIPR co-founding claim is dropped: no source found tying it to Rosskam and the Delanos; DIVEDCO is the verified Puerto Rico story. The only verified "cut" from the book is the photographed gangster, "in the File, I guess." The f10 and f12 caption-vs-image mismatches are carried as logged: captions editorializing poverty where images show tenderness or family. The brief said nothing about Louise, and the study found she is half of it: the team byline is load-bearing, not decorative. Where sources split on minors (Alliance book dates, mural completion details, the million-dollar-order's final figure around $700,000), the study attributes rather than asserts. Two papers most directly about his editing (Saretzky's "Documenting Diversity" and the 2004 chapter) were cited but not read; they are the first honest gap for any follow-up.
