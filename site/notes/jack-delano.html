Jack Delano

Study date: 2026-11-03. ig-artist-study #299.

Site: Jack Delano has no personal Instagram presence: verified absence, not a gap. He died in August 1997 in San Juan, Puerto Rico, and the living archive is the federal one: his 1940-43 negatives and color transparencies in the Farm Security Administration / Office of War Information Collection at the Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division, public domain as United States government work, with the railroad Kodachromes reintroduced to public view by the 2004 book and the 2005-2006 LOC exhibition "Bound for Glory: America in Color" and by John Gruber's 2014-2015 Chicago History Museum show "Railroaders: Jack Delano's Homefront Photography" (which located 48 of the 49 portrait subjects' families, through census rolls, directories, and cold calls). Identity anchors: born August 1, 1914 in Voroshylivka, Podolia Governorate, Russian Empire (now Ukraine), to a Jewish family; the name at birth is recorded as Yakov Ovcharov (Wikipedia) and as Jacob Ovcharov (Arthur H. Bleich's catalog essay, drawing on Delano's autobiography; Rangefinder), carried unsettled with sources rather than resolved; the family reached New York aboard the SS Homeric on July 5, 1923 and settled in Philadelphia; scholarship student at the Settlement Music School (graphic arts, photography, viola, composition) and at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts (illustration, with the 1935 Cresson Traveling Scholarship to Europe, where he bought a small camera and photography began). The name became Delano officially in 1940 (Bleich: at a friend's suggestion, "a more American last name"; single-source, carried as attributed). Pronounced "de-LAY-no" (Bleich). In 1937 a soap advertisement, his first commercial assignment, was rejected, and he decided commercial photography was wrong for him; he wanted, in Rangefinder's telling, "to capture the lives of ordinary people as Van Gogh had done in his paintings of Dutch peasants." The Federal Art Project approved his coal-mining study proposal in 1938; he lived for a month with a family in Pottsville, Pennsylvania, shot several hundred negatives in Schuylkill County anthracite country, hung a Philadelphia museum show, and bound two handmade spiral books of original prints; Paul Strand saw the show, was impressed, and promised a recommendation. In 1940 Strand's urging reached Roy Stryker at the moment Arthur Rothstein left the FSA for Look, and Stryker offered Delano the job at $2,300 a year (Delano's own 1965 oral-history figure); Edwin Rosskam and Marion Post Wolcott "put in a good word." He married Irene Esser shortly after starting (the oral history: Stryker denied him a day off for the wedding). Then the assignments: the Eastern-seaboard migrant trail from Florida to Aroostook County, Maine; six months in New England, every state, mills and foundries and fishing ports; three months in Greene County, Georgia with the sociologist Arthur Raper and his family; the Durham, North Carolina bus station in May 1940; the Virgin Islands and Puerto Rico in late 1941 (the Pearl Harbor broadcast heard on a San Juan hotel radio; three weeks in the islands, three months on the main island, county agents as guides); Chicago in 1942; and the railroad assignment, November 1942 through May 1943, not Chicago-only but Chicago yards plus Clinton, Iowa, Albuquerque, Topeka, and Arizona/Nevada freight runs, some 2,500 to 3,000 images with 500-plus Kodachromes, shot with credentials from the FBI, the Association of American Railroads, the Western Defense Command, and the War Department's Chief of Transportation, and with the engineer's consent to stop trains for photographs. In 1943 the FSA folded into the OWI and Delano was inducted; Irene's 1965 account has him at Wright Field in Dayton doing photography for the Air Service Command public-relations office, then transferred to the Air Transport Command in Washington at Per Lorentz's request, with trips to the Pacific. In 1946 the Delanos settled permanently in Puerto Rico, and photography became one medium among many: films for DIVEDCO, the Division of Community Education (Delano composing many of the scores), direction of "Los Peloteros" (1953), the poor rural kids and baseball picture that became a classic of Puerto Rican cinema; the Cinema and Graphics Unit of the Commission of Parks and Recreation, built with Irene and Edwin and Louise Rosskam at the governor's request; the co-founding of WIPR, the island's first publicly funded educational television station, in 1957; orchestral works for the Puerto Rico Symphony Orchestra, ballets, chamber music, choral pieces including "Pétalo de rosa" (1993, San Juan Children's Choir), vocal settings of Puerto Rican poetry especially Tomás Blanco; children's books illustrated with Irene; "Puerto Rico Mio: Four Decades of Change" (1990), the 1940s negatives revisited forty years later; the autobiography "Photographic Memories" (Smithsonian Institution Press, 1997), whose final line is the program: "my lifelong concern for the common people."

Doorway: Hop from the Collins study (#298): Collins took Stryker's assignment as her material and worked the brief's own words against it, shooting the shift unposed, letting the caption carry the seamy negative, pointing the Kodachrome at the routine and the grocery ramp. Delano took the opposite seat at the same payroll and the opposite license with the same tools: where Collins refused the pose, Delano insisted on it, "no compunction about moving people around," directing the sitter to the essence; where Collins named the worker and let her pose in dignity, Delano filled the whole frame with the flash-lit face and let scale do the naming; where Collins pointed color at the ordinary as propaganda that life continues, Delano pointed color at the roundhouse at night on ASA-10 film with long exposures, gambling the rare stock on impossible light; where Collins filed herself quietly among the merchandise at a public sale, Delano stood on the cowcatcher of locomotive 3210 with the 4x5 Graphic in his hand. The pair is the payroll's two Kodachrome answers to the same camera: the ordinary as it is (Collins), the ordinary as it should be felt (Delano). M1. The heroic-scale portrait: the face as the whole frame, flash-lit out of the dark

What it is: the mechanism Hostetler states as doctrine: "Delano's photographs elevate the ordinary individual to heroic status. In the 1940s he often played with scale to underline the strength of character in his subjects, and he enlarged some of his prints beyond the usual proportions to dramatize the subjects' presence." Wikipedia attributes to him the "unconventional use of scale and proportion." The roundhouse portraits are the mechanism at full power: direct flash (or work light) in a dark interior, the worker's head filling the frame, the industrial black pressing in from every side. The Kodachrome transparency is the print; the slide-mount edges visible in the scans are part of the artifact.

What it IS, watchably: f15. Roy Nelin, box packer, Proviso yard: the extreme close-up head shot against a black void, flash-lit, the slight smile, the gaze off-camera, the mount edges framing the frame. f16. William London, 25 years on the railroad: three-quarter portrait, cap with goggles pushed up, cigarette, the face and coveralls grease-dark, the flash carving him out of the machinery dark. In both, the face emerges from black as the single lit mass, and the worker holds while the dark holds back.

Against the chain: Bubley (#295) fired the flash inches from the face to take the census; Delano fires it to raise the monument. DeCarava (#297) refused the flash entirely and printed the dark as pigment; Delano carries the flash into the same dark and aims it at the worker. Parks (#296) researched the sitter and shot where they lived and worked; Delano's portrait needs no dossier, only the face and the dark. Collins (#298) named the worker and let her pose while the caption did half the portraiture; Delano lets the scale do the naming, no caption required.

Takeaway: put the worker's flash-lit face at the scale of a monument and let the dark do the pedestal work: the elevation is in the proportion, not the praise.

M2. The directed essence: posing as documentary truth

What it is: Delano's stated credo, in the 1965 oral history, and the most load-bearing sentence in the whole run: "I never felt any compunction about moving people around and posing them in these documentary photographs... a documentary photograph is an expression of the essence of what you are seeing." He directed for warmth, never for the stolen candid: in September 1940 at Windsor Locks, Connecticut, he told the tobacco farmer Andrew Lyman his pants were falling; Lyman grabbed them and his wife threw her head back laughing, and Delano got the shot Stryker used endlessly and Steichen later chose for "The Family of Man" (1955). In the Georgia church, Irene held the extension flash at the altar while the congregation stared coldly, "Flash! Flash! Flash!" over Irene's protests, three days of silent treatment at home, vindicated when the pictures ran in two FSA book projects. The direction is always in service of the sitter's dignity, never the photographer's punch; Irene's companion line is the ethics: "we tried not to get in people's way... we were tremendously sympathetic with their problem."

What it IS, watchably: f12. The Queen of Wellstown: the young woman in the tinfoil crown and white eyelet dress, smiling down at the lens, a willing and playful sitter, the portrait a collaboration with a visible push behind it. f06. The wagon trio in their Saturday best, coral and magenta against the Kodachrome blue, caught in the finery they chose. f09. The sugar-cane worker and his woman, frontal, hard sun, both staring straight into the lens: the directness is the direction's receipt.

Against the chain: HCB (#286) hid the camera, drew the geometry, and trapped the instant without ever touching it; Delano declares the camera and moves the people, the exact opposite discipline with the identical documentary claim. Arbus (#280) asked permission and induced the gaze; Delano asks permission and issues the instruction. Maier (#290) never showed the practice; Delano's practice is the showing, the direction done in the open. Collins (#298) took the unposed shift as the mechanism and let the complaint it drew be the proof; Delano takes the posed frame and lets the laugh it drew be the proof.

Takeaway: direct the sitter openly toward the essence and let the picture admit the direction: the unguarded laugh at the moment of instruction is the truest frame.

M3. The machine as monument: scale inversion, the worker small in its doorway

What it is: Delano composes the locomotive as architecture and the worker as a figure in its doorway. The engine takes two-thirds of the frame or more; the human is centered but dwarfed, and the dignity comes from standing next to the giant, not from filling the frame. It is the heroic-scale portrait's obverse: M1 enlarges the worker, M3 enlarges the world and lets the worker's smallness read as belonging. He had the authority for it: credentials from the FBI, the Association of American Railroads, the Western Defense Command, and the War Department's Chief of Transportation, and the engineer's consent to stop trains for photographs (Bleich, quoting the autobiography).

What it IS, watchably: f17. The two women wipers in red bandanas and denim coveralls climbing the steps of the 4-8-4 Northern at Clinton, oil cans in hand, the boiler flank, pipes, and rivets towering over them. f19. Burton and Edwards small in the snow beside the steaming AT&SF locomotive at Corwith, comparing pocket watches, the engine breathing over them. f20. Delano himself, hat and jacket, the 4x5 Graphic in his hand, standing on the cowcatcher of 3210 with one hand on the rail: he performs the same composition on his own body, the maker filed as one more figure in the machine's doorway. f13. The roundhouse with no people at all: the locomotives alone as the monument, the forge glowing orange at the right, the window shafts in the smoke at the left.

Against the chain: Evans (#284) squared the camera and deleted the photographer, architecture with the people edited out; Delano keeps the people small inside the architecture and the dignity comes from standing beside it. Brassaï (#285) declared the camera and was admitted to the closed room; Delano arrived with four agencies' credentials and the engineer's consent to stop the train, then composed the admission as a monument. Collins (#298) carried the flash into the plant and let the workers arrange themselves; Delano arranges the workers against the engine and lets the engine do the arranging of the frame.

Takeaway: compose the machine as the monument and the worker as the figure in its doorway: the smallness is the dignity, and the engine does the framing.

M4. Light as the subject: the ASA-10 gamble

What it is: Bleich's verdict on the technical nerve: "The fact that Delano would even attempt to shoot Kodachrome in the dingy interiors of repair shops, roundhouses, and stations or even at night in the yards, shows that he was an experimental camera artist." Rangefinder: with ASA 8/10 film he was "fearless," attempting "seemingly impossible shots with long exposures." He shot 500-plus Kodachromes on the railroad assignment alone. The mechanism converts the film's weakness, its slowness, into the picture's subject: with exposures long enough to need a tripod in a working roundhouse, time itself becomes visible, and the light moving through dust and steam is the event.

What it IS, watchably: f14. Chicago Union Station, January 1943: the skylight shafts over the waiting room are the entire picture; the two uniformed men in silhouette in the foreground are armatures for the light, and the seated passengers sit inside it. f13. The roundhouse: the window shafts cutting through the smoke at the left, the orange forge glow pulsing at the right, engine 2070 at rest between them, the long exposure holding what the eye could not.

Against the chain: DeCarava (#297) printed the available dark as pigment in black and white; Delano shoots the same dark in Kodachrome at ASA 10 on long exposures, the opposite technical gamble in the same room. Wolcott (#294) ran the unit's color experiment on the rural South; Delano takes the color experiment into the roundhouse at night. Meyerowitz (#277) waited for the light to come right; Delano waits with the shutter open and lets the slow film gather it.

Takeaway: shoot the slow film in the dark interior on the long exposure and let the light be the event: the dust in the shaft is the picture, and the waiting is visible.

M5. The empty room speaks: absence as portrait

What it is: the Smithsonian exhibition text marks his "carefully composed interiors" as a distinct body, and the Puerto Rico work keeps returning to the room as the sitter. Delano photographs interiors with no people and lets the objects testify: the poster on the wall, the chair, the switch plate, the framed print. The mechanism is the inventory: the eye walks the wall and assembles the absent occupant from what they left and what was left for them.

What it IS, watchably: f08. The San Juan hotel room, December 1941: the mint-green plank wall, the "MALARIA EN PUERTO RICO / Departamento de Sanidad" poster with its island map, mosquito, and cinchona tree, the framed parlor prints, the white caned chair, nobody home. Public health, colonial administration, and domesticity in one wall. f03. The Brown and Sharpe facade at quitting time, Providence, December 1940: the long brick face as a portrait of the firm, the streaming employees as punctuation, the "Office" entrance doing the titling.

Against the chain: Atget (#287) outwaited the street and photographed the empty city; Delano photographs the empty room and lets the poster on its wall do the testifying. Evans (#284) deleted the photographer; Delano deletes the people and lets the wall talk. Nobody else in the chain made a public-health poster the sitter's face.

Takeaway: photograph the room with nobody in it and let the wall testify: the inventory of objects is the portrait of the absent.

M6. The cultural pattern: a region through its rituals, not its faces

What it is: Hostetler's distinction, and the one that separates him from the rest of Stryker's payroll: "Delano documents a region through not only its people and landscape, but also its cultural and social patterns; this attitude differentiated his work from that of most other FSA photographers." Wikipedia's version: "he generally focused more on cultural and social patterns of regions rather than focusing solely on the people and landscape." In practice it was a method of staying: in Connecticut, within a few miles, he shot the Colchester Jewish dairy farmers at dawn with the cows and the synagogue, a Polish community that spoke no English, and a Scotch-Irish Thanksgiving (1965 oral history); in Greene County he shot the church service, the funeral, the jail, the newspaper editor, and the sheriff, the whole social structure learned through Raper's introductions. The ritual unfolds at its own pace; the camera stays for the whole ceremony, not the decisive instant. The pattern is the portrait.

What it IS, watchably: f05. The Georgia funeral: the crowd around the fresh grave mound, the man with his back to the camera in the foreground, communal and quiet, the ritual doing all the composing. f06. The Saturday wagon to town: the weekly ritual of leisure as the subject, the dresses chosen for it. f12. The Wellstown crowning: a Black Chicago social ritual, the tinfoil crown treated with full seriousness. f10. The Christiansted street: the basket-on-the-head economy, bougainvillea, palm shadows, the small centered figure inside the graphic noon light.

Against the chain: Collins (#298) assembled the hyphenated Americans in their own place and let the belonging argue; Delano stays for the whole ceremony and lets the ritual argue. Parks (#296) made the return the unit across decades, the dossier accruing time; Delano makes the custom the unit inside one assignment. Mark (#292) stayed thirty-two years to earn the inside; Delano stays the length of the ritual and earns it that afternoon.

Takeaway: document the region through its rituals, not its faces: stay for the whole ceremony, and let the pattern be the portrait.

Evolution

The apprentice eye, 1924-1937: the Settlement Music School scholarship (graphic arts, photography, viola, composition) and PAFA illustration, the European camera, the Van Gogh aspiration stated plainly: ordinary people as the Dutch peasants. The coal-town proof, 1938-1940: the Federal Art Project proposal, the month living with the Pottsville family, the several hundred negatives, the museum show, the two handmade books, Strand's validation. The method is set before Stryker ever sees him: live with the subjects, shoot deep, print big. The FSA years, 1940-1943: the migrant trail, the six New England months, the three Greene County months with Raper (and the church service shot knowing it was for Wright's book), Durham, the Caribbean stranding, Chicago, and the railroad apotheosis, November 1942 to May 1943. Black-and-white documentarian early; Kodachrome from 1941; fearless with it by the roundhouse winter of 1942-43, 500-plus color frames of machines, faces, and light. The war service, 1943-1946: the camera keeps working and the subject becomes the military machine. The Puerto Rican lifework, 1946-1997: photography becomes one medium among many, film, score, television, illustration, teaching. "Puerto Rico Mio" (1990) closes the circle, the 1940s sites revisited forty years later. "Photographic Memories" (1997) is the final statement. The through-line is not a style but a conversion: the payroll photographer becomes the island's maker of images, sounds, and broadcasts, and the concern stays fixed the whole way: the common people, at heroic scale.

The aesthetic logic: the essence, directed; the musician's photograph

The Settlement Music School scholarship is the Rosetta stone, and it runs under every mechanism. Delano approached a roundhouse the way a composer approaches a hall: the heroic-scale portrait is the fortissimo, the single lit mass against the dark; the ASA-10 long exposure is the sustained note, time made audible in dust and steam; the empty room is the rest, the silence that makes the wall's testimony legible; the directed laugh is the downbeat, the instruction that lands the phrase. That is why the credo works and why it is not a license for manipulation: a conductor moves the players to serve the music, and Delano moved the sitters to serve their own essence. Irene's ethics line is the other half of the score: "we tried not to get in people's way... we were tremendously sympathetic with their problem." Steichen's embrace is the contemporary receipt: "you're the artist in the group!" Every mechanism serves the same program, the one in the autobiography's last line: "my lifelong concern for the common people." The camera is never hidden, the direction is never denied, the film is never fast enough, and the picture is honest about all three. That is the signature: the document that admits it was made, and is truer for the admission.

Network (evidenced, with direction labels)

  • Roy Stryker: one-way, employer and mentor. The 1940 telegram job offer at $2,300 a year (car and license as conditions); the shooting scripts that "stimulate, never dictate" (1965 oral history); the holes punched in negatives he disliked (Rangefinder). The license and the limit in one man.
  • Paul Strand: one-way, champion. Saw the 1938 Philadelphia coal show, was impressed, promised the recommendation, and urged Stryker to hire Delano when Rothstein left for Look; on his deathbed, "I'm responsible for you, you know" (Rangefinder). Delano's own placement: "I respect people like Paul Strand... that kind of photography is one thing and my kind of photography is another" (1965 oral history).
  • Edwin Rosskam: lateral. The Philadelphia friend who "put in a good word for me with Roy"; the godfather of the first Virginia assignment; the photo editor who selected Delano's Greene County work for "12 Million Black Voices" (1941); decades later the co-founder with the Delanos of Puerto Rico's Cinema and Graphics Unit.
  • Marion Post Wolcott (#294): lateral. With Rosskam she "began working on Roy" to hire him (oral history); the fellow government Kodachrome shooter, the color experiment's other pair of hands.
  • Irene Esser Delano: two-way. Held the extension flash at the Georgia altar, loaded the film holders, wrote the captions, kept the exposure records, edited and criticized ("best of all, editing and criticizing my work," Jack, 1965); "having a woman along helped" smooth the access; later ran Stryker's Standard Oil publications (the Photo Memo magazine), co-made the DIVEDCO films, illustrated the children's books. The collaborator the archive hides.
  • Ben Shahn: lateral, admired. Irene's second cousin (stated by Jack himself, 1965); Delano claimed kinship with Shahn's camera-as-sketchbook approach and, "having studied painting myself," with Evans's studied composition both: "I had sympathy with both."
  • Walker Evans (#284): one-way, influence. Admired for "seeing beauty in the sordid and composing it as art, rather than merely a document" (1965 oral history); the NYT obituary's secondhand verdict: "Certain of his beautifully detailed, crisp black-and-white prints show the Evans influence."
  • Arthur Rothstein: lateral. The man whose Look-magazine departure opened the Stryker slot Delano filled; the Kodachrome circle's other early hand.
  • Henri Cartier-Bresson and Robert Capa: one-way, influence. "We were being influenced by what we had seen of Cartier Bresson and Robert Capa... we admired their work and were glad that there was this kind of kinship in approach" (1965 oral history).
  • Richard Wright: one-way-out, lateral. Delano shot the Greene County Black church service knowing it was for Wright's book; the photographs ran in "12 Million Black Voices" (Viking, October 1941) under Rosskam's photo direction.
  • Edward Steichen: one-way, embrace and selection. "Oh well, you're the artist in the group!" (1965 oral history); then the selector's receipt, the Lyman photograph chosen for "The Family of Man" (1955, MoMA).
  • Arthur Raper: lateral, collaborator. The sociologist whose three Greene County months with his family gave the Delanos the whole community: the jail, the newspaper editor, the mayor, the sheriff; his books and their negatives drew on the same material.
  • Per Lorentz: lateral. Requested Delano's transfer to the Air Transport Command in Washington (Irene, 1965); the FSA's filmmaker, the film side of the same payroll.
  • Gordon Parks (#296), John Vachon, Russell Lee, Alfred Palmer: Kodachrome-circle laterals, the unit's color shooters in the "Bound for Glory" cohort, staff overlap confirmed in the oral history.
  • John Gruber: one-way, archivist and interpreter. The Center for Railroad Photography and Art's "Railroaders" (2014) spent the years locating the portrait subjects' families; the 25-year reading of the railroad work.
  • Pablo Delano: one-way-out. The son (born 1954, San Juan), photographer, who shot the Chicago descendants for the 2014 exhibition; the lifework's second generation.

Study-method note: this study was built against the run's own brief where the brief was wrong, per the existence-checks rule, and the corrections are load-bearing. The brief's "railroad photographs, Chicago" understates the assignment: November 1942 through May 1943, Chicago yards plus Clinton (Iowa), Albuquerque, Topeka, and Arizona/Nevada freight runs, 2,500 to 3,000 images, 500-plus Kodachromes (Gruber; Bleich). The brief's FSA-hire question is answered by Delano himself in the 1965 oral history: 1940, $2,300 a year, effectively Rothstein's replacement, at Strand's urging, with Rosskam and Wolcott putting in the word. The brief's Kodachrome question is answered yes and centrally: Delano was shooting color on FSA assignments from 1941, and the roundhouse winter of 1942-43 is the program's most daring run. The Army record follows Irene's 1965 firsthand account (Wright Field, Dayton; then the Air Transport Command in Washington at Lorentz's request, with Pacific trips) over Wikipedia's "South Pacific 1943-46" summary; the "classified output" claim is unsourced and is not repeated. The death date is carried unsettled (August 12 vs 14, 1997; the study says August 1997) and so is the birth name (Yakov vs Jacob Ovcharov, both sourced). Two frame-level corrections: the Commons file named "Child selling Chicago Tribune" shows The Chicago Defender, and the image wins; f02's 8c-series number marks a color transparency, but the Commons derivative arrived grayscale, and the study claims no color for it. Where sources split on minors (Settlement Music School 1924-32 vs 1925-33; "Bound for Glory" exhibit end date; the WIPR year as 1957 per Wikipedia), the study attributes rather than asserts.
