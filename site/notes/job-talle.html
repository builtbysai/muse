Job Talle — koi ponds and living water

studied 2026-11-01/16 min read/3,228 words

Study date: 2026-11-01. Hans-directed (no ig-artist-study number). Subject: Job Talle — jobtalle.com, @jobtalle on X. Dutch indie game developer / simulation programmer. Known for: Koi Farm (koi breeding game, Steam Feb 2021, open source github.com/jobtalle/Koi), and a blog of unusually well-explained simulation write-ups, each with live interactive demos.

This study focuses on his koi pieces and water movement: how the fish steer, how the water ripples, and how the two systems talk to each other.

  1. Koi Farm (released 26 Feb 2021; Steam/itch.io/iOS/Android; 94% positive, ~1k Steam reviews;

nominated for the Cinekid game & app award). A koi breeding game: crossbreed procedurally generated koi, discover mutations, collect them as trading cards. Open-sourced 31 Mar 2021 (github.com/jobtalle/Koi, ~413 stars). Still maintained — latest commit Feb 2026 (Serbian translation). Per his X bio he is "Making a sequel."

The articles (read in full, with their interactive demos)

  2. "Convolutional textures" (7 Jan 2019) — GPGPU self-updating textures: Game of Life, falling

  1. "Swarm behaviour" (1 Feb 2018) — his Boids implementation after Craig Reynolds (1986):

repulsion/alignment/attraction zones, attraction angle (field of view), gravitation toward view center. Notes the O(n²) cost honestly.

  1. "Simulated ecosystems" (3 Dec 2018) — closed ecosystems with constant mass: hex grid,

agents (plants, rabbits, foxes) with mass-costed actions (move 10%, copy 10%, idle 1%...), eat-only-if-2x-heavier rule. Population graphs follow Lotka-Volterra cycles. Inspired by a real mason-jar ecosystem on his windowsill.

  1. "Neuroevolution in squids" (24 Apr 2020) — spiking neural networks evolved to swim;

tentacle segments with spring return; fitness = food eaten / body mass.

  1. "Simulating hydraulic erosion" (14 Jun 2020) — "snowball" droplet erosion (drops gain

sediment downhill, shed it when too big, erode at their previous position so they don't dig themselves in). Evolution

Talle's career reads as a technique pipeline that converges on Koi Farm:

  • 2017 — tools. "Cubic noise" gives him his own coherent-noise primitive; it later shows up

in the sand floor, wind gusts, and koi pattern generation. He builds primitives, not pieces.

  • 2018 — behavior. "Swarm behaviour" (Boids) becomes the koi steering core; "Simulated

ecosystems" gives him the closed-system, mass-conserving ecology mindset (plants → rabbits → foxes is the dry-land rehearsal for pond → koi → player).

  • 2019 — water. "Convolutional textures" is the breakthrough: the GPU ping-pong heightmap

that becomes Koi Farm's water and its wind. He publishes the technique two years before the game that depends on it.

  • 2020 — craft. "Hydraulic erosion" (with its shore-wave appendix) and "Neuroevolution in

squids" (procedural swimming) are the last two missing organs: believable shores and believable locomotion.

  • 2021 — synthesis. Koi Farm ships: Boids fish + convolutional water + weather state machine

+ genetic patterns, all in one pond. Open-sourced a month later.

  • 2022–2023 — deepening. "Digital koi breeding" documents the genetics; "Distance field

transparency" polishes rendering edges.

  • 2026 — maintenance + sequel. Still committing translations; X bio says a sequel is in

development.

The pattern: every article is a subsystem audition; Koi Farm is the cast that got hired. Nothing in the game was invented for the game — each system had already survived its own write-up and demo.

Network (all evidenced, no guesses)

  1. Craig Reynolds — cited by name in "Swarm behaviour": "One of the first algorithms for

flocking called Boids was made by Craig Reynolds in 1986. The simulation I have implemented mostly follows his design." The koi schooling in Koi Farm is a direct descendant.

  1. Hugo Elias — credited in "Convolutional textures": "I won't go into the method that

updates the heightmap, but I borrowed it from the website of Hugo Elias." The entire water simulation (and the waves.js shader I read) is the Elias discrete wave equation.

  1. Alan Wolfe (blog.demofox.org) — cited in the "Simulating hydraulic erosion" shore-wave

appendix: "This blog post explains the jump flooding algorithm which was used to generate the Voronoi diagram on the GPU." His shore-distance wave field comes from Wolfe's technique.

  1. Community collaborators — itch.io devlog, 21 Jun 2021: "Thanks to ItchyTanuki (who paints

lovely watercolors), a Mac build for koi farm is now available." Players contributed Japanese, Spanish, and Serbian translations. He builds in the open and absorbs contributions.

Mechanisms — what makes the koi and water feel alive

All of the following are read from his articles and/or source code (file + line context given). Nothing here is inferred from screenshots.

M1. The wake is a record of tail effort (fishBody.js: disturbWater)

Each frame, the body measures how far the tail vertebra (spine[length-2]) moved since the last frame. If that displacement exceeds SPEED_WAVE_THRESHOLD (.055), it paints a flare into the water heightmap at the tail's position with intensity = WAVE_INTENSITY_MIN (.05) + (tailSpeed − threshold) × WAVE_INTENSITY_MULTIPLIER (2), jittered by WAVE_TURBULENCE (.4). Radius .15. Watchable behavior: a gliding fish leaves almost no wake; a fish that flicks its tail hard throws a visible ring. The water remembers effort, not position. The same function also fires positional tail-splash audio (pan by x-position, volume by tail speed, different samples for big vs. small fish).

M2. Nibble ripples (fish.js: update)

When a fish's speed decays below SPEED_NIBBLE (.0155), a countdown (20–40 ticks) fires: the fish gets a small random turn (NIBBLE_TURN_FORCE .07) and paints a water flare (NIBBLE_RADIUS .1, NIBBLE_DISPLACEMENT .25) at its own position. Watchable behavior: slow fish periodically dip and release a small expanding ring — it reads as feeding. The pond is never still because the fish themselves keep plucking it.

M3. Turn contagion (fish.js: interact)

Fish pair up within RADIUS_ATTRACTION (1.5). recognizes(other) is true only when both fish share the same palette index — same-colored koi are the same "species" and school together; different colors ignore each other. Within the pair:

  • Speed matching (SPEED_CATCH_UP .0045): the slower fish accelerates toward the faster —

schools converge on a common pace.

  • Repulsion (FORCE_REPULSION .45, radius .6): proximity-weighted push, applied symmetrically.
  • Turn copying (TURN_FOLLOW_CHANCE .025): if one fish is mid-turn and its neighbor isn't,

the neighbor may copy the turn direction with TURN_CARRY (.95) of the force. Watchable behavior: one fish decides to wheel, and the turn ripples through the school like a rumor. This is the single most alive-looking thing in his fish code.

M4. Pulse-glide locomotion (fish.js: update)

Speed decays by SPEED_DECAY (.996) every tick toward SPEED_MIN (.015); a random BOOST_CHANCE (.003) triggers a tail-flick burst (BOOST_POWER .0015/tick, 5–30 ticks, capped at SPEED_MAX .078). Random turns fire with TURN_CHANCE .0016 — but .05 when raining (30× more restless in rain). Speed scales with body size: bigger fish cruise faster. Watchable behavior: fish don't swim at constant speed — they surge and coast, and the whole pond gets agitated when rain starts. Weather is a behavior parameter, not just a backdrop.

M5. Vertebra-chain spine (fishBody.js: update)

The body is a chain of vertebrae. The head is placed, then each vertebra follows the previous at fixed spacing, corrected by a spring term that pulls the chain toward the head's heading (springiness increases for small fish). The head's target angle oscillates: direction.angle() + π + cos(phase) × speedFactor × SWIM_AMPLITUDE (11) — wiggle amplitude grows with speed above SPEED_SWING_THRESHOLD (.01). The phase advances by SWIM_SPEED (6) × speed / size — large fish undulate slowly, small fish buzz. Watchable behavior: the whole body S-curves through turns; the tail lags the head exactly like a real fish. No keyframed swim cycle anywhere.

M6. Convolutional water (water.js, waves.js, "Convolutional textures")

The water is a GPU ping-pong texture at 1/17 scene scale. Each pixel: R = height, G = previous height (momentum), B unused. The fragment shader (Hugo Elias scheme): newHeight = (left + right + up + down) − 2.0 − momentum, then × DAMPING (.997), re-centered on 0.5. 16-bit channel precision (8-bit rounding would eat the subtle waves). NEAREST filtering during simulation, LINEAR only for display. Influences (fish tails, nibbles, rain, cursor) are painted by a separate influence-painter shader so alpha blending can't contaminate the momentum channel. Watchable behavior: ripples from different sources interfere and pass through each other; everything calms to glass via the .997 damping. One cheap texture does the whole pond.

M7. The pond compositor (ponds.js)

The final water look is a shader that:

  • derives surface normals from heightmap gradients (finite differences of the .gr channels,

time-interpolated),

  • refracts the pond floor: background sampled at uv − depth × normal.xz — the sand visibly

bends under ripples,

  • reflects: a reflections texture sampled at uv + height × normal.xz, weighted by

REFLECTIVITY (.15), with shore distance in its alpha,

  • draws shore-hugging wave bands: cos(shift − shoreDistance × 6.0) — foam lines that cling

to the pond edge and drift,

  • tints everything with a weather color filter (overcast cools the water, sunset warms it).

Watchable behavior: you see the floor wobble before you notice the surface — depth is faked by bending the sand, not by drawing water.

M8. Shore waves from a distance field ("Simulating hydraulic erosion", appendix)

A Voronoi diagram of the shoreline (jump flooding, GPU) gives every water pixel its distance and direction to the nearest shore. A sine wave's phase is set by the shore direction so crests travel toward the beach; the pattern is then stylized and broken into wavelets so each wave reads as its own entity. Watchable behavior: waves arrive at the shore instead of sliding past it — the edge of the water feels like a place, not a crop.

M9. Noise-slice pattern genetics ("Digital koi breeding")

Patterns come from slicing 3D cubic noise with a plane and thresholding: above threshold = spot. Mutation = nudging the plane a little through the same noise (small visual steps); crossbreeding = interpolating the parents' planes (offspring land between the parents, then mutate). Multiple pattern layers stack for 3+ colors; stripes/ridges/brindle are variants. Fish bodies are shaded by darkening toward the edges (no 3D lighting), fins and eyes drawn on top of the pattern texture. Watchable behavior: breeding is legible — you can watch a spot migrate toward the head over generations, because the parameter space is continuous.

M10. Weather as a system (weather.js, rain.js)

A 5-state machine (SUNNY → OVERCAST → DRIZZLE → RAIN → THUNDERSTORM) with a transition matrix. Rain particles that "land" call water.addFlare() — every raindrop is a ripple. Wind gusts (cubic noise) displace the air buffer, which the vegetation vertex shader samples — plants sway in the same wind that ruffles the water. Fish turn-chance jumps 30× in rain (M4). Ambient audio crossfades per state. Watchable behavior: a storm is one event expressed in four media at once — sky, water, plants, fish. Nothing is decorative; every visual has a simulation behind it.

Piece prompts

Each prompt is grounded in ONE mechanism above. Interaction follows Hans's rule: only where the system earns it, never click-to-reload; watch-only where the motion is the point.

P1. "The Wake Remembers" (mechanism M1)

  • Visual: top-down pond at dusk. Three koi cruise slow loops. Wherever a tail sweeps hard, a

luminous ring blooms and fades over ~20 seconds; overlapping wakes interfere into moiré.

  • Palette: deep indigo water, warm amber wake-rings, pale sand floor visible through the

shallows.

  • Composition: full-bleed pond, no shore visible — the water is the whole world.
  • Motion/interaction: watch-only. The piece earns stillness: the wakes are the event.
  • Technical approach: heightmap ping-pong at 1/16 scale (M6); tail-vertebra displacement

above a threshold paints flares (M1); vertebra-chain bodies (M5). Wake brightness ∝ tail speed so bursts read as flashes.

  • Avoid: constant-speed fish (kills M4's pulse-glide); wakes that follow the body instead of

the tail; decorative particle trails.

  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M1, M5, M6.

P2. "Rumor of a Turn" (mechanism M3)

  • Visual: a school of twelve same-colored koi (pale gold) drifting. Every so often one fish

wheels sharply — and the turn propagates through the school fish by fish, a visible wave of turning.

  • Palette: gold fish on slate-blue water, white sand.
  • Composition: the school starts as a loose ellipse center-frame; turns carry it across the

frame and it re-forms.

  • Motion/interaction: tap anywhere to startle the nearest fish (chase() + boost) and watch

the turn contagion do the rest. Tap is a disturbance, not a reload.

  • Technical approach: Boids with palette-gated recognition, speed matching, and

turn-copying at low probability (M3); pulse-glide speed model (M4). One instigator is enough — emergence does the choreography.

  • Avoid: scripting the school's path; making all fish turn at once (the delay is the

beauty); mixing colors (breaks the recognition rule).

  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M3, M4.

P3. "Nibbling Hours" (mechanism M2)

  • Visual: shallow pond, morning. Koi drift almost motionless; every few seconds one dips its

head and a perfect small ring expands from it, overlapping with others into a slow lace of circles.

  • Palette: pale jade water, soft white rings, dark green lily pads for contrast.
  • Composition: sparse — six fish maximum, lots of empty water so each ring reads.
  • Motion/interaction: watch-only. A meditation on small disturbances.
  • Technical approach: speed-decay locomotion with nibble countdowns that paint flares

(M2); high damping (.999) so rings live long; minimal fish count.

  • Avoid: busy water — more than a few rings at once turns lace into noise; fish that never

slow down.

  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M2, M6.

P4. "Arriving Waves" (mechanism M8)

  • Visual: an island pond seen from above. Wavelets form in open water and travel visibly

toward the shore, breaking into stylized foam arcs as they arrive, then dissolving.

  • Palette: turquoise shallows grading to deep teal, white foam, warm sand island.
  • Composition: island off-center, occupying a third of the frame; open water for waves to

cross.

  • Motion/interaction: watch-only loop. Drag could stir a gust (M10's air buffer) but the

piece doesn't need it — keep it still.

  • Technical approach: shore distance+direction field (jump flooding or a static SDF);

sine-wave phase keyed to shore direction, amplitude growing as distance → 0 (M8); pond compositor for refraction (M7).

  • Avoid: waves sliding parallel to shore; uniform sine grids (break them into wavelets);

foam that doesn't fade.

  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M8, M7.

P5. "Rain Makes Them Restless" (mechanisms M4 + M10)

  • Visual: the pond under a passing shower. Raindrops stipple the water with tiny rings;

the koi, calm a moment ago, begin darting and wheeling — then settle as the rain eases.

  • Palette: grey-green overcast water shifting back to blue; silver rain rings.
  • Composition: full pond with a few lily pads; weather is the protagonist.
  • Motion/interaction: watch-only. The storm arrives on its own 90-second cycle.
  • Technical approach: weather state machine driving rain-particle → water-flare coupling

(M10) and a 30× turn-chance multiplier on the fish (M4); color filter lerps with the state.

  • Avoid: rain as a visual overlay with no simulation behind it; fish ignoring the weather.
  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M4, M10.

P6. "One Fish, Swimming" (mechanism M5)

  • Visual: a single large koi, close up, turning slow figure-eights. The body S-curves, the

tail lags and sweeps, pectoral fins flutter. Nothing else moves except its wake.

  • Palette: white-and-vermillion koi (Kohaku) on near-black water — a portrait.
  • Composition: the fish fills a third of the frame; camera gently follows.
  • Motion/interaction: drag to lead the fish — it steers toward your pointer with the same

turn physics, body lagging behind. Touch earns its place: you feel the spine.

  • Technical approach: vertebra-chain spine with speed-scaled wiggle amplitude and

size-scaled phase rate (M5); tail-effort wake (M1). One fish, all budget on the body.

  • Avoid: keyframed tail wag; the head turning without the body following; trailing the

pointer rigidly.

  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M5, M1.

P7. "The Spot Migrates" (mechanism M9)

  • Visual: a koi seen from directly above, slowly rotating. Its pattern — a red patch —

drifts across its body over a minute, morphing from saddle to head-spot, as if generations are passing.

  • Palette: white fish, vermillion pattern, still pale-blue water (the fish is the exhibit).
  • Composition: centered fish, generous negative space, museum-calm.
  • Motion/interaction: watch-only. Tap to reseed the noise field (a fresh lineage), not to

reload.

  • Technical approach: pattern = thresholded slice of 3D cubic noise; animate the slice

plane drifting through the noise volume (M9); edge-darkened body shading, fins on top.

  • Avoid: crossfading between textures (the morph must be continuous through the noise

space); spots popping in/out.

  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M9.

P8. "The Floor Bends" (mechanism M7)

  • Visual: a shallow pond over rippled sand. There are no fish. A breeze crosses the water in

gusts; wherever a ripple passes, the sand floor visibly bends and shimmers beneath it — depth rendered as refraction.

  • Palette: sunlit aqua shallows, golden sand, white glints.
  • Composition: low sun angle implied by long soft shadows; the floor is the subject.
  • Motion/interaction: drag across the water to rake ripples with your finger and watch the

floor warp under them. Direct, physical, earned.

  • Technical approach: heightmap normals → background UV offset by depth × normal (M7);

gust field from cubic noise displacing an air buffer that also sways a few reeds (M10).

  • Avoid: drawing "water" as a blue overlay; caustics without a heightmap behind them.
  • Ref: studies/job-talle.md M7, M10.
