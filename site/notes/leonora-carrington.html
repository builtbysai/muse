Shelf VII, Paint & Sculpture

Leonora Carrington

studied 2026-11-04/20 min read/3,991 words

Study date: 2026-11-04. ig-artist-study #318.

Site: Mary Leonora Carrington (Clayton-le-Woods, Lancashire, England, 6 April 1917 -- Mexico City, 25 May 2011). No Instagram: verified absence, not a gap. British-born Mexican surrealist painter, novelist, playwright, tapestry-maker, and late bronze sculptor; Mexico from 1942 to her death, Mexican citizen from 1942 (unlike her friend Remedios Varo, who never took it). Identity anchors: the animal surrogate; the giantess; the nested miniature; the creature-vessel; the checkered and chalk-circle floor; the curtain threshold; egg-tempera devotion; the kitchen-rite; the four-world cosmology; the crone.

Doorway: Hop from the Remedios Varo study: the checkered floor. In The House Opposite (1945) Carrington laid a black-and-white tile floor in an alchemist's chamber; a decade later Varo gathered those same tiles into a cloak in The Useless Science (per Whitney Chadwick). Varo took Carrington's room and turned it into a machine with inputs and outputs; Carrington's room was never a machine. It was a kitchen, a nursery, a chapel. Where Varo's figures are technicians tending circuits, Carrington's are priestesses tending rites -- and the rite is not the means to the painting's end. It is the painting. Varo engineered the impossible; Carrington housekept it.

M1. The animal surrogate: the self, distributed

Carrington never appears alone; she brings a proxy. In the Self-Portrait (1937-38) the hyena at her hand mirrors her exact posture -- theartstory's reading, confirmed on the frame: her wild mane echoes the hyena's coat, and the gesture is doubled. Through the window a white horse gallops while a giant rocking horse flies on the wall behind her: three horses at three scales, all her. In Portrait of Max Ernst (1939) the proxy logic turns outward: Ernst the shaman holds a glass lantern, and inside the lantern is a miniature white horse -- her animal, kept. In Green Tea (1942) a red dog drags a white horse by its reins across the estate; in Grandmother Moorhead's Aromatic Kitchen (1975) the great white goose stands at the table as a guest of honor, not food. The mechanism: identity is not inside the figure; it is parked in the animal beside her, and the animal does the same work she does. The surrogate is always active, never decorative -- it drags, it mirrors, it dines.

Against the chain: Varo's (#317 M9) cat was a witness, a non-operator. Carrington's animals are co-operators: the hyena performs the self-portrait's gesture, the dog pulls the horse. Lazo's horse (#316 M8) had the monopoly on motion; Carrington gives the animal a monopoly on identity.

Takeaway: Never paint the figure alone -- park her identity in an animal that shares the frame and does the same work. Watch the pairing: when the beast mirrors the human's gesture, the mechanism is on.

M2. The giantess: scale as sanctity

The sacred figure is landscape-sized; everything else is incident. In The Giantess (The Guardian of the Egg) (1947) a monumental woman in a red gown towers over trees, ships, and harvesters; her face is a flat Byzantine icon ringed by a gilded wheat halo, her gown painted with tiny animal figures, an egg clasped in her hands while geese pour from her cape. In The Temptation of Saint Anthony (1945) the bearded hermit is the same device in male form: a white mountain of a saint while the temptations -- ravens, cauldron, veiled women -- play out small at his feet. In Chiki, ton pays (1944, detail) the giant woman wears a crown of trees; the couple shelter under her scale. The mechanism: the hierarchy is not composed, it is sized. Sanctity is rendered as literal bigness, and the small figures are not a crowd but footnotes -- each doing one tiny specific thing (harvesting, pouring, fleeing) that the giantess contains without noticing.

Against the chain: Varo's figures are all the same small size inside their machines; nobody towers. Lazo kept one chamber scale throughout. Carrington is the only one in the chain who uses impossible scale as the primary sacred signal -- the triangle painted importance into faces; Carrington paints it into acreage.

Takeaway: Make the sacred figure landscape-sized and shrink the incident to footnotes at her feet. Watch the ratio: when the small figures each do one tiny specific thing the giantess contains, the mechanism is on.

M3. Worlds within worlds: the four-quadrant cosmology

One frame holds several independent worlds, each with its own physics. In Les Distractions de Dagobert (1945) the canvas splits into elemental zones -- a fire pit where a bat-creature descends into flames, a watery world with a tree-horse standing in ripples, floating islands with ghostly figures in a dark sky, a boat with a shell-sail carrying a reclining woman -- and the titular Dagobert is small, lost in the sprawl, ferried on a cow-headed cart. Curator Matthew Affron (artnet, end to end) reads the quadrants as earth, air, fire, and water: a cosmology, not a scene. The mural The Magical World of the Maya (1963-64) runs the same engine at 4.5 meters: a golden-red cosmic landscape with a root-tree, an arcing serpent, suns, and tiny processions -- mythic geography with no vanishing point. The mechanism: perspective is refused. Worlds are stitched by atmosphere and color temperature, not by space; each zone is internally coherent and mutually indifferent. The viewer does not look INTO the painting; she tours it.

Against the chain: Varo's rooms are single coherent machines with one viewpoint. Lazo changed one backdrop per sheet. Carrington is the only one who runs four simultaneous landscapes in one frame and lets them ignore each other -- Bosch is the ancestor here (Carrington saw him at the Prado in 1940, per Affron), and nobody else in the chain claimed him.

Takeaway: Refuse single perspective -- run two to four internally coherent worlds in one frame, stitched by atmosphere, each ignoring the others. Watch the tour: when the eye travels between worlds instead of into depth, the mechanism is on.

M4. Egg-tempera devotion: the facture earns the fantasy

The impossible is painted with devotional precision. Sotheby's (end to end): Dagobert's "radiant tonality she achieves through thin glazes" and "exceptionally fine draftsmanship"; Affron (artnet): she learned egg tempera in art school in London, and the medium is what lets the scenes be "very detailed... very vividly colored." EN Wikipedia: "small-brushstroke techniques, building up layers in a meticulous manner." On the frames: the Giantess's gown carries dozens of tiny delineated animals; Ferret Race renders each white beast and each thin human with hairline control; the Dagobert fire-pit bat-creature is charred black and brown down to the claw. The mechanism: precision is not style, it is permission. The meticulous surface is what lets the viewer accept the bat-creature, because the claw was drawn as carefully as the halo. Edward James's line (Tate Liverpool): her works "are not merely painted, they are brewed."

Against the chain: Varo's two-pass build (#317 M10) used chance against precision; Carrington uses precision against disbelief -- no blotting, no decalcomania, no weather. Lazo stippled; the triangle stayed matte and flat. Carrington is the chain's only miniaturist: the fantasy is earned one hairline at a time.

Takeaway: Render the impossible with the patience of an illuminator -- thin glazes, hairline detail, no chance textures. Watch the claw: when the monster's smallest part is drawn as carefully as the saint's halo, the mechanism is on.

M5. The domestic ritual chamber: cooking as alchemy

The room is a rite, and the rite is housework. In The House Opposite (1945) robed women gather around a vessel on a black-and-white checkered floor while a ladder rises to an upper bed -- the chamber is alchemical, the furniture domestic. In Night Nursery Everything (1947) the rite is bedtime: a hammock-child, candles, a towering golden woman, a small girl with outstretched arms -- the nursery staged as ceremony. In Grandmother Moorhead's Aromatic Kitchen (1975) the rite is dinner: hooded women cooking around a table laden with corn and fruit while a horned attendant sweeps and the great goose presides. Chadwick (via maramarietta, snippet-verified): Carrington and Varo evolved "their highly personalized vision of the woman creator whose creative and magical powers were a higher development of traditional domestic activities like cooking." The mechanism: no special apparatus is built -- the kitchen, the nursery, the dining table already are the apparatus. The magic is not added to the domestic; the domestic is revealed as the magic.

Against the chain: Varo re-plumbed furniture into machines (#317 M6) -- the funnel funnels. Carrington leaves the furniture as furniture and changes what the act means: cooking is already alchemy, no pipes required. Lazo left the room intact; Carrington leaves the room intact too, but consecrates it.

Takeaway: Stage the rite with ordinary furniture -- kitchen, nursery, table -- and let the act be the magic, no apparatus added. Watch the chore: when cooking reads as alchemy without any machine, the mechanism is on.

M6. Stable hybrids: metamorphosis, frozen

Transformation is not a process in Carrington; it is a species. In The Meal of Lord Candlestick (1938) the banquet women have horse heads, white manes, green serpent hair -- they are not becoming, they have become, and they are eating. In Ulu's Pants (1952) the foreground is a frieze of finished monsters -- owl-fish, horse, green lion-beast -- standing in a row "as if acting in a play" (theartstory), each a stable citizen of the canvas. In Daughter of the Minotaur (1953) the bull-headed man in the red robe sits at table as calmly as the children in their cloaks; the flower-headed woman stands behind him like a chandelier. In Ferret Race the long white beasts are simply what runs the track. The mechanism: nobody is mid-change. The hybrid is the steady state, and the painting's calm -- everyone posed, nothing surprised -- is what makes it uncanny. The Tate Etc line: creatures "standing, waiting somewhere evocatively theatrical between benign and malign."

Against the chain: Varo's birds were escapes in progress (#317 M7); Lazo's shadows were evidence of motion. Carrington's hybrids never move toward anything -- they have arrived, they are posed, they are waiting. The chain's only artist who treats the monster as a settled fact rather than an event.

Takeaway: Freeze the hybrid mid-state and pose it calmly -- the monster has arrived, it is not becoming. Watch the stillness: when the impossible creature stands waiting like it belongs, the mechanism is on.

M7. The creature-vessel: travel as a flock

The vehicle is always an animal, and the journey is always ceremonial. In the bronze Ship of Cranes (design 2010, monumental SLP casting) the boat is a bird with a curving neck-prow; four crane passengers ride inside while a tall crane-helmsman paddles from the stern. In Cocodrilo (donated 2000) the boat is a crocodile with an open toothed jaw and seven passenger heads rising from its back. In Portrait of Max Ernst (1939) the vessel is a glass lantern carrying a whole horse. In Temptation of St Anthony the river itself is poured from a giant jug off a cliff -- the water arrives as a delivered object. The mechanism: transit is never mechanical. The vessel must be a creature (or a held thing), the passengers ride inside the animal, and the helmsman is the tallest figure. NMWA's label: the boat calls on "myths from the Egyptian afterlife and Mexican spiritual guides" -- the journey is a crossing, not a trip.

Against the chain: Varo improvised vessels from household objects (#317 M2) -- umbrella into boat. Carrington does the reverse: she starts from the animal and finds the boat inside it. Nobody else in the chain made the vehicle a beast; the triangle never left the room.

Takeaway: Build every vessel out of a creature -- the passengers ride inside the animal, the tallest figure steers. Watch the crossing: when the boat is a beast and the journey reads as a rite of passage, the mechanism is on.

M8. Curtains and thresholds: the staged world

Every painting is a stage with a marked entrance. In the Self-Portrait yellow curtains frame the window where the white horse runs -- the curtain marks which world is the real one. In The House Opposite a ladder climbs to an upper level where a figure lies on a bed: the painting has an upstairs. In Daughter of the Minotaur curtains, columns, and a doorway dancer frame the central table; in El Juglar a drawn theater curtain reveals the juggler against red. In Green Tea the threshold is horizontal: a thin ground line with the estate above and a bat-filled underworld below. The mechanism: the frame always contains its own backstage. Curtains, ladders, ground-lines, and doorways tell the viewer where the ritual space starts -- and the space beyond the threshold always runs different rules (the window's forest, the bed's upper room, the underworld strip).

Against the chain: Varo cut one wall off the room (#317 M1) to show the machine inside. Carrington keeps all four walls and hangs a curtain instead -- the point is not to see the works, it is to know a performance is happening. Lazo's shadows (#316 M5) hinted at an unseen operator; Carrington's curtains promise an unseen world.

Takeaway: Mark the threshold -- curtain, ladder, ground-line, doorway -- and let different rules run on each side of it. Watch the edge: when the painting contains its own backstage, the mechanism is on.

M9. The contained miniature: the hand holds the landscape

Scales nest: what is vast outside is tiny inside something held. In Portrait of Max Ernst the white horse -- the animal that gallops life-size through the Self-Portrait's window -- fits inside a hand-held lantern. In The Giantess the monumental woman clasps a single egg while her gown is painted with miniature animals and tiny harvesters work at her feet: three scales in one figure. In Daughter of the Minotaur glass orbs float around the table like held worlds. In Ulu's Pants a small red-headed figure guards an egg inside a stone niche at the frame's edge while the labyrinth sprawls behind. The mechanism: the miniature is never a model or a symbol -- it is the same creature, genuinely small, genuinely held. The hand that holds the horse is the proof that scale is negotiable in this universe, which is what licenses the giantess in M2.

Against the chain: Varo's doubles were countable objects (#317 M4) -- faces in chests. Carrington's miniatures are not doubles; they are the same being at a different size, held without surprise. Nobody else in the chain nested scale as a casual fact of the world.

Takeaway: Put the same creature at two scales in one frame -- vast in the landscape, tiny in a held thing -- and treat both as unremarkable. Watch the hand: when the held miniature is the same beast that runs wild outside, the mechanism is on.

M10. Bronze after tempera: the bestiary goes public

Late Carrington extrudes the painted hybrids into bronze and sets them in the street. Cocodrilo -- the crocodile-boat from Lewis Carroll's Alice poem -- stands on the Paseo de la Reforma, donated to Mexico City in 2000. The Ship of Cranes rises in the Museo Leonora Carrington's own courtyard in San Luis Potosi. The sources (inverarte, Sotheby's) date the bronze turn to the 1980s-90s, after polychrome wood in the 1950s and a silver foundry in 1975: a slow material migration across four decades. The mechanism: the same grammar -- creature-vessels, passenger crews, tall helmsmen -- translated from intimate egg tempera to public monument. The facture flips (M4's hairlines become cast bronze) but the syntax does not move an inch. The studiointernational note that the late bronzes are "controversial... not everyone accepts [them] as a Carrington" is itself evidence: the market wants the tempera miniaturist, but the artist wanted the menagerie in the plaza.

Against the chain: Varo died at the height of the tempera run; her syntax never left the panel. Carrington lived to 94 and gave her beasts a second, public life -- the only member of the chain whose late work changes material without changing mind.

Takeaway: Translate the private bestiary into public bronze without changing its syntax -- same vessels, same crews, new scale. Watch the plaza: when the street monument reads as a tempera panel grown huge, the mechanism is on.

Evolution

1936-38 London: Ozenfant's academy -- six months drawing one apple, the chemistry of materials. The discipline under everything later. 1937-40 Paris and St Martin d'Ardeche (Ernst years): the first masterpieces -- Self-Portrait 1937-38, The Meal of Lord Candlestick 1938, Portrait of Max Ernst 1939. Small, fierce, autobiographical; the surrogate system (M1) and the hybrid (M6) already complete. 1940-42 asylum, New York: Down Below memoir and painting; Green Tea 1942, the estate-and-underworld diptych. 1942-55 Mexico, marriage to Chiki, two sons: the great tempera run -- Chiki, ton pays 1944, Dagobert 1945, The House Opposite 1945, Temptation 1945, The Giantess 1947, Night Nursery Everything 1947, Ferret Race 1952, Ulu's Pants 1952, Daughter of the Minotaur 1953, El Juglar 1954. Alchemy, Kabbalah, Popol Vuh with Varo; egg tempera learned in London now perfected; the giantess (M2), the cosmology (M3), and the ritual chamber (M5) all mature. 1960-64: the public phase -- MAM retrospective 1960, the Anthropology Museum mural 1963-64. 1970s: the crone enters -- Bird Bath 1974 (the artist as old woman baptizing a bird in red paint), Grandmother Moorhead's Aromatic Kitchen 1975; the Mujeres conciencia poster 1973; The Mansion of Madness 1973. 1980s-2011: bronze -- the bestiary goes monumental; museums posthumous (SLP 2018). The arc: from the apple's chemistry to the plaza's bronze, one continuous discipline -- the miniature and the monument are the same drawing at different sizes (M9 is the whole career).

Aesthetic logic: alchemy as housekeeping

Every impossible thing in Carrington is staged as a household rite -- cooking, bathing, nursing, feasting, racing -- performed with devotional precision at cosmic scale. The Tate critic's phrase is the thesis in miniature: a "dinner-table-practical otherworldly vision." Nothing is engineered (that was Varo); everything is tended. The giantess is nursed like a household god, the kitchen cooks a cosmology, the nursery performs a bedtime that is also an initiation, the race is run like a parish fete with a referee. The egg tempera (M4) is the technique this logic demands: you cannot housekeep the impossible with a loaded brush; you need the small stroke, the thin glaze, the patience of someone who has done the chore a thousand times. That is why the late bronzes still read as hers: the plaza is just a bigger kitchen. And it is why the men never got her: Breton's surrealism wanted the marvelous as shock; Carrington served it as routine, which is far stranger.
